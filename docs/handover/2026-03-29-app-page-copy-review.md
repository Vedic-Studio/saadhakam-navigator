### 2a. Persona: The Philosopher

**Description:** Socratic questioning across all six darshanas. Ask about consciousness, and the tutor walks you through Samkhya, Advaita, and Nyaya perspectives with verse citations.

**Sample Q:** Is consciousness produced by the brain?

**Sample A:** Samkhya says no. Purusha (consciousness) is eternal and independent of Prakriti (matter). Yoga Sutras 1.3 states that when mental fluctuations cease, the seer rests in its own nature. Nyaya takes a different route, arguing consciousness is a quality that arises when the self contacts the mind. Two schools, two mechanisms, same starting observation.

**Citations:** Yoga Sutras 1.3, Nyaya Sutras 1.1.10

**Doctrinal accuracy check:**
- Yoga Sutras 1.3: "tada drashtuh svarupe avasthanam" — correct usage, this verse describes the seer abiding in its own form
- Nyaya Sutras 1.1.10 — this is the sutra listing prameyas (objects of knowledge) including atman. The claim that Nyaya argues consciousness is a "quality that arises when the self contacts the mind" is a fair simplification of Nyaya's position that jnana (knowledge/consciousness) is a guna (quality) of atman, not identical to atman itself. Accurate.

### 2b. Persona: The Practitioner

**Description:** Guided sadhana recommendations based on your temperament and available time. Cites Yoga Sutras, Bhagavad Gita, and traditional commentaries.

**Sample Q:** I have 20 minutes each morning. What should I do?

**Sample A:** Patanjali recommends abhyasa (steady practice) in Yoga Sutras 1.12. For 20 minutes: 5 minutes of pranayama (Nadi Shodhana, as described in Hatha Yoga Pradipika 2.7), then 15 minutes of seated meditation on a single point of focus. The Gita (6.25) says "shanair shanair uparamet" — settle the mind gradually, not by force.

**Citations:** Yoga Sutras 1.12, Bhagavad Gita 6.25

**Doctrinal accuracy check:**
- YS 1.12: "abhyasa vairagyabhyam tan nirodhah" — abhyasa is indeed steady practice, correct
- HYP 2.7: Nadi Shodhana (alternate nostril breathing) is discussed in HYP Chapter 2, though the exact verse numbering varies by edition. Close enough for a demo conversation.
- BG 6.25: "shanair shanair uparamet buddhya dhriti-grihitaya" — correct, this verse is about gradual settling of the mind

### 2c. Persona: The Scholar

**Description:** Word-by-word textual analysis. Compare how Shankara, Ramanuja, and Madhva interpret the same Upanishadic passage differently.

**Sample Q:** What does "tat tvam asi" actually mean word by word?

**Sample A:** "Tat" (That, Brahman), "tvam" (you, the individual self), "asi" (are). Chandogya Upanishad 6.8.7. Shankara reads it as strict identity: you ARE Brahman. Ramanuja reads it as qualified identity: you are Brahman's body. Madhva reads it as "tat tvam asi" meaning "you are not that" (a-si), through a different sandhi split. Three readings, three schools, one sentence.

**Citations:** Chandogya Upanishad 6.8.7

**Doctrinal accuracy check:**
- CU 6.8.7: correct location for "tat tvam asi" (one of the mahavakyas)
- Shankara's reading: correct (identity interpretation)
- Ramanuja's reading: correct (sharira-shariri, body-soul relationship)
- Madhva's "a-si" split: this is the famous Madhva re-parse. Historically documented. Some scholars dispute whether Madhva himself made this argument vs. later Dvaita commentators, but it's a standard representation of the Dvaita position.

### 3a. Conversation: Shankara on Maya

**User:** What did Shankara mean by maya? Is it just illusion?

**AI msg 1:** Shankara defines maya not as "illusion" in the popular sense but as misidentification. In Vivekachudamani (verse 20), he describes it as beginningless, indescribable, and neither real nor unreal. Maya is the power that makes the finite self appear separate from Brahman.
*[Vivekachudamani v.20]*

**AI msg 2:** Ramanuja rejects this entirely. In his Sri Bhashya commentary on Brahma Sutras, he argues the world is genuinely real as the body of God. For Ramanuja, maya is not cosmic deception but God's creative potency (shakti).
*[Sri Bhashya on Brahma Sutras]*

**AI msg 3:** The practical difference matters: for Shankara, recognizing maya dissolves attachment through jnana (knowledge). For Ramanuja, the world's reality is precisely what makes bhakti (devotion) meaningful. Two frameworks, each internally consistent, each producing distinct contemplative traditions.

**Doctrinal accuracy check:**
- Vivekachudamani v.20: The verse describes maya as "anirvachaniya" (indescribable), "anadya" (beginningless). Correct.
- Sri Bhashya: Ramanuja does argue the world is real as God's body (sharira). Correct.
- "God's creative potency (shakti)" — Ramanuja's tradition typically uses "shakti" less than Shankara does (for whom maya IS shakti of Brahman). For Ramanuja, the world is real prakriti. Consider: "God's real transformative power" instead of "creative potency (shakti)"?

### 3b. Conversation: Yoga Darshana vs Modern Yoga

**User:** How is classical Yoga Darshana different from what we call yoga today?

**AI msg 1:** Patanjali's Yoga Sutras (1.2) defines yoga as "yogash chitta vritti nirodhah" — the cessation of the fluctuations of the mind. The entire system aims at stilling consciousness, not physical flexibility.
*[Yoga Sutras 1.2]*

**AI msg 2:** Patanjali's ashtanga (eight-limbed) path places asana as the third limb, after yama (ethical restraints) and niyama (personal disciplines). The physical postures are preparation for dharana (concentration), dhyana (meditation), and samadhi (absorption). Modern postural yoga inverts this hierarchy.
*[Yoga Sutras 2.29]*

**AI msg 3:** Hatha Yoga, which emerged centuries later through texts like the Hatha Yoga Pradipika (15th century), expanded the physical dimension significantly. Most studio yoga today descends from this Hatha tradition filtered through 20th-century teachers like Krishnamacharya, not directly from Patanjali.
*[Hatha Yoga Pradipika]*

**Doctrinal accuracy check:**
- YS 1.2: exact quote, correct
- YS 2.29: lists the eight limbs, correct
- "Modern postural yoga inverts this hierarchy" — strong claim but defensible (Mark Singleton's scholarship supports this)
- HYP dating: traditionally attributed to Svatmarama, 15th century. Correct.
- Krishnamacharya attribution: well-documented through Singleton, Sjoman. Correct.

### 3c. Conversation: Stoic Logos vs Brahman

**User:** How does the Stoic concept of logos compare to Brahman?

**AI msg 1:** Marcus Aurelius in Meditations (IV.40) describes logos as the rational principle pervading all things — the ordering intelligence of the cosmos. The Mandukya Upanishad (verse 2) calls Brahman "sarvam hyetad brahma" — all this is indeed Brahman.
*[Meditations IV.40, Mandukya Upanishad v.2]*

**AI msg 2:** The structural parallel is real: both posit a single principle underlying all phenomena. But the epistemological paths diverge. The Stoics reach logos through rational observation of nature. The Upanishadic rishis reach Brahman through direct meditative realization (anubhava), which they consider prior to and more fundamental than rational inference.

**AI msg 3:** There is also an ontological gap. Stoic logos is material — a kind of refined fire pervading matter. Brahman in Advaita is pure consciousness, neither material nor immaterial in the Western sense. Shankara would say logos describes an effect (vyavaharika), not the ultimate cause (paramarthika).
*[Brahma Sutra Bhashya 1.1.2]*

**Doctrinal accuracy check:**
- Meditations IV.40: need to verify exact content of this passage. Marcus Aurelius discusses logos throughout, but IV.40 specifically should be cross-checked. The characterization of logos as "rational principle pervading all things" is accurate to Stoic philosophy generally.
- Mandukya Upanishad v.2: "sarvam hyetad brahma" — this is correct, verse 2
- Stoic logos as material/fire: correct (Stoics were materialists, logos = pneuma = fire-like substance)
- vyavaharika/paramarthika distinction: correct Advaita terminology
- BSB 1.1.2: Shankara does discuss the nature of Brahman here, though the specific vyavaharika framing is a general Advaita concept not limited to this verse

### 3d. Conversation: Starting a Daily Practice

**User:** I want to start a daily spiritual practice but I don't know which path suits me.

**AI msg 1:** Krishna addresses this directly in the Bhagavad Gita (3.3). He describes different temperaments suited to different paths: jnana yoga for the contemplative mind, karma yoga for the active temperament, bhakti yoga for the devotional heart, and dhyana yoga for the meditative disposition.
*[Bhagavad Gita 3.3]*

**AI msg 2:** A few questions to start: Do you find yourself drawn more to intellectual inquiry (understanding the nature of things), to devotional expression (prayer, chanting, relationship with the divine), to selfless action (service without attachment to results), or to silent meditation?

**User:** Probably intellectual inquiry and silent meditation.

**AI msg 3:** That combination points toward jnana yoga with a dhyana foundation. A practical starting point: 15 minutes of seated meditation (following Patanjali's Yoga Sutras 1.12 on abhyasa — steady practice), followed by contemplation on a passage from the Upanishads or the Gita. The Isha Upanishad (18 verses) or Gita Chapter 2 are strong starting texts.
*[Yoga Sutras 1.12, Isha Upanishad]*

**Doctrinal accuracy check:**
- BG 3.3: "loke 'smin dvi-vidha nishtha" — Krishna describes two paths (jnana and karma yoga). The verse specifically mentions two, not four. The four-yoga framework (jnana, karma, bhakti, dhyana/raja) is a broader categorization drawn from multiple Gita passages, not strictly BG 3.3. **This citation is imprecise.** Consider citing BG 3.3 alongside BG 12.8-12 (which discusses the paths more comprehensively) or reframing to say "across the Gita" rather than pinning it to 3.3.
- Isha Upanishad has 18 verses: correct
