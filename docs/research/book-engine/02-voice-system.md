# Voice System

How the Sadhaka book should sound. Architecture of voice, register, persona, pronoun use, and mood.

Cross-reference: [sources/bam-analysis.md](sources/bam-analysis.md) §3; [sources/trp-analysis.md](sources/trp-analysis.md) §3, §11.

---

## 1. Four voice archetypes (the anthology's inner chorus)

A cult book that visits the same concept from four distinct voices across its length feels bigger than its page count. BAM does this inside one narrator via register-swing; TRP does it via multi-author stacking. For Sadhaka, we use four named voices that alternate post-to-post:

### The Ācārya
**Register**: systematic, calm, patient, architecturally clear. Defines terms. Owns frameworks. Never pleads.
**Pronouns**: implied *we* of the tradition ("the Śāstra teaches…"); second-person *you* for the reader. No "I."
**Signature moves**: labelled frameworks, numbered rules, precise scope qualifiers (*vyāvahārika* / *pāramārthika*), etymological asides.
**Analogue**: Rollo Tomassi in TRP; the opening declaratives of BAM.
**Use for**: framework posts, concept primers, cosmology orientations, definitional openings.

### The Sādhaka
**Register**: first-person, confessional, in-progress. Admits failure before claiming insight. Specific: dates, places, names, hours.
**Pronouns**: *I* throughout. Second-person only at the end, when the lesson is offered.
**Signature moves**: a humiliation or relapse with date/place; a moment of recognition; a specific śloka that broke through; a warning to the reader.
**Analogue**: Old Fat and Bald's "BRIFFAULT'S LAW" opening in TRP; the "I was beaten up in the alley" asides in BAM.
**Use for**: every 3rd–5th post, as emotional glue.

### The Satirist
**Register**: sharp, comic, concrete, un-reverent — but never cruel. Targets *patterns*, not people: the weekend-retreat spiritualist, the Instagram-quoter, the corporatized yoga studio, the interfaith-erasure of specifics.
**Pronouns**: second-person ("you know the type"); occasional third-person anatomical description of a type.
**Signature moves**: inversion of a platitude; lurid concrete imagery of the type being punctured; unapologetic lampoon followed by a pivot to something serious.
**Analogue**: BAM's descriptions of "bugmen"; IllimitableMan's glossary-as-mockery in TRP.
**Use for**: roughly every 6th–8th post. Less frequent than BAM uses satire — the Sadhaka book is in the elevation business, not the tearing-down business. Satire opens space; it doesn't occupy it.

### The Imported Voice
**Register**: the tradition speaks directly. Blockquoted primary sources: Upaniṣadic passages, Gītā ślokas, Yoga-sūtra aphorisms, Shankara's bhāṣyas, Abhinavagupta, Tulsidas, Ramana, Aurobindo, Nisargadatta. Also pre-modern non-Sanatan voices when they rhyme — Heraclitus, Plotinus, Ibn Arabi — sparingly, only when the point requires a cross-reference.
**Pronouns**: whatever the source uses.
**Signature moves**: block quotation → one-sentence gloss → integration into the surrounding argument. Translation provided once, then the Sanskrit is trusted to carry.
**Analogue**: TRP's stacking of Schopenhauer / James / Vilar on a claim; BAM's "Heraclitus say…" and "Nietzsche says…" scaffolding.
**Use for**: every post has at least one imported-voice block; pillar posts have two or three from different layers of the paramparā.

**Rotation rule**: no two consecutive posts lead with the same voice. Pillar posts use three voices within one post.

## 2. Register-swing inside a single post (the BAM move)

Even within one voice, register can modulate. BAP gets away with mystical pronouncements because they arrive alongside gutter-specific detail. The contrast is the trick: the reader accepts the cosmic claim because the surrounding texture is somatically real.

**For the Sadhaka book, the analogous swing is**:

| Register | What it sounds like | Sample anchor |
|---|---|---|
| **Darśanic** | Metaphysical, sūtra-terse | *"The seer is not the seen. This is the first knife."* |
| **Observational / somatic** | Concrete body, Indian landscape, temple detail | *"The smell at Kashi ghats at 4am is not cleanliness. It is something older."* |
| **Mundane-contemporary** | Urban life, phones, traffic, call centres | *"You check the phone again. You know you've already checked it."* |
| **Tender** | Kinship, grief, childhood memory, grandfather at deathbed | *"He asked for the Gītā. We were not ready. He was."* |
| **Mythic** | Puranic, cosmic, kalpa-scale | *"Markandeya, child of eternal twelve, saw what we forget every hour."* |

**The rhythm inside a mid-length post**: typically darśanic → observational → mythic → tender → darśanic. The arc returns to where it started but with added pressure. Sūtra opens, sūtra closes, story and image in between.

## 3. Pronoun rules

| Pronoun | Use when | Avoid when |
|---|---|---|
| **You (second-person, direct)** | Default. Any post-sentence that is not (a) a confessional I, (b) a paramparā citation, or (c) a Satirist sketch of a *type*. | Using as euphemism for "people in general." If it's not you-the-reader personally, recast. |
| **I (first-person)** | Only in Sādhaka-voice confessional posts. One I-post per 3–5 posts. | In Ācārya voice. In framework posts. In any post where the claim should feel tradition-grounded, not personal. |
| **We** | The paramparā ("the tradition teaches"), or occasionally humanity-in-general ("we are all here for a brief window"). | As a sneaky way to get the reader to agree ("we all know that…"). This is TRP's manipulative we and must not be used. |
| **They** | The paramparā ("the ācāryas"); the types being gently satirized; the mainstream commentators being corrected. Never individual real people. | Naming real contemporaries, even unfavorably. Pattern-naming is fine; person-naming is not. |

**The triangulation**: *I (when I'm writing as Sādhaka) am your peer walking the same road. You are the protagonist of this book. They are the paramparā — silent witnesses whose ślokas we are reading together.*

This is the same triangulation TRP achieves with peer-author / reader / imported masters. It's also the native guru–śiṣya–paramparā architecture already in place in the Sanatan tradition. The book form lesson is to **make this triangulation visible on the page**, not buried in theology.

## 4. Persona architecture for a named author

BAM is anonymous. BAP can sustain prophet-in-exile because no biographical rival narrative exists. A named Sadhaka author writing in pure prophet-voice would read as vanity.

The adaptation: the named author is **not the narrator** of the book. The named author is the **curator** of a book whose four voices (Ācārya / Sādhaka / Satirist / Imported Voice) speak. The author's name on the spine is that of a scholar-practitioner who has assembled these posts and stands behind them — not a seer who claims all four voices as himself.

This makes possible:
- The Sādhaka voice can admit failures that are specific and humbling without the author being exposed as a fraud for having also written the Ācārya voice.
- The Satirist voice can be sharper because the Ācārya voice will arrive two posts later to restore altitude.
- The Imported Voice is literally what it is — an authority older than the book.

**Implementation**:
- Book opens with a short "About this book" page in the curator's voice, in plain first-person, naming the four voices and declaring their rotation.
- Each post is headed by the voice label in small type (e.g., *"— Ācārya"*) so the reader can track.
- No single post tries to do all four voices at once, except pillar posts (every 10th or so) which deliberately braid.

## 5. Mood palette (the seven moods available)

Any Sadhaka post draws from this palette. Most posts use 2–3 moods; pillar posts use 4–5; short posts use 1.

1. **Exhortation** — "Do this. Do it now. Nothing else is available to you."
2. **Tenderness** — childhood memory, grandfather's hand, an old pandit's untaught kindness.
3. **Awe** — cosmic scope, Markandeya in the deluge, the kalpa-mathematics of yugas.
4. **Disenchantment** — puncturing a modern spiritual-marketplace pretense with one sharp image.
5. **Diagnosis** — naming a condition the reader has and couldn't articulate.
6. **Invitation** — offering a practice, a verse, a sitting time; opening a door.
7. **Release / śānti** — exhale; nothing is demanded; the verse does the work.

The book's mood arc across its length: diagnosis → disenchantment → awe → tenderness → exhortation → invitation → release. Early posts do more diagnosis and disenchantment; middle posts more awe and exhortation; late posts more tenderness and invitation; the final post is pure release.

## 6. Diction rules

### What to build
- **Sanskrit terms used unglossed after first appearance.** Define once in the early glossary post; thereafter the term carries its own charge. If the reader doesn't know it yet, they learn by repetition and context.
- **Concrete Indian-landscape nouns**: banyan, peepal, bel, tulsi, neem, langur, peacock, cobra, tiger, elephant, cow. Used as anchors, not as ornaments. Replace BAP's Greek stag with the Indian peacock; his ocean pirate with the river ferryman; his Aetna volcano with Kedarnath in a storm.
- **Temple-specificity**: *kumkum, ghee-lamp, bell-tongue, granite floor, conch, ash-mark, banyan at dawn*. The reader should be able to smell the place.
- **Time-depth anchors**: *kalpa, yuga, manvantara, muhūrta, sandhyā*. Mundane time and cosmic time should sit in the same paragraph.

### What to avoid
- **New Age vocabulary**: "energy," "vibration," "manifestation," "spiritual journey," "inner work" — any word that has been degraded by the marketplace. When the idea is needed, reach for the Sanskrit term or the concrete image, not the English euphemism.
- **Corporate-spiritual mashups**: "spiritual CEO," "mindful leadership," "ancient wisdom for modern success." These are the exact targets of the Satirist voice, not the book's own register.
- **In-group slurs of any kind.** BAM's "bugman" coinage is a transposable mechanic; its actual words are not. When pattern-naming is needed, coin a Sanskrit-grounded term or describe the type precisely without a slur. (See [08-sadhaka-transposition.md](08-sadhaka-transposition.md) §"Anti-patterns".)
- **Manosphere or political vocabulary.** The book must not be legible as a tribal signal in either direction. The reader's politics are irrelevant to the book's business.

## 7. Sentence rhythm

- **Sūtra-short sentences** adjacent to long periodic ones. BAM does this; the same rhythm works for śāstra-adjacent prose. "You are not the body. You are not the mind. The one who denies these — that also, not it. Rest where nothing can be said."
- **Em dashes — sparingly**, as a BAP-signature device. One or two per post, where a pause needs to feel physical.
- **Trailing ellipses** — rare. Only when the unsaid is itself the point. ([04-rhetoric-and-conviction.md](04-rhetoric-and-conviction.md) §"The unsaid as authority".)
- **Parallel triplets** — BAP's "list of three" is a Sanskrit-native rhythm (*śraddhā-medhā-dhī*, *jāgrat-svapna-suṣupti*). Use liberally.
- **Imperative without argument** as closing moves: "Sit at 4am. Don't check the phone first. Begin."

## 8. Voice anti-patterns (do not do)

- **Do not smuggle "we know this is edgy but…" into any paragraph.** If it's worth saying, it's said without apology. If it can't be said without apology, it doesn't go in this book.
- **Do not explain the framework before making the reader feel the absence that the framework answers.** The Ācārya arrives after the diagnostic pressure is already on the reader, not before.
- **Do not let the Satirist voice dominate.** One Satirist-led post per 6–8 is the ceiling. More than that, and the book reads as complaint rather than elevation.
- **Do not have the Ācārya voice confess.** That's the Sādhaka's job. If the Ācārya admits uncertainty, it should be about scope (*"from the vyāvahārika standpoint…"*), not about the teacher's own failings.
- **Do not narrate the reader's inner state with certainty.** BAP does this ("you know what I mean") and gets away with it via shared internet culture. The Sadhaka book does not have that shared culture. Say *"perhaps you have felt…"*, *"a reader who has felt this will…"* — leave a door the reader can step back from.

## 9. One-line test for any draft paragraph

Read it aloud. It should pass **three of five**:

1. Could this land on Twitter as a standalone unit and make sense?
2. Does it carry a Sanskrit term, a concrete image, or a specific body-detail?
3. Does the register shift at least once (darśanic → somatic, or somatic → mythic)?
4. Is the reader addressed or present?
5. Does it close in a way that invites the next sentence (not a full stop to attention)?

If the paragraph passes fewer than three, rewrite.

*Voice is upstream of everything. Get this right and the rest is technical.*
