# Templates Kit

Nine reusable post-shape templates. Each template has: purpose, when to use, word-count target, voice assignment, structural beats with slot-fillers, worked example, common failure modes.

Drafters pick a template before drafting. The template front-loads every structural decision so the writing can focus on voice and specificity.

Cross-reference: [03-structure-system.md](03-structure-system.md) §3, §4; [06-narration-system.md](06-narration-system.md) §2; [02-voice-system.md](02-voice-system.md) §1.

## Template 1 — Framework Post

**Purpose**: Introduce or deepen a named construct with labelled sub-items.
**When to use**: Pillar posts introducing a core concept-axis (the four yogas, the three guṇas, the puruṣārthas, śravaṇa–manana–nididhyāsana, the kośas, adhikāra).
**Target length**: 1500–3000 words (pillar) or 800–1200 words (ordinary framework post).
**Primary voice**: Ācārya. Pillar posts braid in Imported Voice.

### Beats
1. **Title** — the labelled construct, one phrase. *"The Four Veils."* / *"The Three Modes."* / *"The Ladder Śaṅkara Named."*
2. **Opening (2–4 sentences)** — name the condition the reader has that this framework will organize. Do not yet introduce the framework. Just name the felt absence. *"You have read many things and they have not cohered. This is not a failure of your reading. It is a failure of the reading-order that was given to you."*
3. **The skeleton (one paragraph)** — state the four/three/five/seven items. No justification yet. *"The tradition names three: śravaṇa, manana, nididhyāsana."*
4. **Branch-by-branch exposition** — one paragraph per item. Each paragraph: name, one-sentence definition, one concrete example, one Sanskrit source (even a half-verse), one failure-mode a beginner encounters.
5. **How the items relate** — one or two paragraphs on the internal logic. Do they unfold sequentially? Simultaneously? Do they transform each other? The tradition's precision on relationships.
6. **Imported Voice deep-reference** (pillar posts only) — a longer quotation from an ācārya-bhāṣya that compresses the whole framework. Quote at length; gloss sparingly.
7. **Why this framework matters for sādhana** (one paragraph) — the reader's practice, not the abstract point.
8. **Closing imperative / invocation** — a single-line imperative ("Sit with these for a week. Do not confuse them.") or a Sanskrit half-verse.

### Slot-fillers
- `{CONSTRUCT_NAME}`: The Four Yogas / The Three Guṇas / etc.
- `{ITEM_LIST}`: the labelled sub-items
- `{SHARED_FELT_ABSENCE}`: what the reader feels but can't organize
- `{SHASTRIC_SOURCE}`: the primary-source location for this framework
- `{ACARYA_BHASYA_SOURCE}`: the commentary that clarifies it
- `{BEGINNER_FAILURE_MODE}`: how the framework is typically misapplied

### Worked example (outline, not full draft)
Title: *"The Four Śraddhās"*
Opening: You have been told śraddhā is faith, faith is belief, belief is optional. Three mistranslations. The reader is left with an English word carrying none of śraddhā's weight.
Skeleton: The tradition distinguishes four postures the sādhaka takes toward what is not yet verified: toward the teacher, toward the text, toward the practice, toward the self.
Branch 1 — Guru-śraddhā... [etc.]

### Common failure modes
- **Opening with the framework instead of the felt absence.** Kills the post. The reader needs to feel the problem before meeting the answer.
- **Too-even paragraphs.** Each branch paragraph sounding like the others. Vary the texture: one paragraph ends in a Sanskrit verse, one in a field-report detail, one in a confessional half-sentence.
- **Over-justifying the framework.** The tradition does not owe the reader a justification. Present the framework with calm authority.

## Template 2 — Confessional Post

**Purpose**: First-person testimony of failure, recognition, and hard-won understanding.
**When to use**: Every 3rd–5th post in the book's sequence. As emotional glue. As the ground from which an abstract point becomes felt.
**Target length**: 600–1500 words.
**Primary voice**: Sādhaka. No other voice appears.

### Beats
1. **Title** — often a date or place. *"Rishikesh, 2019."* / *"The Day Before the Yātrā."* / *"What the Boy at the Ashram Asked."*
2. **Specific opening (3–6 sentences)** — date, place, object, context. Enough detail that the reader is inside the scene. *"I was on day 40 of a 41-day schedule. I had not spoken to my family in eight days. The room had one window that faced the street. A small boy from the ashram kitchen passed it every afternoon at 3:17."*
3. **The frame I was operating under (2–4 sentences)** — what I thought I was doing. Said with retrospective honesty. *"I thought I was doing sādhana. That is not quite right. I was performing sādhana. For whom, I could not have said."*
4. **The crack (the precipitating moment — 4–8 sentences)** — the specific event, conversation, observation that cracked the self-deception. One moment, one detail, told slowly.
5. **The silence after (1–3 sentences)** — what happened in the hours after. Often, nothing dramatic. The confessional does not need catharsis.
6. **What I came to see (2–4 sentences)** — the lesson, offered briefly. Quiet.
7. **What I do not know (1–3 sentences)** — preserve uncertainty. The reader trusts the confessor who still does not know everything.
8. **The reader-invitation (1–3 sentences)** — not advice. An invitation. *"You will have to notice what you are counting. I do not know what you will find."*

### Slot-fillers
- `{DATE_PLACE}`: specific setting
- `{OBSERVABLE_DETAILS}`: 2–4 sensory or temporal anchors
- `{PRIOR_FRAME}`: what I thought I was doing
- `{CRACK_EVENT}`: the specific moment of recognition
- `{LESSON_OFFERED}`: one sentence, quietly
- `{UNRESOLVED}`: what remains unclear
- `{READER_INVITATION}`: what the reader might do

### Common failure modes
- **Confessor looks good by the end.** The confessor must cost something. If the post ends with the confessor looking more enlightened, it reads as humblebrag.
- **Over-generalizing.** The reader generalizes; the confessor stays specific.
- **Fabricated details.** Readers detect this. The confessional must be true; if the author cannot supply, delegate to named contributors.
- **The crack is too dramatic.** Most recognitions are quiet. A confessional that turns on a spectacular event reads as fiction.

## Template 3 — Claim → Scene → Slogan (the default post)

**Purpose**: Deliver a single claim through a concrete scene, closing on a pull-quote.
**When to use**: The book's default post-shape. Most mid-length posts.
**Target length**: 500–1500 words.
**Primary voice**: Any — Ācārya, Sādhaka, or blended.

### Beats
1. **Claim (10–20% of post)** — one or two sentences. Landed, not argued. *"There are two silences. The silence between thoughts, and the silence beneath them. Do not confuse them."*
2. **Scene (60–80% of post)** — a mini-myth, sensory walk, field report, or confessional vignette. This is where the proving happens. See [06-narration-system.md](06-narration-system.md) §2 for mode selection.
3. **Return to the claim (5–10% of post)** — one sentence that re-states the opening claim in light of the scene. The claim has now earned the reader's yes.
4. **Slogan (5–10% of post)** — a pull-quote-worthy closing line. An imperative, a Sanskrit half-verse, or a compressed aphorism. Designed to be shared.

### Slot-fillers
- `{OPENING_CLAIM}`: the core proposition
- `{SCENE_MODE}`: mini-myth / sensory walk / field report / confessional
- `{SCENE_CONTENT}`: the specific story or image
- `{RETURN_SENTENCE}`: re-statement in light of the scene
- `{CLOSING_SLOGAN}`: the pull-quote

### Worked example (skeleton)
Claim: *"Śraddhā is not belief. It is the posture that makes sādhana possible at all."*
Scene: A 2-paragraph mini-myth — Nachiketa refuses the three gifts. He does not yet know that Yama will answer the fourth question. He has no evidence the fourth answer exists. He waits three days.
Return: *"Nachiketa did not believe in the answer. He was śraddhā-full of the question."*
Slogan: *"Belief is a product of evidence. Śraddhā is the posture that makes evidence possible."*

### Common failure modes
- **Claim already argued in its first sentence.** Kills the scene — nothing is left for the scene to prove.
- **Scene is not specific enough.** Generic story-beats are wallpaper. Details carry.
- **Return sentence is redundant.** If the scene did its job, the return sentence should feel like recognition, not repetition.
- **Slogan is too long.** If it doesn't fit in a tweet, it's not a slogan.

## Template 4 — Imported Voice Deep-Dive

**Purpose**: Let the tradition speak. Primary-source quotation with minimal gloss.
**When to use**: Every 6th–10th post. Also as a closing piece for each Part of the book.
**Target length**: 800–2000 words.
**Primary voice**: Imported Voice (50–70% of word-count), Ācārya-gloss (30–50%).

### Beats
1. **Title** — often the source itself. *"Kaṭha 1.2.1–9."* / *"Yoga-sūtra 1.12–16."* / *"From Vivekacūḍāmaṇi."*
2. **Orienting paragraph (100–200 words)** — what is this text, when was it composed, what is it doing. Keep scholarly apparatus minimal; the reader is being handed a door, not a history lesson.
3. **The passage, quoted (300–700 words)** — extended quotation with Sanskrit (if short enough) and English translation. Blockquoted. The passage does its own work.
4. **Gloss — paragraph by paragraph or verse by verse (400–800 words)** — unpack the technical terms, the moves the passage is making, the claim-structure. Keep the gloss plain; the author is not adding to the passage, the author is opening it.
5. **Return to practice (100–200 words)** — one paragraph: what does this passage mean for the reader's sādhana tomorrow.
6. **Closing (a line from the passage, re-offered without gloss)** — the verse speaks for itself at the end.

### Slot-fillers
- `{SOURCE_TEXT}`: the primary-source location
- `{ORIENTATION}`: date, genre, place in the tradition
- `{PASSAGE_SANSKRIT}` (optional): short Sanskrit if it fits
- `{PASSAGE_TRANSLATION}`: the translated passage
- `{GLOSS_KEYS}`: the technical terms to unpack
- `{PRACTICE_RETURN}`: the sādhana implication

### Common failure modes
- **Gloss longer than the passage.** The author has replaced the text with their reading. Prune gloss.
- **Over-explanation.** The passage is not a puzzle to be solved. Some ambiguities in the original are load-bearing.
- **No practice return.** An imported-voice post that does not land in the reader's life is academic. Always return.

## Template 5 — Aphoristic Coda

**Purpose**: Palate-cleanser between heavier posts. Dense, short, quotable.
**When to use**: 1 per 5–7 posts. Often positioned after a pillar post or after a heavy confessional, to let the reader breathe.
**Target length**: 150–500 words. Can be as short as 50 words.
**Primary voice**: Ācārya or the curator speaking plainly.

### Beats
1. **Title** — one phrase. *"On śraddhā."* / *"The first hinge."* / *"What does not change."*
2. **The aphorisms** — 3–10 short lines, each standalone, each a potential pull-quote. Often in a single paragraph if short; sometimes as a numbered list; sometimes as 2–4 single-sentence paragraphs.
3. **Closing line (often a Sanskrit half-verse or a single imperative)** — lands the post. No commentary.

### Slot-fillers
- `{THEME}`: the concept being aphorized
- `{APHORISMS}`: 3–10 lines, each using one of the seven stickiness mechanics (see [07-reader-assimilation.md](07-reader-assimilation.md) §4)

### Worked example
Title: *"On śraddhā"*
Body:
> *"Śraddhā is not belief.*
> *Belief is an answer. Śraddhā is a posture.*
> *The one who has answers does not need to show up at 4am. The one who has śraddhā shows up.*
> *You will not know what śraddhā is until you have practiced without it. That absence is the first teacher."*

Close: *"— śraddhāvān labhate jñānam —"*

### Common failure modes
- **Aphorisms that are too similar.** 3 inversions in a row cancel each other. Mix the stickiness mechanics.
- **Aphorisms that need context from prior posts.** Coda must stand alone.
- **Closing line that explains.** Let the Sanskrit close. No gloss.

## Template 6 — Field Report

**Purpose**: Contemporary observation, compressed, attached to a śāstric point.
**When to use**: 1 per 4–6 posts. Often in diagnostic Parts (Part One, Part Two).
**Target length**: 250–800 words.
**Primary voice**: Satirist, or a low-heat Ācārya if the target is corporate-spiritual rather than personally satirical.

### Beats
1. **Title** — often enigmatic, sometimes sharp. *"On a Plane to Delhi."* / *"The Weekend Retreat."* / *"What the Interfaith Panel Said."*
2. **The scene (200–400 words)** — a conversation, an event, a composition of a room. One specific detail that anchors (*the man was drinking tomato juice*; *the dais had three chairs*). No names, only types.
3. **The pivot (2–4 sentences)** — what the scene reveals about a mistranslation or misunderstanding.
4. **The śāstric correction (100–200 words)** — the tradition's precision on this exact point, named. A Sanskrit term, a half-verse, an ācārya's line.
5. **Closing (1–2 sentences)** — release. Often a line that opens a door rather than closing one. *"The Saturday class is not the problem. The theory that it is the whole practice is."*

### Slot-fillers
- `{SCENE_SETTING}`: where the observation happened
- `{ANCHORING_DETAIL}`: the one specific that makes it real
- `{MISTRANSLATION}`: what's wrong with the frame being applied
- `{SHASTRIC_CORRECTION}`: the tradition's precise term or move
- `{DOOR_OPEN_CLOSING}`: an invitation, not a verdict

### Common failure modes
- **Named individuals.** Never. Pattern, always.
- **Cruelty in the scene.** The Satirist voice must not bully a type; it must illuminate what the type has missed.
- **Scene longer than 400 words.** Becomes essay. Compress.

## Template 7 — Pillar Braided Post

**Purpose**: A pillar post introducing a core concept-axis, using all four voices.
**When to use**: 2–4 per Part. For the book's central concepts (māyā, ātman, dharma, mokṣa, śraddhā, etc.).
**Target length**: 2500–5000 words.
**Primary voice**: All four, in deliberate sequence.

### Beats (adapt based on concept)
1. **Field report opening (200–400 words)** — Satirist voice. A contemporary scene that reveals the concept's misunderstanding.
2. **Framework exposition (600–1000 words)** — Ācārya voice. The concept as the darśanas name it; scope qualifiers; distinctions across traditions.
3. **Mini-myth (100–200 words)** — a plucked gesture from śāstra.
4. **Second-person sensory walk (300–500 words)** — the reader at a specific place; the concept landing somatically.
5. **Confessional (400–800 words)** — Sādhaka voice. A specific experience of this concept cracking.
6. **Imported Voice deep-reference (400–700 words)** — a long passage from an ācārya-bhāṣya with gloss.
7. **Return to the framework (200–400 words)** — Ācārya voice. What the braid showed that no single voice could.
8. **Closing invocation (50–100 words)** — a Sanskrit verse, closed.

### Common failure modes
- **Braid feels like assembly.** The transitions must make each voice appear inevitable, not scheduled. Spend revision time on the junctions.
- **One voice dominates.** Length-targets above are rough; if a voice occupies 70%+ of the word-count, the braid has failed.
- **The post is a textbook chapter in disguise.** The braid's purpose is to make a concept land from four angles so it becomes native, not to deliver comprehensive coverage.

## Template 8 — Part Opener

**Purpose**: The first post of each Part. Set register, announce the Part's terrain, deliver the Part's question without exhausting it.
**When to use**: Four times in the book — once per Part.
**Target length**: 300–600 words.
**Primary voice**: Ācārya, occasionally braided with Imported Voice.

### Beats
1. **Title** — the Part's title (mythic-poetic). *"The Veil"* / *"The Ground"* / *"The Path"* / *"The Silence."*
2. **Opening declaration (2–4 sentences)** — what this Part will show. Short, oracular, no hedge.
3. **The felt absence (1 paragraph)** — what the reader has been carrying that this Part addresses.
4. **The question this Part opens (1–2 sentences)** — not answered here.
5. **One gesture from the tradition (1 paragraph)** — a mini-myth or a Sanskrit half-verse that frames the Part.
6. **Closing imperative (1 sentence)** — *"Read what follows slowly. Do not rush to Part Two."*

### Common failure modes
- **Summarizes the Part.** Kills the Part. The opener announces; it does not preview.
- **Too long.** Part openers are thin. Keep under 600 words.
- **No question.** The Part must open with a question the Part will not fully answer.

## Template 9 — Part Closer

**Purpose**: The last post of each Part. Release tension; hand the reader off to the next Part; invite a pause.
**When to use**: Four times in the book.
**Target length**: 200–500 words.
**Primary voice**: Imported Voice or Ācārya at low heat.

### Beats
1. **Title** — short, often a phrase from the quoted source. *"A Verse from the Kaṭha."* / *"As Ramana Said."*
2. **A single primary-source quotation** — blockquoted. Long enough to breathe.
3. **Minimal gloss (2–4 sentences)** — what the quote is doing as a closing.
4. **Invitation to pause (1–2 sentences)** — *"Close the book here. Do not begin Part Two tonight."*
5. **Closing mantra or half-verse** — the reader carries this across the gap.

### Common failure modes
- **Summarizes the Part.** Same rule as openers.
- **Previews the next Part.** The closer ends the current Part; the opener opens the next. Do not bridge.
- **Too long.** Part closers are thin.

## 11. Choosing a template

When planning a post, ask:

1. **What is this post's job?** (Introduce a concept → Framework or Pillar. Make a concept felt → Claim→Scene→Slogan. Land emotional ground → Confessional. Satirize a misunderstanding → Field Report. Let the tradition speak → Imported Voice. Palate-cleanse → Aphoristic Coda.)
2. **Where does it sit in the book?** (First post of a Part → Template 8. Last post of a Part → Template 9. Pillar post → Template 7. Standard mid-sequence → Templates 1–6.)
3. **Which concept-axis does it serve?** (See [03-structure-system.md](03-structure-system.md) §4. This determines the concept.)
4. **Which voice rotation?** (See [02-voice-system.md](02-voice-system.md) §1. Don't repeat the previous post's voice.)

---

## 12. Template adherence rules

- **Use the template as scaffold, not cage.** The beats are a starting architecture. Specific posts may skip a beat or add one.
- **But do not mix templates within a post.** A post is one template. A braided post (Template 7) is a single template that includes multi-voice passages; it is not a merger of Template 1 and Template 2.
- **If a post cannot fit any template, something is wrong.** Either the post is multi-post (split it) or the post lacks a clear job (define the job first).
