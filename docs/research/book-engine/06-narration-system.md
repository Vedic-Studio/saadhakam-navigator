# Narration System

The story-units that do the arguing. Four narration modes, their structure, when to use each, how they stack.

Cross-reference: [sources/bam-analysis.md](sources/bam-analysis.md) §8; [sources/trp-analysis.md](sources/trp-analysis.md) §7, §9.

---

## 1. Why narration carries the book

Both source books replace argument with story. Not as ornament — as *primary evidence*. BAM does not argue for hierarchy; it hands the reader Alcibiades. TRP does not argue for hypergamy; it hands the reader Old Fat and Bald's divorce. The reader processes a character, and the disposition transfers.

This is the single most important transferable move. The Sadhaka book must do at least half its persuasion through story-units. The tradition supplies abundant source material; the craft is in how each story is pruned and delivered.

## 2. Four narration modes

### Mode A — Mini-myth (one-gesture plucked from a large story)
The writer does not retell the myth. The writer **plucks a single gesture** and hands it to the reader as if already known.

BAP exemplifies the move: *"Pythagoras, looking on Euphorbus' shield in Sicily, broke into tears, remembering that he had been this man."* — one sentence, carrying a claim about reincarnation that would require pages to argue. The reader feels initiated because the detail is specific and the story is treated as already shared.

**Sanatan source material**:
- Nachiketa at Yama's door (three-day silence; the refused gifts; the fourth question)
- Arjuna on the battlefield (the moment of collapse between the armies)
- Yājñavalkya leaving his household (the dialogue with Maitreyī; "for the sake of the self")
- Markandeya in the deluge (the child who saw the dissolution and the re-creation)
- Prahlāda's three moments (the furnace, the pillar, the lap)
- Tulsidas and the line of blood (the night the ink ran)
- Ramana at Tiruvannamalai (the seventeen-year-old asking "who dies?")
- Shuka at birth (the sage who came out mature and walked away)
- Hanuman's ring of Rāma
- Sīta's trial by fire — and the śāstric controversy about whether it happened at all
- Uddhava after the Kurukshetra, waiting for the second teaching
- Ādi Śaṅkara at the feet of his mother at the cremation ground, reciting *Mātṛ-pañcaka*

**Craft rules**:
- **One sentence, maximum two.** If you retell the myth, you kill it. Hand over only the gesture.
- **Specificity**: name the place, the number (three days, twelve years, eight years old), the object (the shield, the ring, the pillar).
- **Treat the story as already known**: *"Of course Nachiketa refused the three gifts. You know the three gifts."* — even if the reader does not. The phrasing invites them to know.
- **Integrate with the surrounding argument**: the gesture carries a specific point, not a general vibe. Before writing the gesture, know what claim it is making.

### Mode B — Second-person sensory walk
Put the reader inside a scene. Their body does the argument.

BAP's Aetna passage is the gold standard: the reader stands at the rim, smells the molten rock, feels the heat, recalls an abandoned gas station, pulls back. Five sentences that do what a three-page essay on modern disenchantment could not.

**Sanatan-register sensory walks**:
- Kashi ghat at 4am (the smell of wet stone, boat wood, marigold, smoke; the quality of sound)
- Kedarnath in a storm (the path, the wind, the bell, the approach)
- A village cremation at dusk (the grandfather's hand, the fire, the silence that is not peace)
- The temple kitchen (ghee, copper, the grain on the stone, the priest's hands at the grinding)
- Rameshwaram at dawn (the twenty-two wells; the cold water; the first sun)
- A banyan at noon (the stillness; the sound returning after a long silence; what was always there)
- The river at monsoon (the sound of the water at night; the boat tied to the bank; the rope creaking)
- Govardhan on foot (seven hours; the feet; the changes in the terrain; the shift that is not in the terrain)

**Craft rules**:
- **Present tense, second-person.** *"You are at Kashi."* Not *"imagine you are."* The scene is happening.
- **Three senses minimum.** Sight, sound, smell, touch, temperature. If only sight, the scene does not land in the body.
- **One pivot.** The scene starts in the ordinary, pivots once, lands the reader elsewhere. Pivots can be sensory (a sudden silence), temporal (the first light), or internal (a thought that surfaces).
- **End with an invitation, not a conclusion.** The walk opens a door; it does not close one. *"You are there. Now — what?"*

### Mode C — Confessional / testimony
First-person, in-progress. A specific failure or recognition, told in specifics, with a lesson offered only at the end.

TRP's dominant mode. The confessor stands, names the humiliation, emerges with a law. Old Fat and Bald's "After my first divorce I commiserated with a female secretary…" is a tight example.

**Structural beats of a confessional**:
1. **The specific opening** — date, place, object, conversation. Ground the reader in a real scene. *"In 2017, in a rented room in Rishikesh, I was on day 40 of a 41-day prāṇāyāma schedule."*
2. **The admission** — what I thought I was doing, what I was actually doing. *"I thought I was doing sādhana. I was practicing for someone to notice."*
3. **The moment of recognition** — a specific event that cracked the self-deception. *"On day 40, a small boy at the ashram asked me what I was counting. I had no answer. I had not noticed I was counting."*
4. **The quiet lesson** — one sentence, pulled out at the end, offered rather than preached. *"The practice that cannot be seen by anyone is the practice."*

**Voice rules**:
- **The Sādhaka voice only.** Never the Ācārya, never the Imported Voice.
- **Admit more than is comfortable.** If the confessional feels safe, it is not yet good enough. The cost is the credit.
- **Do not generalize prematurely.** The reader will generalize. Your job is to stay specific.
- **End with a door for the reader, not a command.** *"I do not know what this means for your practice. You will have to notice what you are counting."*

**Constraint**: **these must be true.** If the author cannot supply them from their own life, delegate the Sādhaka voice to named contributors whose stories are real. Fabricated confessionals are the fastest way to destroy the book. TRP gets away with some light embellishment because it is anonymous and communal; the Sadhaka book, publishing under a named author on a living tradition, does not have this license.

### Mode D — Field report
A moment from contemporary life — a conversation, a news image, an encounter — narrated compressed, then attached to a śāstric point.

The anthology contract TRP follows: **no story without a lesson, no lesson without a story**.

**Sanatan-register field reports**:
- A conversation overheard on a plane — a man explaining his "spiritual journey" to a seatmate
- A photograph in the paper — an interfaith panel, the specific composition of the dais
- A week at a wellness retreat — what was said, what was not said, what was sold
- A funeral — what the relatives said to the grieving, and what the paramparā actually teaches
- A temple visit — what the priest assumed you were there for, and what you discovered you were there for
- A yoga class — the teacher's instruction, the class's response, the specific translation that was wrong

**Craft rules**:
- **Compress aggressively.** The field report is 150–400 words. Longer than that and it becomes essay.
- **One detail that anchors.** The plane seatmate was drinking tomato juice. The priest wore three strands of rudraksha. The teacher said "your inner goddess." Specifics are load-bearing.
- **The śāstric pivot comes at the end.** The field report sets up the ground; a Sanskrit term, a śloka, or a single line from the paramparā lands the point. *"This is what the śāstra meant by adhyāsa — the superimposition of one thing on another, so thoroughly that the original disappears."*
- **Never name real people.** Patterns and types, always. A real-named person in a field report compromises the book's ethics and its legal safety.

## 3. How the four modes stack within a single post

A pillar post (2000–3000 words) can use all four. Example architecture for a post on *māyā*:

1. Open with a **field report** (200 words) — overheard conversation: someone describing their "manifestation" practice. Land the pivot: this is not what māyā names.
2. Ācārya-voice **framework** (400 words) — what māyā actually is in the darśanas; the difference between *avidyā* and *māyā* and their readings across Advaita, Viśiṣṭādvaita, Kashmir Shaiva.
3. **Mini-myth** (100 words) — Markandeya's moment in the deluge, as anchor.
4. **Second-person sensory walk** (300 words) — the reader at the ghat at 4am, the moment the city disappears and something remains.
5. **Confessional** (400 words) — the Sādhaka voice, a specific moment of dissolution, a specific return.
6. Ācārya-voice **return to the framework** (300 words) — what the four layers together showed that no single layer could.
7. **Invocation** (50 words) — a śloka, closed.

A standard mid-length post (800–1500 words) uses 2–3 of the modes. A short post uses 1.

**Sequencing craft**: adjacent modes should contrast. Never field-report into field-report. Never two confessionals in the same post. The reader's attention needs the register-change the modes provide.

## 4. When to use each mode

| If the post is… | Lead with mode | Support with |
|---|---|---|
| Introducing a new concept | Framework (Ācārya) + Mini-myth | Sensory walk |
| Diagnosing a modern condition | Field report | Mini-myth or sensory walk |
| Making a counter-cultural claim | Staircase of sources (see [05](05-edgy-ideas-playbook.md)) + Mini-myth | Confessional if available |
| Describing a practice | Sensory walk + Ācārya instructions | Mini-myth or confessional |
| Deepening a concept the reader has met before | Confessional | Mini-myth; Ācārya only if a new scope distinction is needed |
| Making a subtle reversal | Field report + concession-then-flip | Sensory walk |
| Closing a Part | Sensory walk or Imported Voice | Nothing else — keep it thin |

## 5. Anti-patterns in narration

- **Retelling a myth in full.** If you tell the full Kaṭha in your book, you have replaced the Kaṭha with your retelling. Pluck the gesture. The full text is elsewhere; your book points at it.
- **Generic sensory walks.** "You are sitting quietly. You feel peace." This is not a sensory walk; this is pop-spirituality. Specifics: 4am, granite, the bell, the boat, the particular quality of light. Without specifics the scene does not land.
- **Confessionals without cost.** A confessional in which the confessor looks good by the end is not a confessional. The reader has a working detector for this; false confession burns the book's credit worse than no confession.
- **Field reports with named individuals.** Never. Pattern, always.
- **Myth as ornament, not evidence.** If the myth does not carry a specific point, cut it. The book is not a retelling; it is an argument-through-story.
- **Narration that stacks same-mode.** Two confessionals in a row. Three mini-myths in a paragraph. The reader's rhythm breaks. Mix modes or separate with a voice-change.

## 6. The reader's trust curve

Narration modes are weighted differently on the reader's trust meter:

- **Mini-myth** — inherited authority. Trust comes free from the tradition. The risk is under-trust: reader feels distant because the story is old.
- **Second-person sensory walk** — earns trust through somatic specificity. The reader's body agrees before their mind does.
- **Confessional** — high trust if specific and costly; burns all trust instantly if fake or vague.
- **Field report** — high trust if the observation is exact; low if generic.

**The engineering**: early in the book, lean on mini-myths (inherited authority) and sensory walks (somatic earning). Once the reader trusts the book, deliver the confessionals (highest emotional charge) and field reports (sharpest contemporary edge). Reverse this order and the confessionals arrive before the reader has reason to trust the confessor.

## 7. Stocking the book with narration

Before drafting, build three repositories:

1. **Mini-myth bank** — 40–60 gestures plucked from śruti, itihāsa, purāṇa, and hagiography. For each: source, one-line version of the gesture, one-line claim-it-carries. This is the book's narrative reservoir.

2. **Sensory walk library** — 15–20 places, each walked in first draft at 200–400 words. Kashi, Kedarnath, Rameshwaram, Varanasi's kitchen streets, Govardhan, Tiruvannamalai, Ujjain, Prayagraj, a village temple in the author's hometown, a cremation at dusk. Each walk is a reusable asset.

3. **Confessional archive** — the author's own, plus named contributors'. Specific, dated, named. This is the most difficult to stock and the most valuable. The archive must be honest; it need not be public — some confessionals are private and only their lessons are delivered into the book.

These three repositories are prerequisites to drafting. Without them, the book reverts to essay. See [09-templates-kit.md](09-templates-kit.md) for how each mode fits into post-templates; see [10-prompt-engine.md](10-prompt-engine.md) for LLM prompts that draft each mode.

*Narration is how ideas enter the body. The book's conviction is the sum of its stories, not of its claims.*
