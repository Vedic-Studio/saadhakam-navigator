## Territory

Purva Mimamsa is the philosophical school dedicated to interpreting Vedic injunctions (vidhi) and defending the authority of Vedic ritual action (karma). Its central claim is radical: the Vedas are authorless (apaurusheya), eternal, and self-validating. Dharma is not knowable through perception, inference, or divine revelation. It is knowable only through Vedic injunction. Ritual action performed with correct procedure generates an unseen potency (apurva) that produces results, including heavenly rewards and worldly prosperity. This makes Mimamsa the most epistemologically disciplined school in Sanatan philosophy and the one most committed to textual hermeneutics.

Within the broader tradition, Mimamsa occupies a paradoxical position. It is the philosophical backbone of Vedic orthodoxy, providing the interpretive framework that every other school either builds upon or reacts against. Yet it has no theology of liberation in the later Vedantic sense. Jaimini's concern is dharma (right action), not moksha (release). This made Mimamsa the "establishment" school that Vedanta (Uttara Mimamsa) partially superseded by redirecting attention from ritual to knowledge. Writers should understand that when any Vedantic philosopher argues about scriptural interpretation, they are using Mimamsa's hermeneutic rules, whether or not they acknowledge the debt.

## Lineage & Key Figures

- **Jaimini** (~3rd-2nd c. BCE) — Mimamsa Sutras (12 chapters, ~2500 sutras). Established the school's foundational hermeneutic framework.
- **Shabara** (~1st c. BCE) — Shabara Bhashya, the authoritative commentary on Jaimini's Sutras
- **Kumarila Bhatta** (~7th c.) — Shlokavartika, Tantravartika. Bhatta sub-school. Defended Vedic authority against Buddhist epistemology. Recognized inference and other pramanas alongside scripture.
- **Prabhakara** (~7th c.) — Brihati. Prabhakara sub-school. Stricter on pramanas: holds that error is not misapprehension but non-apprehension (akhyati).
- **Mandana Mishra** (~8th c.) — Vidhiviveka. Bridge figure between Mimamsa and Advaita Vedanta.

## Core Concepts

- **Apaurusheya** (authorlessness) — the Vedas have no author, human or divine. They are eternal sound patterns that encode dharma.
- **Vidhi** (injunction) — the Veda's primary function is to prescribe action. Statements of fact (arthavada) serve only to motivate compliance with injunctions.
- **Apurva** (unseen potency) — the invisible force generated by correctly performed ritual that bridges the gap between action and its future result
- **Dharma** (duty/right action) — known exclusively through Vedic injunction. Not discoverable by reason alone.
- **Shabda-pramana** (verbal testimony as a valid means of knowledge) — Mimamsa's most influential contribution. Scriptural language is a pramana on par with perception and inference.
- **Arthavada** (explanatory passage) — Vedic passages that describe rather than prescribe. Subordinate to injunctions, serving to praise or blame.
- **Yajna** (sacrifice/ritual) — the structured ritual act that, when performed correctly, generates apurva and sustains cosmic and social order

## Common Misreadings

- Dismissing Mimamsa as "mere ritualism." Its hermeneutic methods and epistemological arguments are among the most sophisticated in Sanatan philosophy.
- Treating apaurusheya as a faith claim. Mimamsa defends it through epistemological argument: if the Vedas had an author, their authority would depend on that author's reliability, creating an infinite regress.
- Assuming Mimamsa has no philosophical depth because it lacks a liberation theology. Its contributions to philosophy of language, epistemology, and legal interpretation are foundational.
- Confusing Purva Mimamsa (karma kanda, ritual hermeneutics) with Uttara Mimamsa (Vedanta, knowledge of Brahman). They share a name and textual basis but have different concerns.

## Internal Tensions

- Bhatta vs. Prabhakara on the theory of error: is error a positive misapprehension (viparita-khyati, Bhatta) or a failure to apprehend fully (akhyati, Prabhakara)?
- Can Mimamsa accommodate moksha? Later Mimamsakas attempted to integrate liberation into the system, but Jaimini's original framework has no obvious place for it.
- Relationship to Vedanta: if the Vedas' primary purpose is injunction (karma kanda), what is the status of the Upanishads (jnana kanda)? Shankara argued that knowledge supersedes action; Mimamsa resists this.
- Status of Vedic sacrifice in a world where fire rituals are rarely performed: is the philosophical framework separable from its ritual application?

## Modern Bridges

- Apaurusheya maps onto mathematical Platonism: truths that exist independently of any mind discovering them. Breaks because Mimamsa applies this not to abstract objects but to prescriptive language.
- Vidhi (injunction-centered reading) parallels legal positivism: the law is what the authoritative text says, not what reason derives. Breaks because Mimamsa's "legislator" is no one; the text is self-authorizing.
- Apurva resonates with the concept of deferred execution in programming: an action now produces a result that manifests later. Breaks because apurva is not mechanistic but operates through a metaphysical potency with no physical substrate.
- Shabda-pramana (testimony as knowledge) anticipates epistemology of testimony debates in analytic philosophy (Coady, Lackey). Breaks because Mimamsa grants testimony inherent authority, not authority contingent on speaker reliability.

## Adjacent Domains

- **advaita-vedanta** — Shankara's Vedanta is literally "Uttara Mimamsa" (later Mimamsa). He uses Mimamsa hermeneutics but redirects from action to knowledge. Mandana Mishra bridges both.
- **yoga-samkhya** — Samkhya provides a cosmological framework that can complement or compete with Mimamsa's ritual cosmology. Different epistemological commitments.
- **shaktism-tantra** — tantric ritual both absorbs and transforms Vedic ritual structures. Mimamsa's hermeneutic categories reappear in tantric scriptural interpretation.
