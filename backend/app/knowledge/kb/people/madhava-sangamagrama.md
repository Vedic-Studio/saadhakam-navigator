## One-line identity

The founder of the **Kerala school of mathematics and astronomy** (14th–17th c. CE) whose work on infinite series for pi, sine, cosine, and arctangent anticipated parts of the calculus by roughly 250 years.

## Life

Very little is known about Madhava's life directly. He lived and worked at **Sangamagrama** (modern Irinjalakuda, Kerala) in the late 14th and early 15th centuries. His dates are generally given as c. 1340–1425 CE but the precision is limited. He was the first in a brilliant lineage of Kerala mathematicians running through **Parameshvara** (his student, c. 1360–1455), **Damodara**, **Nilakantha Somayaji** (c. 1444–1544, author of the Tantrasangraha), and **Jyesthadeva** (c. 1500–1575, author of the Yuktibhasa).

**Almost all of Madhava's own writings are lost.** What we know of his mathematical results comes primarily from the works of his intellectual successors, especially Nilakantha's Tantrasangraha and Jyesthadeva's Yuktibhasa, which attribute specific infinite series and theorems to him by name. This is an unusual situation in the history of mathematics — Madhava is an attested historical figure whose contributions are preserved almost entirely at second hand through his school.

## Works

- **Venvaroha** — a short work on lunar positions; one of the few surviving texts attributed to Madhava directly
- **Various lost works** on trigonometry and astronomy referenced by later Kerala mathematicians
- The **infinite series results** attributed to him by Nilakantha and Jyesthadeva — the bulk of his mathematical legacy

## What they are cited for in this KB

- The **Madhava-Leibniz series** for pi: π/4 = 1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − 1/7 + ... — derived by Madhava roughly 250 years before Leibniz
- The **power series for sine, cosine, and arctangent** that are mathematically equivalent to the Taylor series for these functions — derived by Madhava with remainder terms and accuracy estimates
- The Kerala school's **method of successive approximations** and systematic treatment of convergence
- Founding the intellectual lineage that produced the Tantrasangraha, Yuktibhasa, and Karanapaddhati — the texts that preserve the Kerala school's calculus-like results
- The argument that the Kerala school's work is "substantial pre-calculus" or "early calculus" depending on how one defines the term (see below for the scope limit)

## What they are NOT to be credited with

- **Inventing the calculus.** The Kerala school developed infinite series for specific functions (pi, sine, cosine, arctangent) with remarkable precision and rigor, but they did not develop the full differential/integral duality or the fundamental theorem of calculus. Whether this counts as "inventing calculus" depends on what one means by calculus. Careful historians frame it as "substantial pre-calculus work" or "calculus-like results," not as "calculus."
- **Definitively transmitting calculus to Europe.** The argument (most prominently by C. K. Raju) that Jesuit missionaries in Kerala transmitted the Kerala school's results to Europe, which then informed Newton and Leibniz, is suggestive but not conclusively established. Most historians find the evidence interesting but not a proof of transmission.
- **Working alone.** The results preserved under Madhava's name are products of a school with continuous transmission over ~200 years; Madhava is the founder but the school as a whole is the unit to credit.
