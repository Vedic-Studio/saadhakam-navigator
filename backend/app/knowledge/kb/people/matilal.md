## One-line identity

The modern scholar who did more than any other single figure to place classical Indian philosophy — especially **Nyaya** and Indian logic — into serious dialogue with analytic philosophy, and who shaped a generation of English-language work on Indian philosophy as the first **Spalding Professor of Eastern Religions and Ethics** at Oxford (1977–1991).

## Life

B. K. Matilal was born in Calcutta in 1935, studied at Calcutta University and then at Harvard (Ph.D. 1965 under Daniel Ingalls), taught at the University of Toronto, and in 1977 became the Spalding Professor of Eastern Religions and Ethics at Oxford — the chair Radhakrishnan had held — a position he retained until his death in 1991. His work covered Navya-Nyaya logic and epistemology, perception theory, Indian philosophy of language, ethics (including a notable book on the Mahabharata as moral philosophy), and comparative philosophy. He trained a generation of scholars in both traditional Indian learning and analytic philosophy; **Jonardon Ganeri**, Arindam Chakrabarti, and several other leading contemporary scholars of Indian philosophy are in his intellectual lineage.

Matilal's distinctive methodological move was to take Indian philosophical arguments seriously **as philosophy** — not as religious doctrine, not as cultural heritage, but as philosophical positions that can be engaged, criticized, and compared with analytic philosophical work. Before Matilal, much Western academic study of Indian philosophy treated it as a historical or cultural artifact; after Matilal, the argument that Indian philosophy is a live tradition with philosophical claims that analytic philosophers can productively engage with became mainstream.

## Works

- **Epistemology, Logic, and Grammar in Indian Philosophical Analysis** (Mouton 1971) — early programmatic statement
- **The Navya-Nyaya Doctrine of Negation** (Harvard 1968) — technical work on Navya-Nyaya's formal apparatus
- **Perception: An Essay on Classical Indian Theories of Knowledge** (Oxford 1986) — perhaps his most widely cited single book
- **Logic, Language and Reality** (Motilal Banarsidass 1985)
- **The Character of Logic in India** (SUNY 1998, posthumous; edited by Jonardon Ganeri and Heeraman Tiwari) — his most accessible general statement on Indian logic
- **Collected Essays**, 2 vols., edited by Jonardon Ganeri (Oxford 2002)

## What they are cited for in this KB

- The programmatic argument for engaging classical Indian philosophy as philosophy, not as cultural artifact
- Rigorous modern treatment of **Navya-Nyaya** — the 13th c. onward technical logical tradition starting with Gangesha
- The contemporary standard account of classical Indian **theories of perception and inference**
- The argument that Nyaya contains genuinely sophisticated logical work without overclaiming that it is simply equivalent to modern first-order logic
- Training the generation of modern scholars (Ganeri, Chakrabarti, etc.) who now carry this program forward

## What they are NOT to be credited with

- **Writing as an Indian apologist.** Matilal was rigorously critical and did not defend positions merely because they were Indian; his method was philosophical engagement, not cultural advocacy.
- **Claiming that Indian logic is "better than" Western logic.** His comparative work is careful and non-triumphalist.
- **Being the only modern scholar doing this work.** Matilal is foundational for English-language analytic engagement with Indian philosophy but works alongside contemporaries like Karl Potter, Stephen Phillips, Mark Siderits, and others.
