## One-line identity

The 7th-century founder of the **Bhatta school of Purva Mimamsa** whose *Shlokavarttika*, *Tantravarttika*, and *Tuptika* are among the most philosophically sophisticated works of classical Indian philosophy, engaging directly with Buddhist epistemology and defending the authority of the Veda with remarkable argumentative rigor.

## Life

Kumarila Bhatta is generally placed in the 7th c. CE, roughly contemporary with or slightly earlier than Shankara. Traditional biographies associate him with polemical engagement with Buddhism at a time when Buddhism was still intellectually dominant in much of India; a famous but certainly legendary story has him participating in a debate contest with a Buddhist scholar and, upon losing, throwing himself on a pyre. Like most classical Indian thinkers, his biographical details are mostly traditional or legendary; what we have is the texts and their philosophical content.

Kumarila is the founder of the **Bhatta sub-school** of Purva Mimamsa, parallel to the **Prabhakara sub-school** founded by his contemporary Prabhakara. The two sub-schools differ significantly on the theory of meaning, the role of injunctions, and the analysis of perception. Later Mimamsa scholarship largely develops within one or the other of these two lineages.

## Works

- **Shlokavarttika** — verse commentary on the first part of Shabara's Mimamsa Bhashya; contains the most important philosophical material, including extended treatments of perception, inference, verbal testimony, presumption (arthapatti), non-apprehension (anupalabdhi), and the authority of the Veda
- **Tantravarttika** — prose commentary on the middle books of Shabara's Mimamsa Bhashya
- **Tuptika** — commentary on the remaining books

## What they are cited for in this KB

- The **Bhatta school's theory of meaning** and ritual injunction
- The **six means of knowledge** (pramanas) in Kumarila's systematization — perception, inference, verbal testimony, analogy, presumption (arthapatti), non-apprehension (anupalabdhi) — expanding Nyaya's four to six
- The **Mimamsa defense of Vedic authority and apaurusheyatva** — the most sophisticated classical argument for the Veda as authorless
- The **engagement with Buddhist epistemology** — Kumarila's critique of Dignaga's and Dharmakirti's theories of perception and inference is one of the great philosophical confrontations of classical Indian philosophy
- The concept of **svatah-pramanya** (intrinsic validity of cognition) — Kumarila's thesis that a cognition is prima facie valid unless specifically defeated, a position that has modern parallels in foundationalist epistemology

## What they are NOT to be credited with

- **Defeating Buddhism single-handedly.** The traditional narrative of Kumarila as the thinker whose debates caused the decline of Indian Buddhism is a later legendary framing. Buddhism's historical trajectory in India is complex and was not determined by any single polemical episode.
- **Being the originator of Mimamsa.** Kumarila builds on Jaimini's Mimamsa Sutras and Shabara's Bhashya; he is the sophisticator, not the founder, of the tradition.
- **Being straightforwardly a "theist."** Mimamsa's position on Ishvara/God is complex — Kumarila argues for the authority of the Veda but is careful not to make it depend on a divine author.
