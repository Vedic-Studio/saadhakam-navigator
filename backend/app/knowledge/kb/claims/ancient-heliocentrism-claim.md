## Plain-language statement

A popular claim says Aryabhata discovered heliocentrism — that the earth revolves around the sun — "a thousand years before Copernicus." The careful version is that Aryabhata argues for **axial rotation** of the earth (the earth spins on its axis, producing the apparent daily motion of the stars), which is a **distinct and weaker** claim than heliocentrism. His planetary model is not heliocentric: the sun, moon, and planets still revolve around a central earth, just a rotating one. The conflation of axial rotation with heliocentrism is the central category error in popular accounts.

Aryabhata's actual claim is still historically impressive — he made this argument in 499 CE, in the face of a scientific and philosophical culture that overwhelmingly treated the earth as stationary, and he was indeed critiqued for it by later Indian astronomers including Brahmagupta. But the honest framing ("Aryabhata argued for earth rotation a thousand years before Galileo") is more accurate and, for readers who know the history of astronomy, more impressive than the loose framing that conflates this with heliocentrism.

The **separately interesting question** of whether classical Indian planetary models contained heliocentric elements more subtly — specifically whether the equant-like device in some Indian planetary models has a latent heliocentric structure — is a live scholarly question (see Duke 2005), but the answer is not "Aryabhata believed the sun is the center."

## Decomposed sub-claims

1. **"Aryabhata argued that the earth rotates on its axis."** — **Supported.** This is explicit in Gola Pada (Book 4) verse 9 of the Aryabhatiya (499 CE): "Just as a person in a moving boat sees the unmoving trees on the bank moving in the opposite direction, so from Lanka [the equator] a person sees the unmoving asterisms moving uniformly westward." The boat analogy makes the claim unmistakable.

2. **"Aryabhata argued that the earth revolves around the sun (heliocentrism)."** — **Not supported.** There is no passage in the Aryabhatiya that places the sun at the center of the planetary system. The Aryabhatiya's planetary model is geocentric — the earth is at the center of the universe, and the sun, moon, and planets move around it. What is unusual is that this geocentric earth is not stationary: it rotates on its axis.

3. **"Aryabhata's earth-rotation claim was controversial in the Indian tradition."** — **Supported.** Brahmagupta (628 CE), in Chapter 11 of the Brahmasphutasiddhanta, explicitly rejects Aryabhata's earth-rotation claim and defends the standard stationary-earth view. Most later Indian astronomers sided with Brahmagupta. Aryabhata's rotation claim was a minority position within the Indian tradition itself.

4. **"Copernicus was influenced by Aryabhata."** — **Not supported.** There is no evidence of any direct transmission of Aryabhata's work to Copernicus. Copernicus (1473–1543) worked within the European mathematical astronomy tradition that had its own indirect inheritance from Greek astronomy via Arabic intermediaries, but no clear line from the Aryabhata school to Europe is established for the heliocentric question specifically.

5. **"Classical Indian planetary models contain a latent heliocentric structure."** — **Contested; interesting scholarly question.** Dennis Duke (2005, Archive for History of Exact Sciences) has argued that the way Indian astronomers (including the Aryabhata school) handled the planetary equant suggests an implicit heliocentric framing at the computational level. This is a live and technical debate. It does not translate into "Aryabhata was a heliocentrist" — it is a subtler claim about the mathematical structure of the models.

6. **"The Kerala school (14th c. CE) developed a heliocentric model."** — **Partially supported for a specific case.** Nilakantha Somayaji in the Tantrasangraha (1501 CE) develops a planetary model in which the inner planets (Mercury and Venus) revolve around the sun, which itself revolves around the earth — a **partial heliocentric / Tychonic-like model** specifically for inner planets. This is a narrow but historically interesting claim that Indian astronomy did develop in heliocentric-adjacent directions in the late medieval period. It is still not full Copernican heliocentrism.

7. **"The Vedas / Vedangas contained heliocentric cosmology."** — **Not supported.** Claims that the Rigveda or the Vedanga Jyotisha contain heliocentric cosmology are over-readings of metaphorical or poetic passages. These texts do not articulate a heliocentric planetary model. The Vedanga Jyotisha is a calendrical text, not a cosmological one.

## Evidence for

- **Primary**: Aryabhatiya Gola Pada 9 — the explicit earth-rotation passage with the boat analogy
- **Primary counter-evidence**: Brahmasphutasiddhanta Chapter 11 — Brahmagupta's explicit rejection of Aryabhata's rotation claim
- **Primary (Kerala school)**: Tantrasangraha (1501 CE) — Nilakantha Somayaji's partial heliocentric treatment of Mercury and Venus
- **Modern scholarship**: Plofker's Mathematics in India is the standard treatment; Shukla-Sarma's INSA edition of the Aryabhatiya is the scholarly reference; Duke 2005 on the equant question is the current state of the technical debate

## Evidence against / counterpoints

- Aryabhata's planetary model is geocentric; his statements about sun, moon, and planetary positions all presuppose a central earth
- Brahmagupta's explicit rejection of the rotation claim in 628 CE shows that the claim was not considered obvious or convincing within the Indian tradition itself
- The claim that Copernicus was influenced by Aryabhata requires a transmission pathway that has never been demonstrated
- Full heliocentrism — a sun-centered planetary system — is not articulated in any Indian text before the modern period

## Scope boundaries

- This claim is **not** denying Aryabhata's contribution to the history of astronomy. His earth-rotation argument (499 CE) is historically impressive and philosophically sophisticated.
- This claim **is** distinguishing earth rotation (supported for Aryabhata) from heliocentrism (not supported for Aryabhata) — these are different astronomical claims.
- This claim **is** noting that Nilakantha's Kerala-school model contains a narrow heliocentric element (inner planets around the sun), which is historically interesting but not the popular claim.
- The responsible framing: **"Aryabhata argued in 499 CE that the earth rotates on its axis — a remarkable claim for its time, which was rejected by later Indian astronomers including Brahmagupta. This is distinct from heliocentrism (a sun-centered planetary system), which Aryabhata did not propose. Nilakantha Somayaji's Kerala school in the 16th century developed a partially heliocentric model for the inner planets, but full Copernican heliocentrism does not appear in classical Indian astronomy."**

## Reusable examples

- **The boat analogy (Gola Pada 9)**: "Just as a person in a moving boat sees the unmoving trees on the bank moving in the opposite direction, so from Lanka a person sees the unmoving asterisms moving uniformly westward." This is the locus classicus of the earth-rotation claim. It is a beautiful piece of reasoning by analogy — and it is *specifically* about rotation, not revolution around the sun.
- **Brahmagupta's rejection (Brahmasphutasiddhanta Chapter 11)**: Brahmagupta explicitly argues against Aryabhata's rotation claim. The fact that later Indian astronomers did not universally accept the rotation thesis is itself important historical context — Aryabhata was not establishing a consensus Indian view.
- **Nilakantha's partial heliocentrism (Tantrasangraha, 1501 CE)**: a specific and narrow claim — in Nilakantha's model, Mercury and Venus revolve around the sun, which itself revolves around the earth. This is a Tychonic-like partial heliocentrism that is historically interesting in its own right. It is not Copernican heliocentrism but it *is* an Indian astronomical model with heliocentric elements.
- **The category error**: "rotation" and "heliocentrism" are different claims. A person who has heard the popular claim that "Aryabhata discovered heliocentrism" can be corrected by clarifying what Aryabhata actually said (earth rotates) and why that is still historically interesting.

- When an article mentions Aryabhata, ancient Indian astronomy, or "who discovered heliocentrism first"
