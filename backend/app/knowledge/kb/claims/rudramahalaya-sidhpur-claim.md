## Plain-language statement

The Rudramahalaya temple at Sidhpur (Siddhapura) in modern Mehsana district, Gujarat, was a vast Shaiva temple complex commissioned by Siddhraj Jayasimha of the Chaulukya (Solanki) dynasty in the early 12th century CE. The complex was partly destroyed by Ulugh Khan (later Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq's predecessor as Alauddin Khalji's general, conducting the Gujarat campaign of 1297–98) and partly destroyed in the early 15th century by Ahmed Shah I, founder of the independent Gujarat Sultanate (r. 1411–1442). The Jami Masjid of Sidhpur was subsequently constructed using the temple's materials and within the temple's western precinct. ASI excavations have exposed the temple plinth and a Shiva linga within the mosque premises. This case is unusually well-documented in modern Indian government records because the Fourth Annual Report of the Government of India's Minorities' Commission (1983) — a Government of India publication, not a religious-nationalist source — explicitly describes the conversion: "This temple seems to have been destroyed partly by Ulugh Khan in AD 1297–98 and partly by Ahmedshah in AD 1415. Some of the cubicles and a number of pillars on the Western side of the temple it would appear were later converted into a mosque."

## Decomposed sub-claims

1. **"The Rudramahalaya temple at Sidhpur was a major 12th-century Shaiva complex built by Siddhraj Jayasimha of the Chaulukya dynasty."** — **Supported.** Siddhraj Jayasimha (r. c. 1092–1142) was the most powerful Chaulukya ruler; Sidhpur (Siddhapura) is named after him and was a major pilgrimage centre. The Rudramahalaya complex was conceived as a temple dedicated to the eleven Rudras (manifestations of Shiva), and surviving descriptions and architectural fragments suggest it was among the largest western Indian temples of its period — comparable in scale to the better-preserved Modhera Sun Temple (also Chaulukya, c. 1026). Pre-destruction descriptions in Sanskrit prashasti literature and Jain inscriptions confirm its existence and importance.

2. **"Ulugh Khan partly destroyed the Rudramahalaya during Alauddin Khalji's 1297–98 Gujarat campaign."** — **Supported.** Alauddin Khalji's Gujarat campaign of 1297–98 — led by his generals Ulugh Khan and Nusrat Khan — is documented in Ziauddin Barani's *Tarikh-i-Firuz Shahi* and in the Jain chronicle tradition (e.g., Merutunga's *Prabandhachintamani*). The campaign included the famous destruction of Somnath (the Khalji-period reconstruction, not the 1026 Mahmud-period structure), Patan, and several other Gujarat temple sites. The Sidhpur destruction belongs to this campaign cycle. The Government of India Minorities' Commission report dates this phase to 1297–98 explicitly.

3. **"Ahmed Shah I completed the destruction in 1415 CE."** — **Supported.** Ahmed Shah I founded Ahmedabad in 1411 and consolidated the independent Gujarat Sultanate; his temple-destruction policy is documented in the Gujarat Persian chronicles (*Mirat-i-Sikandari*, *Mirat-i-Ahmadi*). The 1415 date for the Rudramahalaya's second destruction comes from these chronicles and the ASI's excavation reports cited by the Minorities' Commission. The two-stage destruction (1297–98 partial, 1415 completion) is the distinctive feature of the Sidhpur case — most Mughal-period destructions were single events; the Sidhpur case reflects the longer process by which a major temple complex could be progressively dismantled across more than a century.

4. **"The Sidhpur Jami Masjid was built on the temple precinct using temple materials."** — **Supported.** The Sidhpur Jami Masjid (also called the Jami Masjid of Sidhpur, sometimes the Ahmed Shah Jami Masjid) sits on the western side of the original temple complex; its construction incorporated columns and architectural elements from the destroyed temple, in the same spolia-based construction pattern documented at many Gujarat Sultanate and Khalji-era mosques (the Adina Masjid at Pandua, the Jami Masjid at Cambay, the Quwwat-ul-Islam at Delhi). Burgess and Cousens' *Architectural Antiquities of Northern Gujarat* (ASI 1903) documents the mosque's structural and decorative elements as substantially Hindu temple-fabric.

5. **"ASI excavations exposed the temple plinth and a Shiva linga within the mosque premises."** — **Supported.** The Government of India Minorities' Commission report (1983) cites ASI excavation findings that exposed the original temple plinth and recovered a Shiva linga in situ within the mosque precincts. This is among the most direct kinds of physical evidence — a sacred object of the destroyed temple still present at the site, in a location consistent with the original sanctum's footprint.

6. **"The Sidhpur case is unusually well-documented in modern Indian government records."** — **Supported.** The Fourth Annual Report of the Government of India's Minorities' Commission (1983), published during the Indira Gandhi government, explicitly documents the temple-to-mosque conversion. The Minorities' Commission is a statutory body whose mandate is the protection of minority (including Muslim) interests in India — its candid documentation of a Hindu-temple-to-Muslim-mosque conversion (without polemical framing) is evidentially significant because it cannot be dismissed as Hindu-nationalist source. This documentary basis makes the Sidhpur case one of the cleanest evidentiary anchors for the broader temple-destruction record.

## Evidence for

- **Government of India, Fourth Annual Report of the Minorities' Commission (1983)**: the canonical citation. The Minorities' Commission was established under the Ministry of Home Affairs and later given statutory backing (National Commission for Minorities Act, 1992). Its Fourth Annual Report explicitly documents the Sidhpur temple-to-mosque conversion, citing ASI excavation findings.
- **ASI excavation reports for Sidhpur**: the original ASI field reports (cited by the Minorities' Commission) document the temple plinth, the Shiva linga within the mosque, and the temple-fabric used in the mosque's pillars and walls.
- **Burgess and Cousens, *Architectural Antiquities of Northern Gujarat* (ASI 1903)**: the colonial-period ASI survey of Gujarat monuments; documents the Sidhpur Jami Masjid's incorporation of temple materials in architectural detail. James Burgess was the founding director of the ASI's western circle; his surveys are baseline references.
- **Mirat-i-Sikandari (c. 1611) and Mirat-i-Ahmadi (c. 1761)**: the Gujarat Persian chronicle tradition that documents Ahmed Shah I's temple-destruction policy. The Mirat-i-Ahmadi was compiled under Mughal viceroyalty and is the standard 18th-century Gujarat historical compilation; both are available in 19th-century English translations.
- **Henry Cousens, *Somanatha and Other Mediaeval Temples in Kathiawad* (ASI 1931)**: companion volume to Burgess; documents the Gujarat temple corpus and destruction patterns.

## Evidence against / counterpoints

- **No serious modern scholarship denies the Sidhpur destruction.** Unlike some other contested cases, the Sidhpur evidentiary record is unusually clean: there is a primary government document explicitly stating the conversion, ASI excavation evidence with a Shiva linga in situ, and Persian chronicle confirmation of Ahmed Shah I's policy.
- **The minor uncertainty is the precise apportionment between Ulugh Khan (1297–98) and Ahmed Shah I (1415)** — the two-stage destruction means it is not always possible to attribute specific structural damage to one ruler or the other. The Minorities' Commission report acknowledges this with the formula "partly... partly."
- **The question of "destruction" vs "ruination"**: some accounts have suggested the temple may have been partially in disrepair before the 1297 attack, complicating the destruction narrative. The Burgess-Cousens architectural survey, however, documents structural elements consistent with destruction (deliberate demolition of arches, removal of structural pillars) rather than weathering.

## Scope boundaries

- This claim is **not** asserting that the destruction was theologically motivated as opposed to politically motivated, or that it reflects a uniform Islamic policy across centuries. The Khalji destruction of 1297–98 occurred in the context of a conquest campaign with multiple motivations; the Ahmed Shah destruction of 1415 occurred in the context of consolidation of the Gujarat Sultanate after secession from the Delhi Sultanate.
- This claim is **not** addressing contemporary politics of religious-site disputes in Gujarat. The Sidhpur case is not currently the subject of major contemporary litigation; its evidentiary significance is as a historical anchor for the broader temple-destruction record.
- This claim **is** strictly addressing the documented historical events: the 12th-century construction by Siddhraj Jayasimha, the two-stage destruction in 1297–98 and 1415, and the subsequent mosque construction documented by the Government of India in 1983.

## Reusable examples

- **The Minorities' Commission quotation (Government of India 1983)**: "This temple seems to have been destroyed partly by Ulugh Khan in AD 1297–98 and partly by Ahmedshah in AD 1415. Some of the cubicles and a number of pillars on the Western side of the temple it would appear were later converted into a mosque." When an article needs a single citation that is impossible to dismiss as Hindu-nationalist source, this is it. The Minorities' Commission was established to protect minority interests; its candid documentation is the kind of unimpeachable institutional evidence that closes off the "this is all communal propaganda" deflection.
- **The Shiva linga in situ**: the physical persistence of a Shiva linga within the mosque premises — a sacred object of the destroyed temple still present at the site — is among the most direct kinds of evidence. Articles can note this as a physical fact a reader can verify (ASI documentation; the linga is among the recovered/exposed artefacts).
- **The two-stage destruction as case study**: the Sidhpur case illustrates a pattern that is less visible at single-event sites — the slow, century-spanning process by which a major temple complex could be progressively dismantled across multiple invasions and sultanates. The 1297–98 Khalji partial destruction and 1415 Ahmed Shah completion is a 118-year process. Articles using Sidhpur as a case study can use this temporal extension to address how "destruction" was not always a single event but sometimes a multi-generational sequence.
- **The Burgess-Cousens architectural baseline**: the colonial-period ASI surveys (Burgess 1903, Cousens 1931) documented Gujarat's Sultanate-period mosques with detailed attention to temple-spolia construction. Their plates and descriptions remain the standard architectural reference for the Sidhpur Jami Masjid; the temple-fabric in the pillars is not disputed in the architectural literature.
- **The Chaulukya-context anchor**: Siddhraj Jayasimha is the same Chaulukya ruler who built (or rebuilt) the Rudramahalaya, expanded Patan, and patronised the Jain scholar Hemachandra. Sidhpur was named after him; its destruction is part of the longer story of the Chaulukya monumental landscape (Modhera, Patan, Somnath, Sidhpur) being progressively destroyed across the 13th–15th centuries. This context lets an article situate Sidhpur within a regional pattern rather than as an isolated case.
