## Plain-language statement

In January 1026 CE, Mahmud of Ghazni led a Ghaznavid army across the Thar desert into Saurashtra (modern Gujarat), assaulted the great Shiva temple at Somnath on the Arabian Sea coast, killed a large number of defenders, broke the central idol, and carried its fragments and the temple's treasure back to Ghazni. The destruction is attested in multiple independent Muslim chronicles, including Mahmud's own court historian Utbi. Around this well-documented event has grown a cluster of secondary claims — "seventeen raids," "rebuilt seventeen times," astronomical loot figures, and theological framings — that need to be separated from the core historical fact and assessed individually.

## Decomposed sub-claims

1. **"Mahmud of Ghazni's army destroyed the Somnath temple in January 1026 CE."** — **Supported.** The event is reported by Utbi (Mahmud's contemporary court historian), al-Biruni (resident in India during Mahmud's reign), Ibn al-Athir, Minhaj-us-Siraj, and later Persian chroniclers. The independent corroboration from al-Biruni — who was a scientist, not a propagandist — is the strongest single evidentiary anchor.

2. **"Mahmud led seventeen campaigns into the Indian subcontinent."** — **Supported as a framing of his career, but only one of those campaigns was Somnath.** The "seventeen raids" count is historically defensible (the figure derives from Persian chronicle traditions and is broadly accepted), but it should not be elided with "seventeen destructions of Somnath" — a confusion that circulates in popular sources. Somnath was a single campaign within the seventeen.

3. **"Somnath has been rebuilt seventeen times."** — **A composite trope, not a documented sequence.** The temple was reconstructed multiple times across centuries (notably by Bhima I and Kumarapala of the Chaulukyas in the 11th–12th c., by Mughal-era patrons, and finally by the Government of India in 1951 under Sardar Patel's initiative). But there is no inventoried "seventeen rebuilds" list; the figure is a rhetorical echo of "seventeen raids" and should not be presented as a documented count.

4. **"Mahmud's loot from Somnath was the equivalent of twenty million dinars (or comparable figures in modern conversion)."** — **Likely exaggerated.** Utbi was Mahmud's court historian, paid to glorify his patron, and the chronicle inflates numbers throughout (army sizes, idol heights, treasure weights). The destruction and looting of substantial temple treasure are well-attested; the specific figures are court-historian rhetoric and should be cited with a "as reported by the court chronicle" qualifier, not as audited accounting.

5. **"Mahmud's motivation was primarily religious iconoclasm."** — **Contested in modern scholarship.** Utbi and later Muslim chronicles foreground the religious framing — Mahmud as "but-shikan" (idol-breaker), as "the right hand of the Caliph." Romila Thapar's *Somanatha: The Many Voices of a History* (Penguin 2004) is the standard modern counter-framing, arguing that the religious motive was real but interwoven with political-economic motives (treasure capture, military prestige, dynastic legitimation through orthodoxy). Mohammad Habib's earlier *Sultan Mahmud of Ghaznin* (1924) similarly argued that wealth, not theology, was the primary driver. The honest summary: religious iconoclasm provided the *justification* and shaped the *method*; treasure and prestige drove the *economics*. Both were operative; the relative weight is a live scholarly debate.

6. **"The central Shivalinga at Somnath was broken into pieces and the fragments distributed to mosques and royal sites."** — **Supported.** Minhaj-us-Siraj records that the idol "was broken into four parts, one of which was cast before the centre of the great masjid at Ghaznin, the second before the gateway of the Sultan's palace, and the third and fourth were sent to Makkah and Madinah respectively." This deliberate distribution of fragments as humiliation tokens is a documented pattern in Mahmud's campaigns and is not a later embellishment.

7. **"The Somnath idol was a representation of the pre-Islamic Arabian goddess Manat brought to India."** — **Not supported; this is a polemical Persian-chronicle conceit.** Several Muslim chroniclers (notably Minhaj-us-Siraj quoted above) claim the Somnath idol was "Manat" — that pre-Islamic Arabs had transported their idol to India. This is a theological flourish, not history. The Somnath Shivalinga belonged to an unbroken Shaiva tradition; the Manat identification serves Persian-chronicle rhetoric (linking Mahmud's victory to the Prophet's destruction of pre-Islamic Arabian idols). Modern scholarship treats it as an interpretive overlay.

## Evidence for

- **Utbi, *Tarikh-i-Yamini***: Mahmud's own court chronicler, writing within a decade of the event. Describes the campaign, the assault, the idol's destruction, and the treasure carried back. The Reynolds translation (1858) is the standard English version; the relevant chapters are summarised in Elliot & Dowson vol. 2.
- **al-Biruni, *Kitab-ul-Hind***: a near-contemporary scientific work composed by a scholar resident in India during Mahmud's later reign. al-Biruni was not a polemicist; his confirmation of the Somnath events is the strongest independent corroboration. He also describes the temple itself with ethnographic detail.
- **Minhaj-us-Siraj, *Tabaqat-i-Nasiri* (c. 1260)**: the standard 13th-century compilation; records the idol-fragments distribution. Reverty's English translation (1881) is the citable form.
- **Ibn al-Athir, *al-Kamil fi-t-Tarikh***: 13th-century Arabic universal history; reproduces and confirms the chronology.
- **Elliot & Dowson, *The History of India as Told by Its Own Historians*, vol. 2 (1869)**: the standard English-language source for all the above. Use as the citable compilation when working from English.
- **Numismatic evidence**: silver coins struck at Lahore in Mahmud's seventh regnal year describe him in Arabic legend as "the right hand of the Caliph" and "the breaker of idols" (but-shikan). The title was self-applied and contemporaneous, not a later projection.

## Evidence against / counterpoints

- **No serious modern scholarship denies the destruction event itself.** The disagreement is about motivation, scale of loot, and how the event has been mythologised in later memory.
- **Romila Thapar, *Somanatha: The Many Voices of a History* (Penguin 2004)** — the standard secular re-reading. Thapar's key contribution is to distinguish (a) what the Persian chronicles say (a religious iconoclastic raid against an Arabian-idol-projection); (b) what Jaina merchant records, Sanskrit inscriptions, and local Shaiva tradition say (a more complex picture in which Somnath continued to function as a major pilgrimage centre, Muslim merchants worshipped at it, and the destruction's communal salience emerged largely in later British-colonial and post-Independence framings). Thapar does not deny the 1026 destruction; she argues its place in modern Hindu-Muslim memory is shaped by 19th- and 20th-century politics more than by medieval reality.
- **Mohammad Habib, *Sultan Mahmud of Ghaznin* (1924)**: the influential early-Indian-Muslim scholarly position that Mahmud's motivation was primarily plunder, not theology — that he raided rich temples because temples held the era's accumulated wealth, and used religious framing as justification.
- **Loot figures**: Utbi's specific numbers (e.g., "twenty thousand idols," gold weights in maunds) should not be cited as audited; they are court-chronicler rhetoric.
- **Continuity of pilgrimage**: post-1026 inscriptions and Chaulukyan rebuilding records show Somnath functioned as a pilgrimage site through the medieval period, complicating the "annihilated and forgotten" narrative.

## Scope boundaries

- This claim is **not** denying that Mahmud destroyed Somnath. The destruction event is one of the most evidentially secure single facts of 11th-century Indian history.
- This claim is **not** endorsing the inflated framings ("seventeen times rebuilt," astronomical loot figures, Mahmud as a uniquely religious villain compared to other medieval invaders) that accumulated around the event in later popular memory.
- This claim is **not** taking a position on whether Mahmud's motivation was "really" religious or "really" economic. The historical evidence supports both, in proportions modern scholars debate.
- This claim **is** insisting that the destruction be cited from the Muslim chroniclers themselves (Utbi, al-Biruni, Minhaj-us-Siraj, Ibn al-Athir) and not from later Hindutva or Hindu-nationalist secondary literature. The case is strong enough that it does not need rhetorical inflation; it needs careful sourcing.
- This claim **is** distinguishing the secular historical question (what happened in January 1026?) from the cultural-memory question (how has the event been remembered, weaponised, and re-narrated since?). Both are legitimate questions; they are not the same question.

## Reusable examples

- **The fragments distribution**: Minhaj-us-Siraj records that the Somnath idol "was broken into four parts, one of which was cast before the centre of the great masjid at Ghaznin, the second before the gateway of the Sultan's palace, and the third and fourth were sent to Makkah and Madinah respectively." This is the kind of detail that an article can quote directly; it captures the deliberate humiliation logic of the campaign more clearly than aggregate framings do.
- **The numismatic anchor**: Mahmud's Lahore silver coins of his seventh regnal year carry the Arabic title "but-shikan" (idol-breaker) — a self-applied contemporaneous title, not a later attribution. This is the kind of evidence (a coin you can hold) that resists "the chronicles exaggerate" deflections.
- **The 1951 rebuilding**: Sardar Patel's initiative to reconstruct Somnath as the first major act of post-Independence cultural restoration is itself a primary-source moment in modern Indian history (Patel's correspondence with K. M. Munshi; the inauguration speeches; the Government of India's role despite Nehru's reservations). The 1951 rebuild is the sixth or seventh major reconstruction depending on how one counts the Chaulukyan rebuilds.
- **Thapar on the "many voices"**: a useful contrast for any article that wants to handle the topic with depth — quote Utbi's framing, then quote a Jaina merchant record or a Chaulukyan inscription, then note that both are part of the historical record and the modern memory is shaped by which voices are foregrounded.

## When this claim matters

- When a reader lands on "Mahmud of Ghazni Somnath," "Somnath temple destruction," "seventeen raids on India," "Somnath rebuilt," or "Mahmud of Ghazni idol breaker."
- When writing about Jyotirlinga temples, medieval temple destruction, Saurashtra history, or the medieval Islamic invasions of the subcontinent.
- When the temptation is either to inflate (the polemical "seventeen times destroyed and rebuilt" framing) or to deflect (the deflection that questions whether destruction happened at all). The historical record supports the destruction event clearly; the discipline is in not adding to it.
- When the relevant downstream question is about temple architecture survival patterns (why so much of the surviving temple corpus is in South India and the deeper Deccan).
