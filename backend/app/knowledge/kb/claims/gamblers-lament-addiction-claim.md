## Plain-language statement

Rigveda 10.34, known as the **Aksha Sukta** or **Gambler's Lament**, is a 14-verse hymn voiced in the first person by a compulsive gambler. It describes his ambivalence, compulsion, shame, social and economic ruin, and concludes with an exhortation to give up gambling and return to honest work. The hymn is textually real, well-attested, and extraordinary as an early first-person account of compulsive behaviour. Calling it "the earliest portrait of addiction psychology" is defensible if scoped narrowly — it is among the earliest surviving first-person portraits of addictive behaviour in any literary tradition. Calling it "proto-CBT" or "Vedic addiction treatment" is over-reading.

## Decomposed sub-claims

1. **"Rigveda 10.34 is a real hymn in the Rigveda that describes gambling addiction in the first person."** — **Supported.** The hymn exists in the standard Rigveda Samhita, is 14 verses, is voiced by the gambler, and is translated in every modern critical edition.
2. **"The hymn describes the phenomenology of addictive behaviour with psychological accuracy."** — **Supported.** The hymn captures ambivalence, resolve-and-relapse ("when I resolve 'I will not play with them,' my friends depart and leave me; but when the brown ones rattled have raised their voice, I go to their rendezvous like a woman to her lover"), social consequences (wife abandoned, family alienated), economic consequences (debt, wandering at night seeking money), and shame — all of which are recognizably phenomena of addiction as modern psychology describes it.
3. **"The hymn is among the earliest surviving first-person portraits of addictive behaviour in world literature."** — **Supported with scoping.** Among preserved literature, the hymn's first-person voice on compulsive behaviour has few peers of comparable age. Exact "earliest" claims depend on the Rigveda's dating (typically ~1500–1200 BCE for the oldest strata; Mandala 10 is later but still pre-classical). Careful claims say "among the earliest" rather than "the earliest."
4. **"The hymn contains proto-cognitive-behavioural therapy."** — **Not supported.** The hymn is *descriptive* of addiction phenomenology. Its "cure" is the moral exhortation in the final verse ("play not with dice; plough thy field") — a call to abandon the behaviour, not a structured therapeutic protocol. CBT is a specific 20th-century clinical framework, and claiming the Rigveda contains it is an anachronism.
5. **"The hymn proves that gambling addiction was widespread in Vedic society."** — **Not supported.** The hymn is a first-person portrait, not a sociological survey. Generalizing from one hymn to the prevalence of gambling in Vedic-period society is over-reading.
6. **"The hymn is better understood as a metaphor for life's uncertainties than as literal addiction."** — **Partially supported, but the literal reading is primary.** Some later interpretive traditions extend the gambling metaphor to broader life uncertainty, but the hymn itself is very concrete about dice, debt, and family collapse. The literal reading is the primary textual meaning; the metaphorical reading is a secondary interpretive move.

## Evidence for

- **Primary**:
  - Rigveda 10.34 — the 14-verse hymn itself, in the standard Samhita text
  - Particularly vivid verses: 2 (the wife rejects him), 3 (the family alienation), 5 (resolve-and-relapse), 10 (he wanders at night seeking money), 13–14 (the closing exhortation to plough the field)
- **Modern scholarship**:
  - Jamison & Brereton (2014) — standard modern critical English translation with philological notes
  - Doniger (1981) — the most widely cited accessible English rendering in her Penguin anthology
  - C. K. Raju's working translation and commentary (ckraju.net) — a more recent exposition with psychological framing
  - Modern psychological readings treat the hymn as an early portrait of addiction's phenomenology; these readings are a reasonable literary-critical use of the text, not a forced contemporary imposition

## Evidence against / counterpoints

- The dating of Mandala 10 is later than the core mandalas (2–7), so "earliest Vedic hymn on addiction" is narrower than "earliest Rigvedic hymn overall." Mandala 10 is generally dated ~1000 BCE or later.
- "Earliest in world literature" depends on what counts — Mesopotamian and Egyptian material contains some earlier references to drunkenness and compulsive behaviour, but usually not in the sustained first-person form of the Aksha Sukta. The narrow claim ("earliest surviving sustained first-person portrait of addictive behaviour in a preserved literary text") is more defensible than a blanket "earliest."
- Popular retellings sometimes attribute specific psychological-therapeutic techniques to the hymn that are not in the text. The text describes the *condition*; it prescribes a single moral solution (quit), not a program of graduated cognitive interventions.

## Scope boundaries

- This claim **is** saying: Rigveda 10.34 exists, describes a compulsive gambler in the first person, captures recognizable phenomenology of addiction, and is an early and psychologically vivid record of addictive behaviour in preserved literature.
- This claim **is not** saying: Vedic India had a clinical theory of addiction, or CBT, or a treatment protocol.
- This claim **is not** saying: the Vedic period had especially severe or prevalent gambling problems (we have one hymn, not social data).
- This claim **is not** saying: the hymn is the earliest literary portrait of any addictive behaviour in any tradition — only that it is among the earliest surviving sustained first-person portraits.

## Reusable examples

- **The resolve-and-relapse verse (RV 10.34.5–6, in Jamison & Brereton's translation)**: the gambler resolves not to play, his friends abandon him in disgust, but as soon as he hears the rattle of dice, "I go to their rendezvous like a woman to her lover." This is one of the most striking lines in the Rigveda and is the single verse most often cited in modern psychological readings.
- **The abandoned wife (RV 10.34.2–4)**: the gambler's wife rejects him, his mother-in-law hates him, relatives feign not to recognize him when he asks for help. The hymn is unsparing about the social cost.
- **The closing exhortation (RV 10.34.13–14)**: "play not with dice; plough thy field; rejoice in thy portion; there are thy cattle, there thy wife, O gambler." The hymn ends in direct second-person address, turning the gambler's lament into a warning to the listener.

## When this claim matters

- When writing on addiction, the history of psychology, or the question "what did ancient civilizations know about mental health?"
- When discussing the Rigveda as literature — not just as liturgy — and specifically the thematic range of Mandala 10
- When responding to the popular "Vedic psychology was millennia ahead of modern therapy" framing, which is usually an over-read of exactly this hymn; the responsible response is to affirm the psychological vividness of the portrait while scoping the claim away from anachronistic treatment-protocol language
- When pointing out that the Vedic corpus contains sustained social and ethical observation, not just ritual and metaphysics
