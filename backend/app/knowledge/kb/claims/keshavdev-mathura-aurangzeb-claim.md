## Plain-language statement

In January 1670 (Ramadan 1080 AH), Aurangzeb issued a firman ordering the demolition of the Keshavdev (also called Kesho Rai) temple at Mathura — the great Vaishnava shrine marking the traditional site of Krishna's birthplace. The order was executed; the Shahi Eidgah Mosque was constructed on the temple's footprint at a "large sum"; the temple's jewelled idols were transported to Agra and buried under the steps of the Jami Masjid of the Begum Sahib (Jahanara), so they would be "continually trodden upon"; and the name of Mathura was changed to Islamabad. All four elements — destruction, mosque construction, idol-humiliation rite at Agra, and city renaming — are recorded in Aurangzeb's own court chronicle, the *Maasir-i-Alamgiri*.

## Decomposed sub-claims

1. **"Aurangzeb issued a January 1670 firman to demolish the Keshavdev temple at Mathura."** — **Supported.** The Maasir-i-Alamgiri records the order under the month of Ramadan 1080 AH (corresponding to January–February 1670). This is the same firman-pattern as the 1669 Kashi Vishwanath demolition: a specific imperial order to destroy a named temple, executed by named officers, with the outcome reported back to the court.

2. **"The Keshavdev temple was demolished and the Shahi Eidgah Mosque built on the same footprint."** — **Supported.** The Maasir-i-Alamgiri records: "In a short time by the great exertions of his officers the destruction of this strong foundation of infidelity was accomplished, and on its site a lofty mosque was built at the expenditure of a large sum." The Shahi Eidgah now occupies that site, directly adjacent to the modern Krishna Janmasthan complex; the shared wall between the modern temple and the Eidgah is the contemporary outcome of that 1670 construction.

3. **"The temple's jewelled idols were buried under the steps of the Begum Sahib mosque at Agra to be trodden upon."** — **Supported.** The Maasir-i-Alamgiri records: "The idols, large and small, set with costly jewels which had been set up in the temple were brought to Agra, and buried under the steps of the mosque of the Begam Sahib, in order to be continually trodden upon." This deliberate ritual humiliation of idols — burying them under mosque steps so the faithful would walk over them — is a recurring pattern in Aurangzeb's court record (also documented at Jodhpur 1679, Khandela 1679). The Begum Sahib mosque was the mosque of Jahanara Begum, Shah Jahan's elder daughter and Aurangzeb's sister.

4. **"Mathura was renamed to Islamabad."** — **Supported.** The Maasir-i-Alamgiri records the renaming explicitly: "The name of Mathura was changed to Islamabad." Official Mughal documents and coinage from the period reflect the renaming. The name did not persist in popular usage and Mathura reverted to its original name after the Mughal period.

5. **"The pre-1670 Keshavdev temple was a major Vaishnava shrine of historic significance."** — **Supported.** The temple Aurangzeb destroyed had been built (or substantially rebuilt) by Bir Singh Deo Bundela of Orchha in the early 17th century at a cost of 33 lakh rupees, on a site of traditional Vaishnava pilgrimage going back at least to the Gupta period. Bir Singh built the temple under Jahangir's tolerance (he was a Mughal ally); Aurangzeb destroyed his ancestor's permitted construction. Earlier temples on or near the site had been destroyed by Mahmud of Ghazni (1018) and Sikandar Lodi (c. 1500), so the Bir Singh-Aurangzeb sequence is one episode in a longer cycle of destruction and reconstruction at Mathura.

6. **"The Mathura destruction reflects Aurangzeb's specifically religious motivation rather than political-economic motivation."** — **Partially supported, with nuance.** The Maasir-i-Alamgiri's framing is explicitly religious — the temple is called "this strong foundation of infidelity," the idols are humiliated by burial under mosque steps, and the city is renamed. This is one of the cases where the religious-iconoclastic framing in the court record is most explicit. At the same time, Mathura had also been the centre of a recent Jat rebellion (1669) which Aurangzeb had suppressed; the destruction can be read as combining religious motive with political punishment of a region in revolt. The honest summary: religious motivation is on the surface of the court record; political-punitive motivation is plausibly an additional driver. Audrey Truschke's *Aurangzeb* (2017) develops the political-punishment reading without denying the religious framing.

## Evidence for

- **Maasir-i-Alamgiri (Saqi Mustaid Khan, completed 1710)**: the imperial chronicle records the Mathura destruction with unusual detail — the firman, the execution, the idol-humiliation rite at Agra, and the city renaming. Jadunath Sarkar's 1947 translation (Royal Asiatic Society of Bengal) is the standard English text; the relevant pages cover the events of Ramadan 1080 AH / January 1670. Earlier excerpts in Elliot & Dowson vol. 7 (1877).
- **Jadunath Sarkar, *History of Aurangzib* vol. 3 (1916)**: the definitive scholarly biography devotes sustained attention to the Mathura campaign and the Jat rebellion context. Sarkar treats the destruction as a documentary fact and contextualises it within the broader temple-destruction policy.
- **Maulana Abdul Hai, *Hindustan Islami Ahad Mein* (Nadwa 1973)**: lists the Aurangzeb mosque at Mathura among the seven mosques built on temple sites (along with Quwwatul Islam Delhi, Qanauj Jami Masjid, Etawah Jami Masjid, Babri Masjid Ayodhya, Gyanvapi Benares, and a second Benares mosque). Independent Muslim-scholarly confirmation.
- **Physical evidence**: the Shahi Eidgah at Mathura is constructed in such a way that it shares a wall with the modern Krishna Janmasthan temple complex; the spatial relationship is direct and observable. Architectural elements consistent with temple-spolia construction have been documented in the Eidgah's structure.

## Evidence against / counterpoints

- **No serious modern scholarship denies the 1670 demolition itself.** Like the Kashi case, the disagreement is about generalisability and motivation, not the underlying event.
- **The political-context counterpoint**: Mathura was in the middle of a Jat agrarian rebellion (1669–1670) led by Gokula, who was captured and executed in January 1670 — the same month the temple was destroyed. The destruction can be read as combining religious motive with punishment of a rebellious region. Audrey Truschke (*Aurangzeb*, 2017) develops this framing.
- **The Bir Singh / Jahangir history**: the destroyed temple was built under Mughal tolerance by a Mughal ally (Bir Singh Bundela) in the early 17th century. The fact that an earlier Mughal regime permitted the construction complicates "all Mughals were always anti-Hindu" framings — the policy varied between rulers. This does not soften the 1670 destruction; it sharpens the contrast.
- **The renaming did not stick**: "Islamabad" as a name for Mathura did not persist in usage; Mathura reverted to its original Sanskritic name almost immediately after the Mughal period. The renaming was an act of state, not an act of folk culture.

## Scope boundaries

- This claim is **not** asserting that Aurangzeb's anti-temple policy was uniform or applied equally across all of his territories. The 1669 Kashi and 1670 Mathura cases are particularly explicit; other cases are more ambiguous; many temples received Mughal patronage during Aurangzeb's reign.
- This claim **is** asserting that the Mathura destruction is one of the most clearly documented cases in the Mughal record, with the destruction event, the mosque construction, the idol-humiliation rite, and the city renaming all attested in the imperial chronicle itself.
- This claim is **not** taking a position on the contemporary Krishna Janmasthan-Shahi Eidgah litigation, which is a separate legal and political question.
- This claim is **not** denying that the Mathura case has political dimensions in addition to religious dimensions. The Jat rebellion context is real and relevant. But the religious framing in the court record is also real and is not erased by the political context.

## Reusable examples

- **The Maasir-i-Alamgiri's Mathura entry (the four-element record)**: "During this month of Ramzan abounding in miracles, the Emperor, as the promoter of justice and overthrower of mischief, as a knower of truth and destroyer of oppression, as the zephyr of the garden of victory and the reviver of the faith of the Prophet, issued orders for the demolition of the temple situated in Mathura, famous as the Dehra of Kesho Rai. In a short time by the great exertions of his officers the destruction of this strong foundation of infidelity was accomplished, and on its site a lofty mosque was built at the expenditure of a large sum... The idols, large and small, set with costly jewels which had been set up in the temple were brought to Agra, and buried under the steps of the mosque of the Begam Sahib, in order to be continually trodden upon. The name of Mathura was changed to Islamabad." — Maasir-i-Alamgiri, Sarkar trans. This is one of the most complete single-event records in the Mughal chronicle tradition; the four elements (destruction, mosque-construction, idol-humiliation, city-renaming) together capture the deliberate symbolic logic of the campaign.
- **The idol-burial rite as recurring pattern**: the Mathura idols sent to Agra in 1670; the Jodhpur idols cast under the Jami mosque steps in 1679; the Khandela idol-fragments treated similarly in 1679. The pattern of treating idol-fragments as objects to be deliberately profaned by Muslim foot-traffic is a recurring Mughal record, not an isolated rhetorical flourish.
- **The Bir Singh / Aurangzeb contrast**: a useful framing for any article that wants to handle the Mathura case with depth. Jahangir's regime permitted Bir Singh Bundela to build a great Vaishnava temple at Mathura at 33 lakh rupees; Aurangzeb's regime destroyed it sixty years later. The contrast tells the reader that "the Mughals" is a generalisation that obscures real policy variation between rulers — and that the Mathura destruction is what changed, not Hindu temple-building.
- **The Jat rebellion context**: Mathura was at the centre of Gokula's Jat uprising, which Aurangzeb suppressed in January 1670. The temple destruction in the same month is part of the suppression of the rebellious region. This is a real political dimension that does not erase the religious framing in the court record but contextualises it.
