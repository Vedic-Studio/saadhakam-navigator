## Plain-language statement

"India invented zero" is a true-ish statement that hides several distinct developments — each of which is interesting on its own and each of which has its own date. The responsible picture is that **the modern zero has three components** — place-value notation, zero as a placeholder in that notation, and zero as a number with its own arithmetic — and all three come together in classical Indian mathematics between roughly the 3rd and 7th centuries CE. The Babylonians had a placeholder-like notation much earlier; the Greeks had arithmetic but not a full place-value system; the Maya developed a separate zero in Mesoamerica. What Indian mathematics uniquely contributed is the **combination**: the decimal place-value system with zero fully integrated as both placeholder and number, systematized with explicit arithmetic rules in Brahmagupta's Brahmasphutasiddhanta (628 CE), and transmitted through Arabic mathematics to the rest of the world.

## Decomposed sub-claims

1. **"India had decimal place-value notation."** — **Supported.** Classical Indian mathematics uses a decimal place-value system (base 10, positional) by the middle of the first millennium CE at latest. The Aryabhatiya (499 CE) assumes place-value throughout. Earlier textual evidence (inscriptions, the Bakhshali manuscript) suggests the system was developing over several preceding centuries.

2. **"India used zero as a placeholder in place-value notation."** — **Supported.** A symbol for zero as placeholder is attested in Indian mathematics at least by the time of the Aryabhatiya (499 CE) and likely earlier. The 2017 Oxford carbon-dating of (some folios of) the **Bakhshali manuscript** to the 3rd–4th c. CE has been interpreted as pushing this attestation back further, though the dating is contested (see Plofker et al. 2017 response arguing that different folios date to different periods and the folios containing the zero symbol may be later).

3. **"Brahmagupta (628 CE) was the first to systematize the arithmetic of zero and negative numbers as numbers."** — **Supported as the earliest surviving systematic treatment.** Brahmasphutasiddhanta Chapter 18 contains explicit rules for adding, subtracting, and multiplying with zero and negatives. These are the earliest surviving systematic rules of their kind. Brahmagupta's rule for division by zero is incorrect (he writes 0/0 = 0), which is itself historically interesting.

4. **"India was the only culture to develop a placeholder zero."** — **Not supported.** The Babylonians used a placeholder symbol (a pair of wedges or a blank space) in their base-60 system at least by ~300 BCE. The Maya developed a zero in Mesoamerica independently. What is distinctive about India is the combination of decimal place-value, zero as placeholder, and zero as a number with arithmetic — all in one system.

5. **"Zero comes from nothing (philosophical/Buddhist influence)."** — **Suggestive but unprovable.** The philosophical argument that Indian comfort with shunya (emptiness) in Buddhist metaphysics made it easier for Indian mathematicians to treat zero as a number is often repeated but is hard to substantiate from primary sources. The concept is a favored speculation among some historians of mathematics; others treat it skeptically. Best treated as an interesting hypothesis, not an established fact.

6. **"Indian zero was transmitted to Europe via the Arabs."** — **Supported.** This is one of the clearest transmission stories in the history of mathematics. Brahmagupta's Brahmasphutasiddhanta was translated into Arabic in the late 8th c. CE at the court of al-Mansur in Baghdad. The Hindu-Arabic numerals (including zero) entered European mathematics primarily through 12th-c. translations of Arabic mathematicians (especially al-Khwarizmi) and then Fibonacci's Liber Abaci (1202 CE).

7. **"India 'invented' zero in a single moment."** — **Not supported.** The development was gradual over several centuries and involved multiple distinct innovations (place-value, placeholder symbol, zero as a number, the arithmetic of zero). Treating it as a single invention is a simplification.

## Evidence for

- **Primary**: Brahmasphutasiddhanta Chapter 18 (628 CE) — Brahmagupta's explicit arithmetic rules for zero and negatives
- **Primary**: Aryabhatiya (499 CE) — assumes place-value throughout; uses alphabetic-numerical encoding that presupposes positional notation
- **Primary**: Bakhshali manuscript — contains dot-form zero symbols; Oxford's 2017 carbon-dating suggests some folios may be as early as 3rd–4th c. CE
- **Epigraphic**: various early Indian inscriptions (e.g., the Gwalior 876 CE inscription with a clear numerical zero; earlier inscriptions with developing notation)
- **Transmission**: 8th-c. Arabic translation of Brahmagupta's work (the *Sindhind*) at al-Mansur's court
- **Modern scholarship**: Plofker's Mathematics in India is the current standard account; Kaplan's The Nothing That Is is the best accessible history

## Evidence against / counterpoints

- Babylonian mathematics used a placeholder-like symbol in base-60 positional notation by ~300 BCE, independent of India
- Greek mathematics had sophisticated arithmetic and used a zero-like symbol (omicron) in astronomical tables but did not integrate it into a full place-value system
- Maya mathematics developed a zero in Mesoamerica independently, in a base-20 system
- The Bakhshali manuscript carbon dating is contested; the early date (3rd–4th c.) applies to some folios and may not apply to the specific folios containing the zero symbol
- The Indian zero's transmission story is historically clear, but popular accounts sometimes compress this into "India invented zero and gave it to the world," which erases the Babylonian prior and the Maya parallel

## Scope boundaries

- This claim is **not** denying that classical Indian mathematics made a landmark contribution to the history of zero. It clearly did.
- This claim **is** pushing back against "India uniquely invented zero out of nothing" framings. The story is more textured and more interesting.
- The responsible framing: **the decimal place-value system with zero as both placeholder and number was developed and systematized in classical Indian mathematics between roughly the 3rd and 7th centuries CE, transmitted to the Islamic world and then to Europe, and is the direct ancestor of the modern system we all use now.** This framing honors the real contribution without overclaiming.

## Reusable examples

- **Brahmagupta's explicit rules (628 CE)**: "Zero subtracted from a positive or negative is the positive or negative itself. Zero added to zero is zero. The product of a positive and a negative is negative; of two negatives or two positives, positive. The product of zero and a positive or negative, or of zero and zero, is zero." These rules are in Chapter 18 of the Brahmasphutasiddhanta and can be cited directly.
- **The Bakhshali manuscript**: an unusual early mathematical manuscript (found in 1881 in what is now Pakistan) that uses a dot-form zero symbol. The 2017 Oxford carbon dating gives a range including 3rd–4th c. CE for some folios, which would make it the earliest known use of the zero symbol in any mathematical text. The dating is important but also contested.
- **The transmission route**: Brahmagupta's work translated into Arabic (~770s CE) → al-Khwarizmi's arithmetic (~820 CE) → Latin translations (~1140 CE) → Fibonacci's Liber Abaci (1202 CE) → European commercial adoption over the next 300 years. This is one of the best-documented transmission stories in the global history of mathematics.
- **Zero is a combination achievement**: the impressive thing is not that anyone thought of "nothing" as a number (the Babylonians and Maya got there too) but that Indian mathematics *unified* place-value, placeholder, and zero-as-number into a single working arithmetic system with explicit rules.

## When this claim matters

- When an article mentions "India invented zero," "Aryabhata zero," "Brahmagupta zero," or "who invented zero."
- When writing about the global history of mathematics or the transmission of mathematical ideas.
- When explaining why the Hindu-Arabic numeral system is called "Hindu-Arabic" (because it came from India via the Arabs).
- When the audience is scientifically literate — they respond well to the scoped version because the loose version is easily undermined by the Babylonian counterexample.
