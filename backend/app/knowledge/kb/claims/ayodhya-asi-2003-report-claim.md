## Plain-language statement

In response to a direction from the Lucknow Bench of the Allahabad High Court (order of 5 March 2003), the Archaeological Survey of India conducted an excavation between March and August 2003 at the disputed Babri Masjid site at Ayodhya, with court-appointed observers from all parties (Hindu and Muslim) present throughout. The 574-page report submitted to the court documented a sequence of structural activity beginning in the early historical period and continuing through the medieval period; immediately beneath the Babri Masjid floor it documented a series of pillar bases arrayed in a regular geometric pattern, a north–south wall, structural floors at multiple levels, and recovered architectural fragments including amalaka stones, lotus medallions, and other elements characteristic of temple architecture. The report concluded that the cumulative evidence was consistent with a pre-existing temple-style structure dating to the 12th century, built in turn on earlier strata. The Supreme Court of India's 9 November 2019 judgment evaluated the report at length (§786–§800) and accepted its findings as valid evidence. The report does not claim to prove that the pre-existing structure was specifically a Rama temple; that interpretation rests on additional documentary evidence (the four pre-British Muslim sources, the continuous Hindu pilgrimage tradition, and the European travelers' accounts) discussed in the companion claim on the Babri Masjid temple site question.

## Decomposed sub-claims

1. **"The excavation was conducted under court direction with observers from all parties present."** — **Supported (this is documented legal record).** The Allahabad High Court's Lucknow Bench ordered the excavation on 5 March 2003 in the title-suit proceedings. Observers nominated by the Hindu and Muslim parties were present at the site throughout the excavation. Excavation supervisors (Hari Manjhi and B. R. Mani, ASI Joint Directors-General) submitted affidavits documenting the protocol. This procedural anchor is what distinguishes the 2003 excavation from informal or unilateral archaeological surveys.

2. **"The excavation documented pillar bases arrayed in a regular pattern beneath the Babri Masjid."** — **Supported.** The report identifies approximately 50 pillar bases (the exact count varies by which pre-1528 phase is being counted) in a regular pattern beneath the mosque footprint. The pillar bases are consistent in size, orientation, and spacing — consistent with a pillared temple hall (mandapa) rather than a random pre-existing structure. This finding is detailed in the report's stratigraphic plans (chapter VI of the ASI report).

3. **"The excavation documented a north–south wall in lime mortar beneath the mosque floor."** — **Supported.** A wall of significant length (~50m) running north–south, constructed in lime mortar, was exposed at a level immediately below the Babri Masjid foundation. The wall's construction technique and dating are consistent with the 12th-century stratum identified by the report.

4. **"The excavation recovered architectural fragments characteristic of temple architecture."** — **Supported.** Recovered fragments include amalaka stones (the cushion-shaped finials characteristic of north Indian temple shikharas), lotus medallions, divine and semi-divine figures (apsaras, vyala motifs), and pillar bases with mouldings consistent with temple-architecture. The fragments are catalogued in the report's antiquities section.

5. **"The ASI's overall interpretive conclusion was that a temple-style structure pre-existed beneath the Babri Masjid."** — **Supported as the report's stated conclusion; contested by some scholars at the time of submission.** The report's summary concludes that the cumulative evidence is consistent with a pre-existing temple structure dating to the 12th century, built in turn on earlier strata going back to the early historical period. This conclusion was challenged by some signed parties (the affidavits filed by Suraj Bhan and others) arguing that the structural evidence could support alternative interpretations (Eidgah, residential complex, secular building). The Supreme Court in 2019 evaluated and rejected these alternative interpretations, accepting the ASI majority finding.

6. **"The ASI report proves that the pre-existing structure was specifically a Rama temple."** — **Not what the report claims.** The ASI report concludes that the pre-existing structure was *consistent with a temple* — it does not specify which deity, and does not claim to settle the Rama-Janmasthan question. That interpretation rests on the combination of (a) the ASI finding of a pre-existing temple structure, (b) the four pre-British Muslim sources describing the Babri Masjid as built on the Janmasthan temple, (c) the continuous Hindu pilgrimage tradition identifying the site as Rama Janmasthan, and (d) the structural and architectural features consistent with Vaishnava iconography among the recovered fragments. The Rama-specific interpretation is cumulative; the ASI report is one part of the evidentiary basis, not the whole.

7. **"The 2019 Supreme Court judgment accepted the ASI report's findings."** — **Supported (this is documented legal record).** The Supreme Court's 1045-page judgment of 9 November 2019 evaluated the ASI report at length in paragraphs §786 through §800, addressed the challenges raised by Suraj Bhan and others, and concluded that the ASI's findings were valid evidence of a pre-existing temple structure beneath the mosque. The judgment's evaluation of the ASI report is among the most comprehensive judicial assessments of archaeological evidence in modern Indian jurisprudence.

8. **"The 2003 excavation built on earlier work, including B. B. Lal's excavations in the 1970s."** — **Supported.** B. B. Lal led ASI excavations in the Ayodhya region as part of the Archaeology of the Ramayana Sites project (1975–86); his findings included pillar bases at the disputed site that were among the earliest indications of a pre-existing structure. K. K. Muhammad, a Muslim ASI archaeologist who participated in the 1976–77 phase of these excavations, has publicly confirmed (memoir *Njan Enna Bharatheeyan*, 2018) the presence of pre-Islamic temple-architecture evidence at the site. The 2003 excavation was the comprehensive court-directed follow-up to these earlier indications.

## Evidence for

- **The ASI report itself**: *Ayodhya 2002–03: Excavations at the Disputed Site (Ayodhya)*, 574 pages with stratigraphic plans, antiquities catalogues, and plates. Submitted to the Allahabad High Court in August 2003. The primary document.
- **Court records**: the Allahabad High Court's 30 September 2010 judgment in the original Ayodhya title suit (three judges, three separate opinions, all three accepting the ASI report's finding of a pre-existing structure) and the Supreme Court's 9 November 2019 final judgment.
- **Affidavits of ASI supervisors**: Hari Manjhi and B. R. Mani's affidavits before the Allahabad High Court documenting the excavation protocol, the observer-presence, and the findings.
- **B. B. Lal's pre-2003 excavations** (1975–86) provided the initial indications of pre-existing structures at the site; the 2003 excavation was the comprehensive court-directed elaboration of these earlier findings.
- **K. K. Muhammad's memoir** (*Njan Enna Bharatheeyan*, 2018): a Muslim ASI archaeologist who participated in the 1976–77 excavation publicly confirms the presence of temple-architecture evidence. This is significant as Muslim-insider confirmation of the archaeological record.
- **The Supreme Court's 2019 evaluation**: §786–§800 of the judgment is the most comprehensive judicial assessment of the ASI report's methodology, findings, and conclusions.

## Evidence against / counterpoints

- **Suraj Bhan and other dissenting affidavits** (2003–05): Suraj Bhan, an ASI observer at the excavation and a recognised archaeologist, filed affidavits arguing that the structural evidence (particularly the pillar-base patterning) could support alternative interpretations — an Eidgah, a residential complex, or a non-temple secular structure. Other historians filed similar affidavits. The Supreme Court's 2019 judgment evaluated these dissenting views and found the ASI's temple-structure interpretation more compelling on the balance of evidence.
- **The 1991 "Historians' Report to the Nation"** (Romila Thapar, R. S. Sharma, M. Athar Ali, D. N. Jha, Suraj Bhan): a pre-2003 document arguing that available evidence at that time did not support a pre-existing temple finding. This document predates the 2003 excavation and is not a critique of the report itself.
- **Disputes over the dating of specific finds**: some of the recovered architectural fragments have been dated variously by different scholars; the ASI report's 12th-century identification of the temple stratum has been challenged for some specific elements. The 12th-century dating is the report's overall finding; specific fragment dates remain academically contested in narrow ways.
- **Distinction between archaeological finding and devotional claim**: the report establishes the existence of a pre-existing temple structure; it does not (and cannot) establish whether that structure was a Rama Janmasthan temple specifically, or whether Rama was born at the site. Articles that elide this distinction overstate what the report claims.

## Scope boundaries

- This claim is **not** addressing the December 1992 demolition of the Babri Masjid, the question of whether that demolition was legally or morally justified, or the contemporary politics of religious-site disputes.
- This claim is **not** addressing the devotional question of whether Rama was born at this specific physical location. The ASI report addresses a historical-archaeological question, not a religious-faith question.
- This claim **is** strictly addressing what the 2003 ASI excavation documented, what conclusions the report drew, and what role the report played in the 2019 Supreme Court verdict.
- This claim **is** distinguishing the report's stated conclusion (pre-existing temple structure consistent with 12th-century dating) from interpretive extensions (specifically a Rama temple). The latter requires additional documentary evidence that lies outside the ASI report's scope.
- This claim is **not** taking a position on the contemporary legal questions around the 2024 Ram Mandir consecration or the alternative 5-acre mosque plot. Those are post-judgment political-administrative questions.

## Reusable examples

- **The procedural anchor**: the 2003 excavation was conducted under court direction with observers from all parties present throughout. This procedural rigour is what distinguishes the ASI 2003 report from informal or unilateral archaeological surveys, and what gave it standing in the 2019 Supreme Court judgment. Articles handling the report should foreground this procedural context.
- **The ~50 pillar bases pattern**: the regular geometric pattern of pillar bases beneath the mosque footprint is the single most consequential finding. Articles can describe the pattern (rows and columns of pillar bases, consistent in size and spacing) without taking sides in the interpretive debate over what kind of pillared structure they supported.
- **The Supreme Court §786–§800 evaluation**: the 2019 judgment's evaluation of the ASI report is publicly available and is among the most comprehensive judicial assessments of archaeological evidence in modern Indian jurisprudence. Articles citing the legal acceptance of the report should reference these paragraphs.
- **K. K. Muhammad's memoir**: a Muslim ASI archaeologist who participated in the 1976–77 phase publicly confirms the presence of temple-architecture evidence at the site (*Njan Enna Bharatheeyan*, 2018). This is significant as Muslim-insider confirmation that closes off the "this is all Hindu-nationalist interpretation" deflection.
- **The distinction between archaeological and devotional claims**: when articles address the report's findings, they should be careful to distinguish what the report does conclude (pre-existing temple structure) from what it does not conclude (that the temple was specifically Rama's birthplace). The latter is a cumulative inference from multiple evidence sources, not the report's own claim.
