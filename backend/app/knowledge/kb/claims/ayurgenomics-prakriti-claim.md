## Plain-language statement

A widely circulated claim says modern genetics has "proven" Ayurvedic prakriti types (Vata, Pitta, Kapha) by showing they correlate with distinct genetic profiles. The defensible version is narrower: at least one genome-wide SNP study (Govindaraj et al. 2015) reported that principal component analysis could separate self-classified prakriti groups using genetic markers at levels beyond what ancestry alone explains, and follow-up papers have identified candidate genes associated with specific prakriti phenotypes. This is an active research programme, not a settled validation — and it does not, on its own, establish prakriti as a clinically actionable framework for personalized medicine.

## Decomposed sub-claims

1. **"A published study showed genetic separation of prakriti groups."** — **Supported.** Govindaraj et al. (Scientific Reports, 2015) analyzed ~52 SNPs in ~3000 individuals and reported that principal component analysis separated Vata, Pitta, and Kapha self-classified individuals in a way not fully explained by ancestry.
2. **"Specific genes have been identified as correlated with specific prakriti types."** — **Partially supported.** Follow-up work has reported associations between genes like PGM1 and CYP2C19 and Pitta-dominant phenotypes, consistent with metabolic differences predicted by classical Ayurvedic texts. These are associations in specific studies, not replicated consensus across multiple independent cohorts.
3. **"Ayurgenomics validates Ayurveda as equivalent to precision medicine."** — **Not supported.** The current research programme is exploratory. It suggests there may be measurable physiological correlates to classical prakriti categories. It does not establish that prakriti-based treatment is clinically equivalent or superior to modern precision medicine, which is built on very different evidentiary standards.
4. **"Prakriti classification is reliable across classifiers."** — **Partially supported.** Interrater reliability of prakriti assessment is a known methodological challenge in the literature, and different practitioners can classify the same individual differently. The research often uses standardized questionnaires to reduce this, but it remains a meaningful source of noise.
5. **"The WHO or mainstream biomedical community endorses ayurgenomics as personalized medicine."** — **Not supported.** Research papers in biomedical journals discuss the possibility. Some Indian government bodies (Ministry of AYUSH) actively fund the research. Mainstream precision medicine has not adopted the framework.
6. **"Ayurgenomics is a viable research programme worth taking seriously."** — **Supported.** The published work is real, the methodology is improving, and the hypothesis — that there are stable physiological phenotypes correlating with classical Ayurvedic classifications — is empirically testable. "Worth studying" is a defensible claim; "already proven" is not.

## Evidence for

- **Primary empirical**:
  - Govindaraj et al. (2015), "Genome-wide analysis correlates Ayurveda Prakriti," Scientific Reports 5:15786. The landmark paper establishing that PCA on ~52 SNPs separates prakriti groups.
  - Chatterjee & Joshi review (2022) in Frontiers in Pharmacology summarizing the ayurgenomics literature.
  - Dey & Pahwa review in J. Ayurveda Integr. Med. on prakriti-metabolism-genotype associations.
- **Textual**: Charaka Samhita and Sushruta Samhita on prakriti, dosha, and individual variation in treatment.
- **Modern scholarly context**: Wujastyk's The Roots of Ayurveda remains the best accessible overview for situating these claims in the longer history.

## Evidence against / counterpoints

- Sample sizes and replication: the 2015 paper is one study, and the follow-up literature includes smaller studies with methodological heterogeneity.
- The PCA separation is statistical, not mechanistic. Even if prakriti groups cluster in some genetic space, that does not establish that prakriti classifications capture the *same* underlying biological variation that precision medicine uses.
- Self-classification bias: if participants know their "type" before the test, any correlation can reflect shared cultural assumptions rather than biology.
- Peer-reviewed precision medicine literature has not incorporated prakriti as a stratification variable, which indicates that the mainstream biomedical community has not found the evidence compelling enough to adopt it.

## Scope boundaries

- This claim is **not** saying Ayurveda has no empirical basis or is "just superstition." The classical texts contain millennia of clinical observation encoded in their own framework.
- This claim is **not** saying ayurgenomics research is pseudoscientific. It is published peer-reviewed work and should be treated with the same care as any emerging research programme.
- This claim **is** pushing back against the viral framing "science has proven Ayurveda" and the symmetric dismissal "Ayurveda has no science." Both miss what the actual research shows, which is preliminary support for a hypothesis that deserves more work.

## Reusable examples

- **The Govindaraj 2015 PCA figure.** This is the image most often cited in ayurgenomics presentations: three clusters in PCA space corresponding to Vata-, Pitta-, and Kapha-dominant individuals. Reference it with care: it is suggestive, not definitive.
- **The CYP2C19 association.** Pitta-dominant individuals in some studies show different CYP2C19 genotype frequencies. Since CYP2C19 metabolizes many common drugs (including clopidogrel, some antidepressants), this is an exact spot where the ayurgenomics hypothesis makes a testable prediction about drug response — the kind of claim precision medicine can actually evaluate.
- **The clinical gap.** Mainstream precision medicine asks "will this patient respond to drug X?" and answers with a genotype or biomarker. Ayurgenomics asks "what is this patient's constitution?" and answers with a three-way classification. Even if both end up useful, they are not answering the same question.

## When this claim matters

- When a reader asks "has science proven Ayurveda," "ayurgenomics," "prakriti genetics," or "Ayurveda personalized medicine."
- When writing about Ayurveda for an audience that is skeptical of "tradition" but open to evidence.
- When trying to hold the defensible middle between uncritical enthusiasm and reflexive dismissal — both of which alienate the audience we want.
