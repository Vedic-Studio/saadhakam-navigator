## Plain-language statement

Sultan Sikandar Shah Miri, sixth ruler of the Shah Mir Sultanate of Kashmir (reigned c. 1389–1413 CE), conducted a systematic campaign of destruction against the pre-Islamic Hindu and Buddhist temple corpus of the Kashmir Valley. The Martand Sun Temple — a magnificent 8th-century structure built by Lalitaditya Muktapida of the Karkota dynasty, in a striking colonnaded form on the Mattan plateau in Anantnag — was destroyed during this campaign, leaving the ruins now visible as an ASI-protected monument. Many other major temples (Vijayeshvara at Vijabror, Bhimasvamin, Sureshvari, the temple complex at Bijbihara, the great Bhutesha temple) were either destroyed or stripped to their foundations. The title "Butshikan" (idol-breaker) given to Sikandar comes from Kashmir's own Persian-language Muslim chroniclers — most notably Mirza Haidar Dughlat in the *Tarikh-i-Rashidi* (1546), Pir Hassan Shah in the *Tarikh-i-Hassan*, and the anonymous *Baharistan-i-Shahi* — and was an honorific in their tradition. Together with the contemporary Sanskrit account by the Kashmiri Brahmin chronicler Jonaraja (writing his continuation of Kalhana's *Rajatarangini* during and immediately after Sikandar's reign), this gives Kashmir's temple destruction an unusually dense documentary record from both sides.

## Decomposed sub-claims

1. **"Sultan Sikandar of Kashmir was given the title 'Butshikan' (idol-breaker) by Kashmir's own Muslim chroniclers."** — **Supported.** The title appears across the Kashmir Persian chronicle tradition — Mirza Haidar Dughlat's *Tarikh-i-Rashidi*, Pir Hassan Shah's *Tarikh-i-Hassan*, the *Baharistan-i-Shahi*, and Saiyid Ali's *Tarikh-i-Kashmir*. These were Muslim sources internal to Kashmir, not hostile external ones. The title was not pejorative in their tradition; it was honorific, marking Sikandar as a Sultan who fulfilled the ruler's duty of destroying idols. This Muslim-internal usage is what makes the "Butshikan" title particularly difficult to dismiss as Hindu propaganda — it is Kashmir's own Muslim chroniclers who chose this title for him.

2. **"Sikandar Butshikan conducted a systematic temple-destruction campaign in the Kashmir Valley c. 1389–1413."** — **Supported by both Muslim and Hindu contemporary sources.** The destruction is documented in the Persian Kashmir chronicles (Tarikh-i-Rashidi, Tarikh-i-Hassan, Baharistan-i-Shahi, Tarikh-i-Kashmir) and, independently, by the contemporary Sanskrit *Rajatarangini* continuation by Jonaraja (Brahmin court historian writing during and just after Sikandar's reign). The convergence of Muslim-source and Hindu-source contemporary accounts is unusual and evidentially strong.

3. **"The Martand Sun Temple was destroyed during Sikandar's campaign."** — **Supported.** Martand (the great Sun Temple on the Mattan plateau, built c. 725–750 CE by Lalitaditya Muktapida) is identified in both Persian and Sanskrit sources as a target of the Sikandar-period destruction. The ruins — well-preserved enough to show the original 84-column peristyle plan, the central shrine plan, and the surrounding monastic cells — are consistent with deliberate destruction rather than natural collapse: the structures were systematically dismantled or burned, not weathered. ASI surveys (Cunningham onwards) have documented the destruction pattern.

4. **"Sikandar's destruction extended to a wide range of Kashmir's pre-Islamic monuments, not just Martand."** — **Supported.** Persian chronicles and Jonaraja's Sanskrit continuation document destruction at multiple sites — Vijayeshvara (the great Shiva temple at Vijabror/Bijbihara), Bhimasvamin, Sureshvari, the Bhutesha temple, and many smaller shrines across the valley. The list of converted or demolished monuments in Srinagar alone is substantial. The destruction was not a single targeted operation but a sustained valley-wide campaign.

5. **"Sikandar's brahmin minister Suhabhatta (who converted to Islam and took the name Saifuddin) was a principal architect of the temple-destruction policy."** — **Supported.** Jonaraja's Sanskrit continuation of the *Rajatarangini* explicitly identifies Suhabhatta as the convert-minister who, having abandoned his ancestral tradition, pressed the Sultan to enforce restrictive measures against the Brahmin population, including destruction of temples, prohibition of public Hindu worship, and imposition of jizya. The Persian chronicles confirm Suhabhatta/Saifuddin's role. This is a rare case where the contemporary Hindu source names the specific official responsible.

6. **"Kashmir's pre-Islamic temple corpus, documented by Kalhana's 12th-century Rajatarangini, was largely destroyed within Sikandar's single reign."** — **Substantially supported.** Kalhana's *Rajatarangini* (1148 CE) catalogues hundreds of temples across the Kashmir Valley by name, founder, and location — providing a pre-Islamic baseline against which to measure the post-Sikandar landscape. The surviving pre-Islamic monument corpus in modern Kashmir is small: Martand (ruined), the Avantisvamin temple at Avantipur (ruined), the Pandrethan temple, the Awantiswami temple, and a handful of smaller structures. The discrepancy between Kalhana's catalogue and the surviving corpus is consistent with the systematic destruction documented in the chronicles. (Some attrition is attributable to other causes — Aurangzeb-period destructions, natural decay, and the 18th-century Afghan period — but the bulk of the disappearance dates to Sikandar's reign.)

7. **"The destruction was solely caused by religious motivation."** — **Partially contested.** The convert-minister Suhabhatta/Saifuddin's role suggests a domestic political dynamic (a convert seeking to demonstrate orthodoxy by enforcing the harshest measures against his former community) as well as religious motivation. Some modern Indian-Muslim historiography (Mohibbul Hasan, *Kashmir Under the Sultans*, 1959) treats Sikandar's policy as the result of Suhabhatta's influence rather than as Sikandar's own initiative; this is debated. The honest framing: religious motivation was operative and explicit in the chronicles; political-administrative dynamics around the convert-minister were also operative; the campaign's intensity reflected both.

## Evidence for

- **Mirza Haidar Dughlat, *Tarikh-i-Rashidi* (c. 1546)**: a Mughal-period Persian chronicle by a Timurid ruler who briefly governed Kashmir. The English translation by E. Denison Ross (ed. N. Elias, 1895) is the standard citable form. Contains the canonical account of Sikandar's policy with the "Butshikan" title.
- **Pir Hassan Shah, *Tarikh-i-Hassan***: 19th-century Kashmir Persian chronicle drawing on earlier traditions. The major Kashmir-internal historical source.
- **Anonymous, *Baharistan-i-Shahi* (c. 1614)**: Kashmir Persian chronicle of the Shah Mir Sultanate; one of the earliest sustained Kashmiri-Muslim historical works.
- **Jonaraja, *Rajatarangini* continuation (15th c.)**: the most evidentially significant single source — a Sanskrit chronicle by a Kashmiri Brahmin court historian writing during and immediately after Sikandar's reign. Documents the destruction from the perspective of a witnessing Brahmin community. The Srikant Kaul edition (Sanskrit critical text) and the Walter Slaje annotated work on the late Rajatarangini chronicles are the scholarly sources.
- **Kalhana, *Rajatarangini* (1148 CE)**: the pre-destruction baseline. Kalhana's 12th-century catalogue of Kashmir's temples (translated by M. A. Stein, 1900) provides the inventory against which post-Sikandar losses can be measured.
- **Mohibbul Hasan, *Kashmir Under the Sultans* (1959, repub. 2005)**: the standard modern scholarly history of the Kashmir Sultanate, by an Indian-Muslim historian. Treats Sikandar's policy candidly but with attention to the Suhabhatta-political-dynamic counterpoint.
- **Archaeological evidence**: ASI surveys of Martand, Avantipur, and other ruined sites document destruction patterns consistent with deliberate demolition or burning, not weathering.

## Evidence against / counterpoints

- **No serious scholarship denies that Sikandar's reign saw systematic temple destruction.** The disagreement concerns the relative weight of religious motivation versus the Suhabhatta political dynamic, and the precise extent of destruction at specific sites.
- **The Suhabhatta agency thesis**: some historians (Mohibbul Hasan most notably) argue that the destruction policy was primarily the work of Suhabhatta/Saifuddin acting as Sikandar's executive, with Sikandar himself being more passive. This does not deny the destruction; it locates responsibility differently. Other historians treat Sikandar as the principal agent with Suhabhatta as instrument. The Persian chronicles credit Sikandar; the Sanskrit Jonaraja emphasises Suhabhatta. Both readings are present in the sources.
- **The "Butshikan" title as honorific**: while the Muslim chroniclers used the title as praise, modern Muslim historiography in Kashmir has sometimes argued that the destruction's scale was exaggerated by later (Mughal-period) chroniclers seeking to align Kashmir's Sultanate history with broader Sunni iconoclast traditions. This argument is genuinely contested in the scholarly literature; the destruction itself is not.
- **Natural decay and later destructions**: some loss of the Kashmir temple corpus dates to causes other than the Sikandar campaign — Aurangzeb-period destructions, the 18th-century Afghan period (Durrani dynasty), and natural attrition. Articles should not attribute the entire loss to Sikandar alone, while recognising that he is the single largest contributor.

## Scope boundaries

- This claim is **not** asserting that all of Kashmir's pre-Islamic monuments were destroyed solely by Sikandar. Other causes contributed; the Sikandar campaign was the largest single cause.
- This claim is **not** addressing contemporary Kashmir politics, the post-1947 question of Hindu and Pandit communities, or the 1990 Pandit exodus. Those are present-day questions, separate from the medieval historical record.
- This claim **is** restricted to the historical question of what the contemporary Persian and Sanskrit sources document about Sikandar's reign, c. 1389–1413.
- This claim **is** treating the convergence of Persian-Muslim and Sanskrit-Hindu contemporary sources as unusually strong evidentially. When chroniclers from both communities, writing roughly contemporaneously, agree on what happened, the historical record is more secure than when one side is silent.

## Reusable examples

- **The Butshikan title as Muslim-source self-description**: when articles need to address the "is the destruction story Hindu propaganda?" deflection, the most economical answer is to note that the title "Butshikan" was given to Sikandar by Kashmir's own Persian-language Muslim chroniclers as praise, not by hostile external sources. The Tarikh-i-Rashidi, Tarikh-i-Hassan, and Baharistan-i-Shahi are Muslim-internal sources. Their honorific use of "idol-breaker" is what closes off the "this is all Hindu fabrication" deflection.
- **The Jonaraja eyewitness baseline**: Jonaraja wrote his continuation of Kalhana's *Rajatarangini* during and just after Sikandar's reign — a contemporary Brahmin court historian whose account of the destruction (including Suhabhatta's role) is the closest Hindu-source contemporary record. The Sanskrit text is preserved; Slaje and Kaul editions are the scholarly forms.
- **Kalhana baseline / current corpus comparison**: Kalhana's 1148 *Rajatarangini* catalogues hundreds of named Kashmir temples; the current standing pre-Islamic corpus in Kashmir is a handful of ruined structures (Martand, Avantipur, Pandrethan). The catalog-to-survivors gap is the most concrete way to convey the destruction's scale. Articles can quote a sampling of Kalhana's temple names (Jayasvamin, Vishnusvamin, Bhimasvamin, Vishnu Ranasvamin, Narendrasvamin, Vikrameshvara, Diddamatha, Skandabhavana Vihara, Meruvardhanaswamin) and note that none survive.
- **The Martand ruins themselves**: the surviving plinth, central shrine ruins, and 84-column peristyle outline of the Martand Sun Temple are visible on the Mattan plateau in Anantnag. The destruction is evident in the controlled patterning of broken structural elements (not random weathering). For an article, the Martand ruins are a primary-source physical object the reader can encounter directly.
- **The Suhabhatta convert-minister dynamic**: a rare case where the historical record names the specific official driving the policy and identifies him as a Brahmin convert to Islam. The contemporary Sanskrit source's identification of this dynamic is a useful complication for any account that treats the destruction as anonymous "the Sultan did X" — there were named actors with specific biographies driving named decisions.
