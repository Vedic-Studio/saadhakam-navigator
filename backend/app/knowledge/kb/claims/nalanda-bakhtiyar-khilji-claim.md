## Plain-language statement

Around 1193 CE, the Turkic commander Bakhtiyar Khilji (Ikhtiyar-ud-din Muhammad ibn Bakhtiyar Khilji), serving Mu'izzuddin Ghori, led a small cavalry force into Bihar and assaulted a major Buddhist monastic complex which he mistook for a fortress. The standard contemporary Muslim source — Minhaj-us-Siraj's *Tabaqat-i-Nasiri* (c. 1260) — records that the inhabitants were "Brahmans with shaven heads" (meaning Buddhist monks), that they were killed, and that a large quantity of books was found whose contents nobody could read because the monks who could explain them had all been killed. The destruction of a major Buddhist monastic complex in Bihar c. 1193 is well-attested. Whether that complex was Nalanda Mahavihara, the better-known Odantapuri vihara (at modern Bihar Sharif), or Vikramashila — or all three in the same campaign — is less certain than popular accounts suggest, and the romantic "library burned for six months" framing rests on the much-later Tibetan account of Taranatha, not on the contemporary record.

## Decomposed sub-claims

1. **"Bakhtiyar Khilji's force assaulted a major Buddhist monastic complex in Bihar c. 1193 CE."** — **Supported.** The Tabaqat-i-Nasiri records the campaign explicitly. Minhaj-us-Siraj wrote within seventy years of the event using oral testimony from people who knew participants, and his account is internally consistent and unembellished.

2. **"The monastic complex Bakhtiyar assaulted was Nalanda Mahavihara specifically."** — **Partially supported; commonly conflated with Odantapuri.** Minhaj-us-Siraj's description of the site as a fortress-like complex at "Bihar" matches Odantapuri (Sanskrit: Uddandapura, modern Bihar Sharif), the major Pala-period vihara approximately 60 km from Nalanda. The name "Bihar" — and the modern state name — derives from "vihara" because Odantapuri was the regional fortified monastery par excellence. Modern scholarship (D. R. Patil, Hartmut Scharfe, Johan Elverskog) tends to identify the principal target of Bakhtiyar's campaign as Odantapuri rather than Nalanda Mahavihara specifically. Nalanda itself may have been destroyed in the same campaign or a slightly later one — the Tibetan source Taranatha conflates them, and the question is genuinely uncertain.

3. **"Nalanda Mahavihara was destroyed by Bakhtiyar Khilji's force."** — **Partially supported; the contemporary record is ambiguous.** Nalanda was clearly in decline by the 12th century — patronage had been declining since the late Pala period, the major faculty had migrated, and archaeological evidence shows reduced occupation. The site shows evidence of late-12th-century destruction (charring, broken statuary), and the Turkic campaigns in Bihar c. 1193–1200 are the most likely cause. But the specific identification of Nalanda as a target of Bakhtiyar's named raid rests on inference from Tibetan sources (Taranatha) and from archaeological dating, not on a contemporary Muslim chronicle naming Nalanda. The most defensible framing: "a major Buddhist monastic complex in Bihar was destroyed by Bakhtiyar's force; Nalanda was destroyed in this period; whether by Bakhtiyar's specific raid or in an immediately subsequent campaign is uncertain."

4. **"The library at Nalanda burned for six months."** — **Not well-supported by contemporary sources.** The vivid "library burned for six months" claim comes from Taranatha's *History of Buddhism in India*, completed in Tibet in 1608 — over 400 years after the event, based on oral traditions transmitted through the Tibetan-Buddhist diaspora. Taranatha is a valuable source for what Tibetan Buddhists believed about the destruction of Indian Buddhism, but his account is secondary and incorporates clear oral-tradition embellishments. The "six months" detail is not in Minhaj-us-Siraj's contemporary account. Articles should cite the library destruction as part of Tibetan-Buddhist memory rather than as documented chronology. (That said: a major monastic library would have been a substantial structure, and a slow burn over weeks or months is physically plausible for a structure full of palm-leaf and birch-bark manuscripts.)

5. **"Bakhtiyar mistook the monastery for a fortress."** — **Supported.** Minhaj-us-Siraj's account explicitly describes the site as a "fort" (qila) that Bakhtiyar attacked. The Pala-period viharas (Odantapuri, Vikramashila, Somapura) were enclosed in massive walls and could resemble fortifications from the outside. The detail that Bakhtiyar's force did not initially recognise the inhabitants as monks until after the assault (when they found books they could not read and had no one left to translate them) is consistent with this military-target framing. It does not lessen the destruction; it locates it within the pattern of cavalry raids that targeted both fortifications and the wealth they contained.

6. **"The destruction of Nalanda/Odantapuri ended organized Buddhist monasticism in northern India."** — **Substantially supported, with qualification.** The c. 1193–1200 destruction of the major Bihar viharas (Odantapuri, Vikramashila, and probably Nalanda) is the conventionally cited terminus for organized Buddhist monasticism in northern India. The disappearance is not solely attributable to this campaign — Buddhism in northern India was already in decline due to the loss of royal patronage, Brahmanical resurgence, and absorption of Buddhist ideas into Hindu tantra and devotional traditions. But the destruction of the institutional infrastructure (large monasteries with international student bodies, libraries, and ordination lineages) closed the institutional pathway by which Buddhism had reproduced itself. The Tibetan-Buddhist tradition's account of fleeing monks carrying texts to Tibet (preserved in the Tibetan canon) is the documentary trace of this institutional collapse.

7. **"Nalanda's decline began before Bakhtiyar."** — **Supported.** Archaeological and inscriptional evidence shows reduced patronage and occupation at Nalanda from the late Pala period (10th–11th c.) onward. The Mahavihara had passed its peak of international prominence (the 7th-century descriptions by Xuanzang and Yijing depict an institution at its height) and was in a slow institutional decline before the c. 1193 destruction. This contextualises but does not erase the destruction event.

## Evidence for

- **Minhaj-us-Siraj, *Tabaqat-i-Nasiri* (c. 1260)**: the standard contemporary Persian chronicle of the Ghurid and Slave-Sultanate period. Records Bakhtiyar's Bihar campaign in detail, including the "Brahmans with shaven heads" (Buddhist monks) and the books-without-translators detail. H. G. Raverty's 1881 English translation is the citable form (Asiatic Society of Bengal). Reproduced in Elliot & Dowson vol. 2.
- **Archaeological evidence at Nalanda**: ASI excavations at the Nalanda site (initiated by A. Cunningham, continued through the 20th century) document late-12th-century destruction layers — charring, broken statuary, collapsed structures. Patil's 1963 *Antiquarian Remains in Bihar* compiles the field evidence.
- **Tibetan-Buddhist textual record**: large numbers of Sanskrit Buddhist texts survive only in Tibetan translation because the Indian originals were lost in this period. The Tibetan canon's structure is itself evidence of the institutional collapse — texts were rescued by monks fleeing to Tibet.
- **Taranatha, *History of Buddhism in India* (1608)**: late but informed Tibetan source; describes the destruction of Indian Buddhism with detail (including the "library burned for six months" claim). To be cited as Tibetan-Buddhist memory, not as contemporary chronology.
- **Hartmut Scharfe, *Education in Ancient India* (Brill 2002)**: scholarly chapter on the monastic universities; distinguishes Nalanda, Odantapuri, and Vikramashila and treats the destruction question carefully.

## Evidence against / counterpoints

- **The Nalanda-vs-Odantapuri identification problem**: the contemporary Muslim source (Tabaqat-i-Nasiri) describes the site as a fort at "Bihar" — most likely Odantapuri at Bihar Sharif, not Nalanda. Popular accounts that say "Bakhtiyar Khilji destroyed Nalanda" elide this identification problem. The honest scholarly framing distinguishes the documented Odantapuri destruction (in Minhaj-us-Siraj) from the more inferential Nalanda destruction (likely in the same period, possibly the same campaign, but not specifically named in the contemporary source).
- **The "library burned for six months" is from a 17th-century Tibetan source, not a 13th-century contemporary one.** The detail is part of Tibetan-Buddhist diaspora memory; it may capture a real event in stylised form, but it cannot be cited as documented chronology.
- **Buddhism's decline in northern India had multiple causes**, of which the c. 1193 destruction is one (substantial but not unique). Loss of royal patronage from the late Pala period, Brahmanical resurgence, and the absorption of Buddhist concepts into Hindu tantra and devotional traditions are documented contributory factors. Articles that present "Bakhtiyar killed Indian Buddhism" as a single-cause explanation oversimplify a multi-century process.
- **The "shaven Brahmans" description**: Minhaj-us-Siraj's use of "Brahmans" for the monks is the Persian chronicler's category-confusion — he is using "Brahman" as a generic term for "Indian religious specialist" rather than as a specifically Hindu term. Articles that quote this passage should gloss the term for modern readers.

## Scope boundaries

- This claim is **not** denying that a major Buddhist monastic complex in Bihar was destroyed by Bakhtiyar's force c. 1193. The Tabaqat-i-Nasiri's record is internally consistent and contemporary.
- This claim **is** distinguishing the well-attested destruction of Odantapuri (the named site in the chronicle) from the more inferential identification of that site with Nalanda Mahavihara (probable but not chronicle-confirmed).
- This claim **is** noting that the "library burned for six months" detail is from a 17th-century Tibetan source, not a 13th-century contemporary one, and should be cited accordingly.
- This claim is **not** asserting that the c. 1193 destruction was the sole cause of Buddhism's disappearance from northern India. The decline had multiple causes; the destruction of the institutional infrastructure was the most discrete and acute one.
- This claim is **not** about Nalanda's foundation date, its peak (7th-century Xuanzang descriptions), or the modern revival of Nalanda University. Those are separate questions.

## Reusable examples

- **The Tabaqat-i-Nasiri passage** (Raverty trans.): "Muhammad Bakht-yar advanced with great vigour, attacked the place, and Muhammad Bakht-yar, with two of his brothers, advanced to the gate of the fortress and engaged in the fight. By dint of the unfailing aid of God Most High the place was conquered. Most of the inhabitants of the place were Brahmans with shaven heads. They were put to death. Large numbers of books were found there, and when the Muhammadans saw them, they called for some person to explain their contents, but all of the men had been killed." This is the kind of direct primary-source quotation an article can anchor to. The "books without translators" detail is darkly evocative and is contemporary; the "library burned for six months" detail (which is not in this passage) is later Tibetan embellishment.
- **The Bihar-vihara-Bihar etymology**: the modern state name "Bihar" derives from "vihara" (Buddhist monastery) because Odantapuri at Bihar Sharif was the regional vihara par excellence. The state name itself is a memorial to the destroyed institutional Buddhism of the region.
- **The Tibetan canon as documentary trace**: large numbers of Mahayana and Vajrayana Sanskrit texts survive only in Tibetan translation because the Indian originals were lost in this period. The Tibetan Kangyur and Tengyur are themselves an artifact of Nalanda/Vikramashila's institutional collapse — they preserve what monks managed to carry out as they fled.
- **Xuanzang baseline**: the 7th-century Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang spent five years at Nalanda and described it as housing thousands of resident scholars from across Asia, a curriculum spanning Buddhist philosophy, grammar, logic, medicine, and astronomy, and a library of substantial scale. The Xuanzang baseline is what makes the 1193 destruction historically significant: it was not the destruction of a marginal local monastery but of the institution that had been Asia's largest centre of Buddhist learning for over five centuries.

## When this claim matters

- When a reader lands on "Nalanda destruction," "Bakhtiyar Khilji Nalanda," "library of Nalanda burned," "end of Buddhism in India," or "destruction of Buddhist universities."
- When writing about Buddhist history in India, the Pala period, the disappearance of Indian Buddhism, the Tibetan canon's origins, or the medieval Bihar region.
- When the temptation is to inflate (the cinematic "library burned for six months" framing as documented chronology) or to deflect (the destruction never happened, or had no impact on Indian Buddhism). Both framings miss the careful middle: the destruction event is well-attested but commonly mis-identified with Nalanda rather than Odantapuri, the romantic embellishments rest on later sources, and the institutional collapse is real but had multiple causes.
- When an article needs to distinguish what the contemporary Muslim chronicle says from what the later Tibetan source adds.
