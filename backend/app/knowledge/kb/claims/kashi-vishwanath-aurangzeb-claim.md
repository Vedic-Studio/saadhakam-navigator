## Plain-language statement

In April 1669, the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb issued a general firman ordering the demolition of "the schools and temples of the infidels" in the provinces — with specific mention of Tatta, Multan, and Varanasi. The Vishweshwar (Vishwanath) temple at Kashi was demolished pursuant to this order; the Gyanvapi Mosque was built on the same site using temple materials, with a substantial portion of the original temple's western wall remaining visible as part of the mosque's qibla wall. The destruction is recorded in Aurangzeb's own court chronicle, the *Maasir-i-Alamgiri*, and independently confirmed in the 20th century by Maulana Abdul Hai, a senior Indian Muslim scholar (Rector of Darul-Ulum Nadwa) writing in Urdu in 1973.

## Decomposed sub-claims

1. **"Aurangzeb issued a general April 1669 firman ordering the demolition of temples and schools in Tatta, Multan, and Benares."** — **Supported.** The Maasir-i-Alamgiri records the firman explicitly: "Orders respecting Islamic affairs were issued to the governors of all the provinces that the schools and places of worship of the irreligious be subject to demolition and that with the utmost urgency the manner of teaching and the public practices of the sects of these misbelievers be suppressed." Jadunath Sarkar's translation places this in April 1669. The firman is also discussed in Sarkar's *History of Aurangzib* vol. 3.

2. **"The Vishweshwar (Vishwanath) temple at Kashi was demolished pursuant to that firman."** — **Supported.** The Maasir-i-Alamgiri records, in the entry for September 1669: "It was reported that, according to the Emperor's command, his officers had demolished the temple of Viswanath at Kashi." This is Aurangzeb's own court chronicle reporting back the execution of his order; the chain of evidence (firman → demolition → court report) is internal to the Mughal state record.

3. **"The Gyanvapi Mosque was constructed on the same site, using temple materials."** — **Supported.** The 20th-century Urdu work *Hindustan Islami Ahad Mein* by Maulana Abdul Hai (Nadwa, Lucknow, 1973) — a major Muslim-scholarly source — confirms: "The mosque [Gyanvapi] which was built by Alamgir is, in fact, the site of the Bisheshwar Temple. Alamgir got the temple demolished and built a mosque there with those very stones." The English translation of Abdul Hai's work (1977) omitted these passages — a redaction that Arun Shourie documented in his 1989 essay "Hideaway Communalism."

4. **"Temple fabric is visibly embedded in the western wall of the Gyanvapi Mosque."** — **Supported by direct physical observation.** The qibla (western) wall of the Gyanvapi Mosque incorporates the pre-existing temple's eastern wall, with carved pilasters, lotus medallions, and the upper portions of the original temple visible to any visitor. Photographic documentation has existed since the colonial period; the structural survival of the temple wall as the mosque's qibla wall is consistent with the documentary record of construction on the site.

5. **"The 1991 Vishwanath Mandir is the original temple rebuilt on its original site."** — **Partially supported, requires distinction.** The Vishwanath Temple now adjoining the Gyanvapi Mosque was reconstructed in 1780 by Maharani Ahilyabai Holkar of Indore, on land adjacent to the original site but not on the precise footprint of the 1669-demolished temple — that footprint is occupied by the Gyanvapi Mosque. The 1780 Ahilyabai temple is itself a major Shaiva pilgrimage centre and the canonical "Kashi Vishwanath" of contemporary worship; the precise question of where the original pre-1669 temple stood is the subject of the ongoing Gyanvapi survey litigation (2022 onward).

6. **"Aurangzeb's motivation was uniform religious iconoclasm directed against all Hindu temples."** — **Contested.** Aurangzeb's temple policy was inconsistent in its application: many temples (including major sites at Mathura, Ujjain, Chitrakoot, and Guwahati) were destroyed; many others were left alone and even granted state patronage (the Brahmin priests at Vrindavan, Chitrakoot, and Ujjain received Mughal grants throughout his reign). The current scholarly position (Audrey Truschke, *Aurangzeb: The Man and the Myth*, Penguin 2017; building on earlier work by Satish Chandra and others) is that Aurangzeb's destructions were largely targeted — often against sites associated with rebellion, political resistance, or rival authority — though sometimes framed in explicitly religious terms in the court record. The 1669 firman and its execution at Kashi and Mathura are among the most clearly religiously-framed cases.

## Evidence for

- **Maasir-i-Alamgiri (Saqi Mustaid Khan, completed 1710)**: the official Mughal court chronicle compiled from state archives during Aurangzeb's son Bahadur Shah's reign. The text records both the general firman (April 1669) and the specific demolition report (September 1669). The standard English translation is Jadunath Sarkar's, published by the Royal Asiatic Society of Bengal in 1947. Earlier excerpts are in Elliot & Dowson vol. 7.
- **Maulana Abdul Hai, *Hindustan Islami Ahad Mein* (Urdu, Nadwa 1973)**: a major 20th-century Indian Muslim scholarly source that explicitly confirms the Gyanvapi-on-Vishweshwar conversion. Abdul Hai (d. 1923) was Rector of Darul-Ulum Nadwatul-Ulama, Lucknow — one of the most authoritative Indian Muslim seminaries. His confirmation as a Muslim insider source is evidentially significant.
- **Physical evidence**: the western (qibla) wall of the Gyanvapi Mosque consists of an unmistakable Hindu temple wall with intact carved iconography. Photographic and architectural documentation is extensive (e.g., ASI surveys, the Banaras Hindu University Department of History records). The structural survival of temple fabric is consistent with the documentary record.
- **Jadunath Sarkar, *History of Aurangzib* (5 vols, 1912–24)**: the definitive scholarly biography, treats the 1669 firman and the Kashi demolition with primary-source citations.

## Evidence against / counterpoints

- **No serious modern scholarship denies the 1669 demolition event.** The disagreement is about the *generalisability* of the case — whether the Kashi destruction reflects a systematic anti-Hindu policy across Aurangzeb's reign, or whether it should be read in conjunction with the many temples Aurangzeb did *not* destroy and to which he granted patronage.
- **Audrey Truschke, *Aurangzeb: The Man and the Myth* (Penguin 2017)**: the most prominent contemporary revisionist position. Does not deny Kashi (acknowledges it explicitly), but argues that the surrounding popular narrative of Aurangzeb as a uniform anti-Hindu zealot is historically inaccurate. Truschke's position has been criticised in turn by other scholars (M. A. Khan, Andrew Truschke critique literature). The honest summary: Truschke's complication of the simplistic "Aurangzeb destroyed everything Hindu" narrative is well-grounded, but does not extend to denying the specific 1669 Kashi case.
- **The Gyanvapi survey litigation (2022–)**: the ongoing Indian court process under which the ASI is conducting a survey of the Gyanvapi Mosque structure. The survey reports (some now public, some sealed) document Hindu temple architecture features within and beneath the mosque. This is contemporary legal record, not historical chronicle, but adds to the evidentiary picture.

## Scope boundaries

- This claim is **not** asserting that Aurangzeb destroyed all Hindu temples, that every mosque in India is on a temple site, or that the Mughal state was uniformly anti-Hindu throughout its reign. Those broader claims are separate and more contested.
- This claim **is** asserting that the specific April 1669 firman, the subsequent demolition of the Vishweshwar temple at Kashi, and the construction of the Gyanvapi Mosque on the same site are well-documented historical events with Mughal-internal documentary confirmation.
- This claim is **not** taking a position on the contemporary Gyanvapi litigation, the Places of Worship Act 1991, or the question of whether the Hindu community should be able to reclaim the original site. Those are legal and political questions; this claim addresses only the historical record.
- This claim is **not** asserting that Maulana Abdul Hai's 1973 Urdu confirmation "proves" the destruction in a way the Maasir-i-Alamgiri does not. The 1710 Mughal court chronicle is the primary source; Abdul Hai's testimony is significant because it shows a senior Indian Muslim scholar acknowledging the conversion in the 20th century, refuting the framing that the Vishweshwar-Gyanvapi story is a Hindu fabrication.

## Reusable examples

- **The April 1669 firman text**: "The Lord Cherisher of the Faith learnt that in the provinces of Tatta, Multan, and especially at Benares, the Brahman misbelievers used to teach their false books in their established schools... His Majesty, eager to establish Islam, issued orders to the governors of all the provinces to demolish the schools and temples of the infidels." — Maasir-i-Alamgiri, Sarkar trans. p. 51. This is the kind of direct primary-source quotation an article can use to anchor the claim without inflation.
- **The September 1669 follow-up entry**: "It was reported that, according to the Emperor's command, his officers had demolished the temple of Viswanath at Kashi." — Maasir-i-Alamgiri, Sarkar trans. p. 55. The court chronicle's matter-of-fact reporting of the execution of the firman.
- **Abdul Hai's Urdu testimony**: "The mosque which was built by Alamgir is, in fact, the site of the Bisheshwar Temple. Alamgir got the temple demolished and built a mosque there with those very stones." — *Hindustan Islami Ahad Mein* (Nadwa 1973). The Muslim-insider confirmation that complicates the "this is Hindu propaganda" framing.
- **The visible qibla wall**: the western wall of the Gyanvapi Mosque is, on direct observation, the eastern wall of a Hindu temple — the carved iconography, the lotus medallions, the shikhara-base mouldings are intact and undisguised. This is not a contested archaeological claim; it is a visible fact that any visitor can confirm.
- **The Ahilyabai 1780 rebuild**: the current Vishwanath Temple is Ahilyabai Holkar's reconstruction, built immediately adjacent to but not on the original 1669-demolished site. This distinction matters: when the claim is "Kashi Vishwanath was destroyed in 1669," the destroyed temple is the pre-1669 structure whose footprint is now under the Gyanvapi Mosque, not the 1780 Ahilyabai temple which has its own (more recent) continuity.
