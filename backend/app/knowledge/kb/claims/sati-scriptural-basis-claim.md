## Plain-language statement

The practice of sati — a widow self-immolating on her husband's funeral pyre — is not prescribed in the core Vedic Samhitas. The famous Rigveda 10.18 verse sometimes cited as the scriptural basis is almost certainly about the opposite: the widow is addressed and asked to *rise from the dead man's side* and return to the living. The practice itself has a complicated historiographical trajectory — attested in some medieval sources, debated in dharmaśāstra, dramatized in early modern accounts, and made a matter of colonial law in 1829 — but the claim that it has clear Vedic scriptural sanction does not survive close textual reading.

## Decomposed sub-claims

1. **"Rigveda 10.18 prescribes or endorses sati."** — **Not supported.** The key verse (10.18.8) addresses the widow and instructs her: "Rise, woman, to the world of the living; he is dead beside whom you lie. Come here to the world of those who are awake." Modern scholarly translations (Jamison-Brereton 2014) are unambiguous that this is a call for the widow to return to life. The medieval "sati reading" of the same passage turns on the substitution of a single syllable (agneh for agre), which Max Müller and later scholars identified as a deliberate or accidental alteration.
2. **"The core Vedic Samhitas (Rig, Sama, Yajur, Atharva) prescribe widow immolation."** — **Not supported.** No passage in the core Samhita layer prescribes the practice. The closest references are funerary hymns that describe burial and cremation customs, not widow self-immolation.
3. **"The Dharmasastras uniformly endorse sati."** — **Not supported.** The Dharmasastra corpus is not uniform on this question. Some later texts discuss the practice; others do not. Mimamsa and Vedanta theologians debated the practice's scriptural standing explicitly, and the debate itself demonstrates that there was no consensus.
4. **"Sati was widely practiced in ancient India."** — **Contested.** The historical frequency of the practice is a matter of ongoing scholarly debate. Some periods and regions saw it more than others; the "ancient India" framing flattens a history spanning millennia and wildly different regional cultures.
5. **"Sati was invented or introduced by colonial-era narratives."** — **Not supported.** The practice existed in some regions before the colonial period, and medieval Sanskrit and Persian sources mention it. What the colonial period did was intensify the visibility of the practice, frame it as emblematic of "Hindu tradition," and eventually outlaw it.
6. **"The 1829 abolition reflected an internal Hindu reformist consensus as well as colonial pressure."** — **Supported.** Raja Rammohun Roy and other Hindu reformers had argued against sati from within the tradition, citing scriptural texts (including Rigveda 10.18) against it. The abolition was a joint project of colonial law and indigenous reform.

## Evidence for

- **Primary**:
  - Rigveda 10.18.8 (Jamison-Brereton translation): "Rise up, woman, to the world of the living; you are lying beside this man whose breath is gone. Come here to the world of those who are awake."
  - Max Müller's 19th-century analysis of the agneh/agre substitution in the received text.
- **Modern scholarship**:
  - Jamison-Brereton (2014) — current standard critical translation, treats 10.18 carefully.
  - Arvind Sharma (ed.), Sati (1988) — comprehensive scholarly treatment of the historical and textual questions.
  - Lata Mani, Contentious Traditions (1998) — landmark study of the colonial-era debate and its distortions.
  - Romila Thapar and others have written on the historical frequency question.

## Evidence against / counterpoints

- Some medieval Dharmasastra and Puranic texts do reference the practice, and some of these can be read as approving it under specific circumstances. Dismissing all post-Vedic references is not defensible.
- The practice existed in some regions in some periods. The claim "sati is not in the Vedas" does not by itself mean "sati was never practiced" — it means the Vedic scriptural sanction is not there.
- Pointing only to Rigveda 10.18 risks suggesting that a single verse settles the question. The broader textual debate (Dharmasastra, Mimamsa, medieval commentary) is where the real argument lives.

## Scope boundaries

- This claim is **not** denying that sati was practiced historically. It was, in some regions, in some periods.
- This claim is **not** denying that some post-Vedic texts discuss the practice. They do.
- This claim **is** pushing back against the specific "sati is prescribed in the Vedas" or "sati is a core Hindu scriptural mandate" framing — which is the form that circulates in bad-faith critiques of Hindu tradition and also in some traditionalist defenses.

## Reusable examples

- **Rigveda 10.18.8 in translation.** The Jamison-Brereton line can be quoted directly: "Rise up, woman, to the world of the living; you are lying beside this man whose breath is gone." This is the single most important textual exhibit.
- **The agneh/agre substitution.** Max Müller identified that a reading of the verse used to justify sati turns on a single letter difference between "let them go to the womb of fire" (agneh) and "let them go first" (agre). This is the kind of textual-criticism detail that distinguishes scholarly reading from polemic.
- **Rammohun Roy's intra-Hindu argument.** The 19th-century reformer did not argue "sati is un-Hindu because the Europeans say so." He argued "sati is un-Vedic" from within the scriptural tradition, citing the Rigveda and other sources. This is the frame that matters for anyone writing about Hindu reform.

## When this claim matters

- When a reader asks "does Hinduism prescribe sati," "Rigveda sati verse," "did ancient India burn widows," or "is sati in the Vedas."
- When writing about Hindu reform, the 1829 abolition, colonial-era representations of Hinduism, or the history of women in Hindu tradition.
- When countering either "Hinduism is inherently patriarchal and prescribes widow-burning" (bad-faith critique) or "sati was never a real practice" (traditionalist denial) — both collapse the actual history.
