## Plain-language statement

The Babri Masjid, constructed in 1528 CE at Ayodhya by Mir Baqi under the patronage of the Mughal emperor Babur, was built on a site that — based on the Archaeological Survey of India's 2002–03 court-directed excavation — had a pre-existing massive structure beneath it. The 2003 ASI report concluded that the structural features beneath the mosque (pillar bases, a north–south wall, structural floors, and architectural fragments) were consistent with a pre-existing temple-style structure of the 12th century, built in turn on still earlier strata going back to the early historical period. Multiple pre-British Muslim sources from the mid-19th century (1856–1869) describe the Babri Masjid as having been built on the Janmasthan temple. The Supreme Court of India's 2019 judgment relied on this combined documentary and archaeological evidence in awarding the disputed site to the Hindu plaintiffs. The separate devotional question — whether Rama was born at this specific physical location — is a question of religious faith that lies outside the scope of secular historical inquiry.

## Decomposed sub-claims

This claim requires unusual care in decomposition because the popular framing collapses several distinct questions into one. We separate them deliberately.

1. **"There was a pre-existing structure beneath the Babri Masjid."** — **Supported.** The Archaeological Survey of India's 2002–03 excavation, conducted under direction from the Allahabad High Court with court-appointed observers from both Hindu and Muslim parties present, documented pillar bases in a regular pattern, a north–south wall, structural floors, and architectural fragments beneath the mosque footprint. The existence of a pre-existing substantial structure is the finding least disputed in the report; opponents of the broader temple-identification reading have generally accepted the existence of a pre-existing structure while disputing its nature.

2. **"The pre-existing structure was a temple (not, for example, a mosque, a residential building, or an Islamic-period structure)."** — **Supported by the ASI report, contested by some scholars at the time.** The ASI 2003 report concluded that the structural features — particularly the symmetrical pillar bases arrayed in a way consistent with a pillared temple hall, the north–south wall consistent with temple architecture, and recovered architectural fragments including amalaka stones and lotus medallions — were consistent with a pre-existing temple structure. A minority position (signed by some historians at the time of the report's release, e.g., the "Historians' Report to the Nation" 1991 by Romila Thapar, R. S. Sharma, M. Athar Ali, D. N. Jha, and Suraj Bhan) was that the structural evidence was insufficient to identify the prior structure as a Hindu temple specifically. The Supreme Court's 2019 judgment evaluated this dispute and concluded that the ASI report's findings were valid evidence of a pre-existing temple structure beneath the mosque (Judgment §786–§800).

3. **"Pre-British Muslim sources describe the mosque as built on the Janmasthan temple."** — **Supported.** Four pre-British or early-British Muslim sources independently describe the Babri Masjid as having been constructed on a temple identified as the Janmasthan: (i) Mirza Jan's *Hadiqah-i Shuhada* (1856, Lucknow) describes the mosque as built on the Janmasthan temple under the patronage of Sayyid Musa Ashiqan in 923 AH (1528 CE); (ii) Muhammad Ashghar, the Khatib (preacher) of the Babri Masjid itself, in an 1858 application to the Deputy Commissioner of Faizabad, refers to the mosque as "masjid-i Janmasthan" and the courtyard as "maqam Janmasthan ka"; (iii) Shykh Azamat Ali Kakorawi Nami's *Muraqqah-i Khusrawi* (also called *Tarikh-i Avadh*), completed 1869, states the mosque was "built up in 923 AH in the Janmasthan temple in Faizabad-Avadh, which was a great place of worship and capital of Rama's father"; (iv) Mirza Rajab Ali Beg Surur's *Fasanah-i Ibrat* describes "a great mosque was built on the spot where Sita ki Rasoi is situated... The mosque was built in 923 AH under the patronage of Sayyid Mir Ashiqan." These are pre-controversy Muslim sources; they do not have a motive to support a Hindu claim. The convergence of four independent Muslim-internal sources is evidentially significant.

4. **"The Supreme Court of India's 2019 judgment found that the site had been used for Hindu worship and accepted the ASI's finding of a pre-existing temple structure."** — **Supported (this is documented legal record).** The 2019 judgment (M Siddiq v. Mahant Suresh Das, decided 9 November 2019, 1045 pages) found that (a) the ASI report's identification of a pre-existing temple structure was valid evidence; (b) Hindu worship at the site had continued in some form throughout the disputed period; (c) the Hindu plaintiffs' claim to the inner courtyard was supported; (d) the Muslim plaintiffs were entitled to an alternative 5-acre plot for mosque construction. The court did not adjudicate the devotional question of whether Rama was born at the site (it explicitly declined to do so), but did find on the historical-archaeological question that a pre-existing temple structure had been there.

5. **"Rama was born specifically at this physical location in Ayodhya."** — **Outside the scope of secular historical inquiry.** This is a devotional claim grounded in Hindu religious tradition. Hindu textual tradition (the Valmiki Ramayana, the Skanda Purana's Ayodhya Mahatmya) identifies Ayodhya as Rama's birthplace; the specific physical location within Ayodhya identified as the Janmasthan rests on continuous Hindu pilgrimage tradition documented from at least the medieval period. The secular historical question can address whether Hindus *believed* Rama was born at this site (yes, demonstrably, for many centuries); it cannot adjudicate whether that belief is metaphysically correct, because the secular historical method does not address claims of divine incarnation in specific physical locations. The Supreme Court's 2019 judgment explicitly distinguished the legal-historical question (which it addressed) from the devotional question (which it declined to adjudicate). Articles should preserve this distinction.

6. **"The mosque was constructed by Mir Baqi under Babur's patronage in 1528 CE / 935 AH (some sources say 923 AH / 1528 CE)."** — **Supported.** The Babri Masjid's foundation inscription (now lost but well-documented in 19th- and early 20th-century surveys) named Mir Baqi as the builder and dated the construction to 935 AH (corresponding to 1528–29 CE) under Babur's reign. The four pre-British Muslim sources give the date variously as 923 AH (1517 CE) or 935 AH; the discrepancy reflects oral tradition vs. inscription, with 935 AH (the inscription's date) being the more reliable.

## Evidence for

- **Archaeological Survey of India, *Ayodhya 2002–03: Excavations at the Disputed Site (Ayodhya)*, submitted to the Allahabad High Court 2003**: a 574-page report on a court-directed excavation conducted with observers from all parties present. Documents pillar bases in regular pattern, north–south wall, structural floors, and recovered architectural fragments. The report's conclusion that the structural features were consistent with a pre-existing temple structure was upheld by the Supreme Court in 2019.
- **The four pre-British Muslim sources** compiled by Harsh Narain in 1990 and subsequently incorporated into the legal record:
  - Mirza Jan, *Hadiqah-i Shuhada*, Lucknow 1856 — eyewitness Persian/Urdu account of the 1855 Hanuman Garhi episode, refers to the Babri Masjid as built on the Janmasthan temple.
  - Muhammad Ashghar (Khatib of the Babri Masjid itself), application to the Deputy Commissioner of Faizabad, 30 November 1858 — the Khatib's own internal Muslim source-language reference to "masjid-i Janmasthan."
  - Shykh Azamat Ali Kakorawi Nami, *Muraqqah-i Khusrawi / Tarikh-i Avadh*, completed 1869 — explicit statement that the mosque was built in the Janmasthan temple.
  - Mirza Rajab Ali Beg Surur, *Fasanah-i Ibrat* — refers to the mosque on the site of Sita ki Rasoi.
- **Supreme Court of India, M Siddiq v. Mahant Suresh Das**, decided 9 November 2019 (1045 pages): the legal record evaluating both the ASI report and the documentary evidence. The judgment is publicly available; the relevant analysis of the historical evidence is §786 through §800 (ASI report evaluation) and §610 through §750 (documentary and travelogue evidence).
- **Hans Bakker, *Ayodhya* (Egbert Forsten, Groningen 1986)**: a Dutch Indologist's pre-controversy study of Ayodhya's religious topography, documenting the city's identification as Rama's birthplace in Sanskrit pilgrimage literature from at least the early medieval period. Bakker's work — completed before the 1990s political controversy — is a useful baseline.
- **European travelers' accounts**: William Finch (1611), Joseph Tieffenthaler (1767), and several others describe a temple or temple-precinct at the Janmasthan site or note Hindu pilgrimage there, providing pre-1850s European corroboration.

## Evidence against / counterpoints

- **The "Historians' Report to the Nation" (1991)** signed by Romila Thapar, R. S. Sharma, M. Athar Ali, D. N. Jha, and Suraj Bhan, argued that the archaeological and documentary evidence available at that time was insufficient to identify a pre-existing temple beneath the Babri Masjid. This report predates the 2003 ASI excavation; its analysis is of the pre-2003 evidentiary picture. After the 2003 report, some of the same scholars (and others, including Irfan Habib) maintained that the ASI's interpretation of the structural features as temple-architecture-specific was contestable. The Supreme Court in 2019 evaluated and rejected these counter-readings.
- **The interpretive question of pillar-base patterning**: critics of the ASI report (notably Suraj Bhan, who served as one of the court-appointed observers) argued that the pillar-base patterning could indicate a non-temple structure (Eidgah, residential complex) rather than a temple specifically. The ASI majority and the Supreme Court found the temple-structure reading more compelling; this remains the contested point in the academic literature.
- **The Buddhist and Jain alternative readings**: a minority position has argued that the pre-mosque structure might have been Buddhist or Jain rather than Hindu. Recovered architectural fragments include some that could be associated with non-Hindu traditions; the majority finding (and the court's finding) is that the overall pattern points to a Hindu temple.

## Scope boundaries

- This claim is **not** addressing whether the December 1992 demolition of the Babri Masjid was legally or morally justified. That is a separate question concerning the rule of law, communal violence, and political accountability.
- This claim is **not** addressing the contemporary politics of Hindu-Muslim relations, the Ram Mandir construction (consecrated January 2024), or the Places of Worship Act 1991. Those are present-day political questions, not historical-archaeological ones.
- This claim **is** strictly addressing the historical question of what was at the site before 1528 and what pre-British Muslim sources said about that prior structure.
- This claim **is** distinguishing the secular historical question (was there a temple? — yes, the ASI report and the four Muslim sources support this) from the devotional question (was Rama born at this exact location? — outside the scope of secular historical method). Articles must preserve this distinction or they confuse two different kinds of question.
- This claim is **not** taking a partisan position on the Ayodhya dispute. It reports what the documentary, archaeological, and legal record contains — including the ASI 2003 findings, the four pre-British Muslim sources, the 2019 Supreme Court judgment, and the dissenting historians' positions.

## Reusable examples

- **The Khatib's 1858 application**: the most evidentially striking of the four Muslim sources is Muhammad Ashghar's 30 November 1858 application to the Deputy Commissioner of Faizabad. Ashghar was the Khatib (preacher) of the Babri Masjid itself — the highest-ranking Muslim religious officer at the site. His own application calls the mosque "masjid-i Janmasthan." This is not an external Hindu claim about the site; it is the Muslim site-administrator's own language. The Khatib of a mosque does not casually call his own mosque "the mosque of [a Hindu temple]" unless that identification is established usage in the community at that time.
- **Kakorawi's 1869 statement**: the Persian/Urdu text from *Muraqqah-i Khusrawi* (1869): "the Babari mosque was built up in 923 AH in the Janmasthan temple in Faizabad-Avadh, which was a great place of worship and capital of Rama's father." This is a 19th-century Indian Muslim scholar — not a 20th-century polemicist — stating the conversion as established fact and naming Ayodhya as the historical capital of Rama's father (Dasharatha).
- **The 2003 ASI report's key findings** (paraphrased from the report's summary): "The northern area of the disputed site shows a sequence of structural activity beginning in the early historical period and continuing through the medieval period. Below the structural debris of the Babri Masjid floor, a series of pillar bases arrayed in a regular pattern, a north–south wall in lime mortar, and structural floors at multiple levels were exposed. Recovered architectural fragments include amalaka stones, lotus medallions, and other temple-architecture elements. The cumulative evidence is consistent with a pre-existing temple structure dating to the 12th century, built in turn on earlier strata going back to the early historical period."
- **The 2019 Supreme Court's evidentiary framework**: the court's 1045-page judgment evaluates the ASI report, the documentary evidence (the four Muslim sources plus European travelers' accounts), and the pre-mosque Hindu worship evidence as cumulative. It explicitly distinguishes the legal-historical question (which it addresses on the basis of evidence) from the devotional question (which it declines to adjudicate). Articles handling Ayodhya should preserve this distinction.
- **The Bakker baseline**: Hans Bakker's pre-controversy *Ayodhya* (1986) documents the Janmasthan identification in Sanskrit pilgrimage literature from at least the early medieval period — useful for showing that the Hindu identification of the site as Rama Janmasthan is not a 1990s political construction but a long-standing devotional tradition.

## When this claim matters

- When a reader lands on "Babri Masjid history," "Ram Janmabhoomi," "Ayodhya dispute," "ASI Babri Masjid," or "was there a temple at Ayodhya."
- When writing about Hindu pilgrimage sites, Rama traditions, the Ayodhya region, Mughal-era mosque construction, or the contemporary Ram Mandir.
- When the temptation is either to inflate (presenting the temple identification as a closed-and-shut case with no scholarly disagreement) or to deflect (presenting the temple identification as a 1990s political fabrication with no documentary basis). The historical record supports the temple identification on the balance of evidence — the ASI 2003 report, the four pre-British Muslim sources, and the 2019 Supreme Court judgment converge — but the academic literature includes dissenting positions whose existence should be acknowledged.
- When the secular historical question is being conflated with the devotional question (was Rama actually born here?). Articles must keep these separate to maintain credibility on either.
- This is the single most politically charged claim in the temple-destruction set; the load-bearing discipline is strict primary-source citation, explicit decomposition of sub-questions, and preservation of the historical/devotional distinction.
