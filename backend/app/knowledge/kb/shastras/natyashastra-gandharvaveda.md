## Territory

**Natyashastra** ("the science of drama/theatre") and **Gandharvaveda** ("the Veda of music") are two names for the classical Indian tradition of **performing arts**: theatre, dance, music, stagecraft, costume, aesthetics, and the theoretical underpinning of all of these. Gandharvaveda is the Upaveda classification — the tradition's place as an applied knowledge system attached to the Samaveda (the musical Veda). Natyashastra is the operational name, derived from its foundational text, **Bharata Muni's Natya Shastra**. The two terms describe the same subject matter from different angles: one classificatory (Gandharvaveda), the other text-anchored (Natyashastra).

This tradition is the classical Indian tradition's single most sophisticated contribution to **aesthetics**, and specifically to the theory of how art affects the audience. Its central theoretical achievement is **rasa theory** — a sustained, elaborated account of aesthetic emotion and its mechanisms that has remained the reference framework for classical Indian performing arts, literary theory, and aesthetics for nearly two millennia.

## Textual strata

- [Natya Shastra (Bharata Muni)](../texts/natya-shastra.md) *(not yet seeded)* — c. 200 BCE – 200 CE (composite); 36 chapters, ~6,000 verses; the foundational text of the entire tradition
- **Abhinavabharati (Abhinavagupta)** *(not yet seeded)* — c. 10th c. CE; the most important commentary on the Natya Shastra; extensively elaborates rasa theory and has been more influential on later aesthetic theory than some sections of the original
- **Dhvanyaloka (Anandavardhana)** *(not yet seeded)* — 9th c. CE; on suggestion (dhvani) in poetry; the central text of later Indian literary theory
- **Dasarupaka (Dhananjaya)** *(not yet seeded)* — 10th c. CE; a summary of Natya Shastra's dramatic theory
- **Sangita Ratnakara (Sharngadeva)** *(not yet seeded)* — 13th c. CE; the most influential classical treatise on music; bridge to the living classical music traditions
- **Nartananirnaya (Pundarika Vitthala)** *(not yet seeded)* — 16th c. CE; dance treatise

## Key figures

- [Bharata Muni](../people/bharata-muni.md) *(not yet seeded)* — the attributed author of the Natya Shastra; largely legendary; "Bharata" in the text itself may be a generic term for an actor
- **Abhinavagupta** *(not yet seeded)* — 10th–11th c. CE Kashmir; the most important theorist in the rasa tradition; his Abhinavabharati is foundational for later aesthetics; also a major Shaiva philosopher
- **Anandavardhana** *(not yet seeded)* — 9th c. CE Kashmir; articulated the theory of dhvani (poetic suggestion)
- **Sharngadeva** *(not yet seeded)* — 13th c. CE; author of Sangita Ratnakara
- **Dhananjaya** *(not yet seeded)* — 10th c. CE; author of Dasarupaka
- **Kapila Vatsyayan** *(not yet seeded)* — 20th c. modern scholar and dance historian; her work established the academic study of classical Indian performing arts
- **V. Raghavan** *(not yet seeded)* — 20th c. Sanskritist; The Number of Rasas is the definitive study of the historical debate over how many rasas there are

## Core concepts

- **Rasa** — the central concept; an aesthetic emotion evoked in the audience by a well-wrought performance. The classical list is eight (shringara/romantic, hasya/comic, karuna/pathetic, raudra/furious, vira/heroic, bhayanaka/fearful, bibhatsa/disgusting, adbhuta/marvelous), with shanta (peaceful) added by Abhinavagupta as a ninth. The debate over whether shanta is a legitimate rasa is a major chapter in later aesthetics.
- **Bhava** — the underlying emotional state that, when artistically expressed, produces rasa in the audience. Sthayi-bhava (stable emotional states) are the raw material that rasa aesthetically transforms.
- **Vibhava, anubhava, vyabhichari-bhava** — the three-part mechanism: vibhava (causes/stimuli of the emotion), anubhava (its bodily expression by the actor), and vyabhichari-bhava (transient emotions). The combination of these produces rasa in the audience.
- **Dhvani** — "suggestion" in poetry; the capacity of language to evoke meanings beyond its literal content; Anandavardhana's development is the central move of later Indian literary theory
- **Abhinaya** — the four modes of expression: angika (bodily), vachika (vocal), aharya (costume/makeup), sattvika (psycho-physical/emotional truth)
- **Natya, nritta, nritya** — the distinction between dramatic performance (natya, with narrative), pure dance (nritta, rhythmic and abstract), and expressive dance (nritya, narrative through movement)
- **Tala and raga** — the foundational rhythmic and melodic categories of classical Indian music; the Natya Shastra and Sangita Ratnakara together lay out the classical system

## Live debates

- **Dating Bharata**: the Natya Shastra is a composite text and its dating is uncertain. Most scholars place the core between ~200 BCE and ~200 CE, with later accretions. "Bharata Muni" is a traditional designation rather than an attested historical author.
- **The rasa debate**: how many rasas are there? Bharata lists eight; Abhinavagupta adds shanta; some later traditions add more (bhakti-rasa, vatsalya-rasa). V. Raghavan's 1940 monograph is the canonical treatment.
- **What does rasa actually refer to?** Is it an emotion in the audience, a feature of the artwork, a transformed mental state, or a mode of cognition? Abhinavagupta's view — that rasa is a distinctive kind of aesthetic experience, neither ordinary emotion nor ordinary cognition — remains influential.
- **Living tradition vs. textual tradition**: the great classical Indian performing arts (Bharatanatyam, Odissi, Kuchipudi, Kathakali, the Hindustani and Carnatic musical traditions) are living traditions that trace themselves to the Natya Shastra but have evolved substantially over the centuries. The relationship between the text and the living practice is always a question of interpretation and reconstruction.

## Common misreadings

- **"Bharatanatyam is 5,000 years old."** The name "Bharatanatyam" in its modern form was coined in the 20th century; the tradition it codifies has classical temple-dance roots but was reformulated and reshaped in the early 20th c. by Rukmini Devi Arundale and others. The Natya Shastra is ~2,000 years old; specific living dance forms are much younger in their current codifications.
- **"Rasa theory is just aesthetics."** Rasa theory is aesthetics, but it is also philosophy of mind, theory of emotion, and theory of artistic cognition in ways modern "aesthetics" doesn't always capture. It has been engaged seriously by modern philosophers (Sheldon Pollock, Arindam Chakrabarti, Daniel Meyer-Dinkgräfe).
- **"Ancient Indians invented all the dramatic categories."** The Natya Shastra's categories are sophisticated but were developed in dialogue with an existing tradition of performance and literature. Calling them "first invented by Bharata" over-reads the evidence.
- **"Rasa and modern emotions are the same thing."** Modern psychological theories of emotion (Ekman's basic emotions, Russell's dimensional models) have real points of contact with rasa theory but are not equivalent. Rasa is a theory of aesthetic *transformation* of emotion, not a list of emotional categories.

## Modern bridges

- **Philosophy of emotion and aesthetics**: rasa theory is a sophisticated classical framework for understanding how art affects the audience. It has been brought into productive dialogue with modern philosophy of emotion, drama theory, and film theory by scholars including Sheldon Pollock, Daniel Meyer-Dinkgräfe, and Arindam Chakrabarti.
- **Performance studies**: the Natya Shastra is now studied seriously in Western performance-studies departments alongside Aristotle's Poetics and classical Chinese drama theory as one of the three great premodern dramatic theories.
- **Living classical music and dance**: Bharatanatyam, Odissi, Kuchipudi, Kathakali, Mohiniyattam, Manipuri, Kathak, and the Hindustani and Carnatic musical traditions all trace themselves to this tradition and continue to evolve. The connection to the text is real; the nature of that connection is a living question.
