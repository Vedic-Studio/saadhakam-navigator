## Territory

**Dharmashastra** is the classical Indian tradition of ethical, legal, and social prescription. It is a Smriti-level literature that codifies norms for individual conduct (*achara*), legal adjudication (*vyavahara*), and expiation (*prayashchitta*), and it functioned as the theoretical basis of classical Indian legal practice for roughly two millennia. The tradition has two clear historical layers: the earlier **Dharma Sutras** (c. 600–200 BCE, in terse aphoristic prose) and the later **Dharma Shastras** (c. 200 BCE – 1000 CE, in verse, more elaborated and systematic). The **Manusmriti** (Manava Dharmashastra) is the most famous and influential work in the second layer and is the focal point of most modern debate, both scholarly and political.

Dharmashastra is also the tradition within which classical Indian **moral and political philosophy** is articulated. Its relationship to Arthashastra (political-economic statecraft) is complementary but distinct: dharmashastra is primarily normative-prescriptive, arthashastra primarily instrumental-prudential.

## Textual strata

### Dharma Sutras (the earlier layer, ~600–200 BCE)
- **Apastamba Dharma Sutra** *(not yet seeded)* — possibly the earliest; part of the Apastamba Kalpa Sutra
- **Gautama Dharma Sutra** *(not yet seeded)* — brief and often cited as the earliest by Indian tradition
- **Baudhayana Dharma Sutra** *(not yet seeded)*
- **Vasishtha Dharma Sutra** *(not yet seeded)*

### Dharma Shastras (the later verse layer, ~200 BCE – 1000 CE)
- [Manusmriti (Manava Dharmashastra)](../texts/manusmriti.md) — the most famous and most translated; Olivelle's 2005 Oxford critical edition is the current standard
- **Yajnavalkya Smriti** *(not yet seeded)* — shorter, more systematic, and historically more important in actual legal practice than Manusmriti
- **Narada Smriti** *(not yet seeded)* — the most procedurally sophisticated on civil and criminal law
- **Parashara Smriti** *(not yet seeded)* — treated as authoritative for the Kali Yuga in some medieval traditions
- **Brihaspati Smriti** *(not yet seeded)* — reconstructed; procedural law

### Nibandhas (medieval commentarial digests, ~1000–1700 CE)
- **Mitakshara (Vijnaneshvara, 11th c.)** *(not yet seeded)* — commentary on Yajnavalkya; basis of one of the two major schools of classical Hindu law
- **Dayabhaga (Jimutavahana, 12th c.)** *(not yet seeded)* — Bengal school; basis of inheritance law in Bengal until modern reforms
- **Vivadaratnakara, Vyavaharamayukha, Dharmasindhu** *(not yet seeded)* — the major late-medieval digests

## Core concepts

- **Dharma** — the central concept; context-dependent, meaning (depending on context) duty, law, virtue, religious prescription, right conduct. Dharmashastra specifically treats dharma as that which is prescribed by shruti and smriti for a given person in a given context.
- **Varna** — the four broad social classes (brahmana, kshatriya, vaishya, shudra); distinct from the thousands of historical jatis (castes) which are a different social reality
- **Ashrama** — the four life-stages (brahmacharya/student, grhastha/householder, vanaprastha/forest-dweller, sannyasa/renunciate)
- **Varnashrama-dharma** — the combined framework assigning duties by varna and ashrama; the ideal-typical social structure assumed by dharmashastra
- **Achara** (conduct), **vyavahara** (legal procedure), **prayashchitta** (expiation) — the three books of a typical dharmashastra
- **Apaddharma** (emergency dharma) — the explicit framework for when ordinary rules are suspended or modified in emergencies
- **Source hierarchy** — shruti > smriti > sadachara (conduct of the good) > atmasantushti (inner satisfaction); the classical order for resolving conflicts

## Live debates

- **The Manusmriti problem**: the text contains prescriptions on caste, women, and outcastes that are morally and politically explosive in modern contexts. The scholarly debate is about (a) how representative the text was of actual classical practice, (b) how much of it reflects normative ideal vs. contested live debate within the tradition, (c) how it should be read today. See Olivelle's introduction to his 2005 edition for the mainstream scholarly framing.
- **Is dharmashastra "law"?** There is a live scholarly question about whether dharmashastra is better characterized as prescriptive ethics, as religious law, or as a theoretical/philosophical tradition about right conduct that was applied in varying ways in actual legal practice. Donald Davis Jr.'s work (The Spirit of Hindu Law) is the best recent treatment.
- **Relationship to actual classical legal practice**: classical Indian courts (in inscriptional and documentary evidence) did not simply apply Manusmriti mechanically; they drew on local custom, royal edict, and community norms alongside dharmashastra. The gap between text and practice is substantial.
- **"Hindu law" as a colonial construct**: the British colonial legal system classified Indian personal law into "Hindu law" and "Muslim law" and used dharmashastra (especially Mitakshara and Dayabhaga) to derive "Hindu law." This colonial legal construct is distinct from classical dharmashastra's self-understanding; several modern scholars (Derrett, Cohn, Davis) have traced how "Hindu law" was partly invented by colonial legal practice.

## Common misreadings

- **"Manusmriti is Hindu law."** Manusmriti is one dharmashastra text among many, and in actual classical legal practice Yajnavalkya (via Mitakshara) was often more influential. Treating Manusmriti as "the" Hindu legal code is a modern distortion, partly inherited from colonial legal history.
- **"The varna system is the Indian caste system."** Varna (four classes) and jati (thousands of endogamous communities) are related but distinct. Dharmashastra's varna framework is an idealized classification; the actual lived caste system is a much more complex social formation that dharmashastra does not fully describe.
- **"Manusmriti was written by one person named Manu."** The text is a composite redaction, likely compiled between ~200 BCE and ~300 CE. "Manu" is a mythic lawgiver figure, not a historical author.
- **"Dharmashastra prescribes sati, child marriage, and caste rigidity."** The texts contain a range of positions, many of them contested within the tradition. See the [sati scriptural basis claim](../claims/sati-scriptural-basis-claim.md) for the sati-specific decomposition. Simple "the texts prescribe X" claims usually over-read.

## Modern bridges

- **Comparative legal history**: dharmashastra is one of the great premodern legal traditions and sits alongside Roman law, Islamic fiqh, and Chinese law as a major civilizational legal heritage.
- **Philosophy of law**: dharmashastra's treatment of rules, exceptions, apaddharma, and the source hierarchy is a sophisticated theoretical apparatus that analytic legal philosophers have begun to engage with.
- **Modern Indian personal law**: classical dharmashastra's influence persists in Indian personal law (Hindu Marriage Act, Hindu Succession Act, etc.), though in heavily modified and modernized form.
- **Ethics and moral philosophy**: dharma as a central ethical concept has been in sustained dialogue with Western virtue ethics, deontology, and consequentialism in modern comparative philosophy.
