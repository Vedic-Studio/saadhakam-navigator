## Territory

Indian political thought has its canonical monument in the **Arthashastra**, a layered treatise traditionally attributed to **Kautilya** (also called Chanakya or Vishnugupta). A deep-research approach to the tradition does two things at once: it reads statecraft as *content* (governance, economy, intelligence, war ethics), and it treats the text's **composition history** as itself part of the story — because the received text contains layers and redactions spanning centuries.

Statecraft sits alongside dharmashastra (law) in the larger apparatus of governance, but where dharmashastra is normative and religious, arthashastra is technical and pragmatic: it aims to tell a ruler how to keep the state functioning, its people fed, its treasury solvent, and its enemies uncertain.

## Textual strata

- [Arthashastra (Kautilya)](../texts/arthashastra.md) — the layered treatise itself, with composition ranging approximately from the 3rd c. BCE to the 3rd c. CE
- **Manusmriti** *(not yet seeded)* — the most famous dharmashastra, partly complementary and partly in tension with the Arthashastra
- **Nitisara (Kamandaki)** *(not yet seeded)* — a later verse treatise on statecraft that depends on the Arthashastra tradition

## Key figures

- [Kautilya](../people/kautilya.md) — the attributed author, historically identified with Chandragupta Maurya's minister; the received text is likely a composite tradition
- [R. Shamasastry](../people/shamasastry.md) — the librarian-scholar in Mysore who rediscovered a manuscript in 1905 and published the Sanskrit edition (1909) and English translation (1915)
- [Patrick Olivelle](../people/olivelle.md) — modern scholar whose 2013 translation and study is the current reference edition in English
- **Kaushik Roy** *(not yet seeded)* — modern historian of warfare ethics in South Asia

## Core concepts

- [Dharmayuddha vs Kutayuddha](../concepts/dharmayuddha-vs-kutayuddha.md) — just war vs pragmatic/strategic war, the long-running debate that frames Indian military thought
- **Mandala theory** — the concentric-circles model of interstate relations (your neighbor is your enemy, your neighbor's neighbor is your ally)
- **Saptanga** — the seven constituent elements of the state (king, minister, country, fort, treasury, army, allies)
- **Dandaniti** — the science of governance/punishment; the broader discipline in which arthashastra sits

## Live debates

- **Who is "Kautilya" historically?** The received text names him; but the layered composition history means "Kautilya wrote the Arthashastra" is a compressed statement about a complex tradition. Treat the authorship question as live.
- **What is the relationship between Arthashastra pragmatism and Dharmashastra normativity?** Modern scholarship (Olivelle and others) treats the two as a real debate inside the tradition, not as a single doctrine.
- **How much of the received text is pre-Mauryan, Mauryan, or later?** This is an open philological problem.

## Common misreadings

- **"The Arthashastra is Machiavelli's The Prince, but older."** The comparison is suggestive but misleading. The Arthashastra is a *systematic* treatise covering economy, administration, law, espionage, and war, far broader in scope than The Prince, and written in a different intellectual register.
- **"Kautilya wrote every word of the Arthashastra in the 4th c. BCE."** The received text contains layers and redactions spanning centuries. Modern editions treat authorship as a composite tradition.
- **"Arthashastra is amoral realism."** It has internal ethical commitments and sits inside a tradition where dharma still matters; reading it as pure Hobbesian realism misses its context.
- **"Ancient India knew nothing about espionage."** The Arthashastra has a structural, not peripheral, treatment of intelligence: spy placement, counter-spying, testing of officials. Shamasastry's translation makes this explicit.

## Modern bridges

- **Mandala theory vs modern geopolitics**: the concentric-circles model of alliances is a recognizable early articulation of the logic that still drives much realist international relations theory. *Breaks because* the modern nation-state system is categorically different from classical Indian kingdoms.
- **Spycraft and information asymmetry**: the Arthashastra's treatment of intelligence as structural governance resonates with modern thinking about information as power. *Breaks because* modern signals intelligence and cryptographic methods are absent.
- **Administrative design**: the saptanga framework is an early articulation of thinking about the state as a system with interdependent parts. *Breaks because* modern public administration adds a massive layer of bureaucratic and legal apparatus not present in the classical framework.
