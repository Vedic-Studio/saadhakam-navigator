## Territory

**Yoga** as one of the six classical darshanas is the philosophical-meditational sibling of Samkhya: it inherits Samkhya's metaphysics (purusha/prakriti dualism, the 25 tattvas, the three gunas) and adds a disciplined practice for achieving **kaivalya** (isolation/liberation of purusha from prakriti). The foundational text is **Patanjali's Yoga Sutras** (c. 2nd c. BCE – 4th c. CE, with active scholarly debate on dating). Classical Yoga differs in some specific ways from Samkhya — notably in accepting **Ishvara** (a distinctive purusha, object of meditation but not a creator God) — and in its central contribution of a structured psychology of attention and absorption.

**Crucial distinction for modern readers**: "Yoga" in classical India means the contemplative-philosophical darshana of Patanjali. **Modern postural yoga** (asanas as the primary content of practice) is a late 19th–20th century development, largely shaped by interactions with European physical culture; the classical Yoga Sutras mention asana only briefly and primarily as a seated meditation posture. See Mark Singleton's Yoga Body (2010) for the canonical historical treatment of the divergence.

## Textual strata

- [Yoga Sutras (Patanjali)](../texts/yoga-sutras.md) *(not yet seeded)* — 196 sutras in 4 padas (Samadhi, Sadhana, Vibhuti, Kaivalya); the foundational text
- **Vyasa's Yoga Bhashya** *(not yet seeded)* — the standard early commentary, functionally inseparable from the sutras in the received tradition; some modern scholarship treats the "sutras + bhashya" as a single compositional unit (the "Patanjala Yoga Shastra")
- **Vachaspati Mishra's Tattvavaisharadi** *(not yet seeded)* — the standard sub-commentary
- **Bhoja's Rajamartanda** *(not yet seeded)* — 11th c. CE royal commentary
- **Vijnanabhikshu's Yogavarttika** *(not yet seeded)* — 16th c. CE late synthesis
- **Hatha Yoga texts (Hatha Yoga Pradipika, Gheranda Samhita, Shiva Samhita)** *(not yet seeded)* — later tradition (15th–17th c. CE) that emphasizes physical practices and is a distinct textual lineage from Patanjala Yoga

## Core concepts

- **Ashtanga (the eightfold path)** — yama (ethical restraints), niyama (observances), asana (posture), pranayama (breath control), pratyahara (sense withdrawal), dharana (concentration), dhyana (meditation), samadhi (absorption). The eightfold path is laid out in Sadhana Pada (sutras 2.29 ff.).
- **Chitta-vritti-nirodha** — Patanjali's famous opening definition of yoga (YS 1.2): "yoga is the cessation of the fluctuations of the mind." Everything else in the sutras is a development of what this means and how it is achieved.
- **Samyama** — the combined practice of dharana, dhyana, and samadhi on a single object; the technical basis of Vibhuti Pada (the third book, on siddhis/contemplative powers)
- **Kaivalya** — liberation as isolation of purusha from prakriti, exactly as in Samkhya
- **Ishvara** — Yoga's distinctive admission of a special purusha untouched by karma and samskara; object of devotional meditation (ishvara-pranidhana) but not a creator God
- **The kleshas (afflictions)** — avidya (ignorance), asmita (I-sense), raga (attachment), dvesha (aversion), abhinivesha (clinging to existence); the five root afflictions that keep the mind bound

## Live debates

- **Dating Patanjali**: proposals range from the 2nd c. BCE to the 4th c. CE. Philipp Maas argues for c. 325–425 CE based on textual analysis of the "Patanjala Yoga Shastra" (sutras + bhashya as a single composition).
- **One Patanjali or two?** Traditional identification conflates the Yoga Patanjali with the grammarian Patanjali (author of the Mahabhashya on Panini). Modern scholarship generally treats them as separate figures.
- **Did Patanjali "invent" yoga, or codify an existing tradition?** The sutras evidently synthesize older yogic material; "Patanjali's yoga" is his systematization, not the origin of the practice.
- **How physical was classical yoga?** Classical Yoga's asana is minimal (YS 2.46: "sthira-sukham asanam" — "posture is steady and comfortable"); the elaboration of asanas into a full physical practice belongs primarily to the Hatha Yoga tradition and even more so to the 20th-century modern postural yoga movement.

## Common misreadings

- **"Yoga is 5,000 years old."** The postural-physical practice most Westerners mean by "yoga" is roughly 100 years old in its current form. The contemplative discipline of Patanjala Yoga is c. 2,000 years old. Yogic practices in a loose sense reach further back into Vedic and even pre-Vedic strata, but "5,000 years" is not a dateable claim about any specific practice.
- **"Yoga = asana."** In the Yoga Sutras, asana is one of eight limbs, and the sutras devote three short verses to it. The center of classical yoga is meditation and absorption (dhyana/samadhi), not physical posture.
- **"Patanjali founded yoga."** He systematized an already-existing tradition. Yogic practices appear in the Katha, Shvetashvatara, and Maitri Upanishads before the Yoga Sutras were composed.
- **"Yoga is Hinduism's religion of the body."** Classical Yoga is a contemplative philosophy of mind and consciousness. It is Samkhya + meditation + ethical discipline, not a body-centered tradition in the modern sense.

## Modern bridges

- **Attention research and meditation neuroscience**: Patanjali's typology of focused-attention states (dharana → dhyana → samadhi) and his detailed catalog of how the mind fluctuates (chitta-vrittis) has surface parallels to contemporary attention research and contemplative neuroscience. *Breaks because* the Patanjalian framework is embedded in a purusha/prakriti metaphysics that modern neuroscience does not share.
- **Clinical meditation and mindfulness research**: the pragmatic aspects of yogic practice (samyama, ethical restraints, gradual training) have been drawn into modern clinical psychology via MBSR and related programs. *Breaks because* clinical mindfulness strips the metaphysical goal (kaivalya) and operates in a secular-clinical frame.
- **Philosophy of attention**: Patanjali's treatment of how the mind wanders, how attention is trained, and how absorption differs from concentration is a sophisticated early philosophy of attention. See work by Jonardon Ganeri (Attention, Not Self, Oxford 2017) that takes this tradition seriously in dialogue with analytic philosophy of mind.
