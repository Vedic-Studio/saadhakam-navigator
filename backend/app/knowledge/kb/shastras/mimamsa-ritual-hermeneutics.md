## Territory

**Purva Mimamsa** ("earlier inquiry"), usually called simply **Mimamsa**, is one of the six classical darshanas. Its distinctive contribution is **the philosophy of interpretation**: a rigorous, systematic theory of how to read the Vedic corpus — specifically its ritual portions (karma-kanda) — and extract determinate meaning from complex, apparently contradictory, or context-dependent injunctions. It is traditionally attributed to **Jaimini** and its foundational text is the **Mimamsa Sutras** (also called Purva Mimamsa Sutras or Dharma Sutras), a vast work of ~2,700 sutras.

The "Purva" ("earlier") label distinguishes it from **Uttara Mimamsa** ("later inquiry") = **Vedanta**, which interprets the Upanishadic/philosophical portions (jnana-kanda). Historically, the two constitute complementary halves of a single hermeneutic enterprise — Mimamsa on ritual, Vedanta on metaphysics.

Mimamsa's reputation in the modern conversation is low precisely because its subject is ritual rather than metaphysics, but its **hermeneutic methods and philosophy of language** are among the most technically sophisticated intellectual achievements of classical India, and they shaped the methodology of every other darshana and of later Indian legal reasoning.

## Core concepts

- [Apaurusheyatva (Vedic authorlessness)](../concepts/apaurusheyatva.md) — Mimamsa's striking claim that the Veda has no author (neither human nor divine); the corpus is eternal and uncreated. This is a foundational move that distinguishes Mimamsa from almost all other classical interpretation traditions worldwide.
- **Arthavada vs vidhi (descriptive statements vs. injunctions)** *(not yet seeded)* — the hermeneutic distinction between Vedic passages that *command* action and passages that merely *describe*, with elaborate rules for how the two interact
- **Dharma as that which is enjoined** *(not yet seeded)* — Mimamsa's distinctive definition of dharma: "dharma is that which is known by Vedic injunction," a strikingly narrow technical definition compared to the broader dharmashastra usage
- [Pramana (means of knowledge)](../concepts/pramana-epistemology.md) — Mimamsa accepts additional pramanas beyond the Nyaya four: arthapatti (postulation) and anupalabdhi (non-apprehension, absence)

## Live debates

- **Kumarila vs. Prabhakara**: the two Mimamsa sub-schools disagree on foundational questions (the theory of error, the number of pramanas, the mechanism of verbal meaning). The internal debate is one of the most technically dense in Indian philosophy.
- **Mimamsa vs. Buddhist epistemology**: Kumarila's attacks on Buddhist pramana theory (via Dignaga and Dharmakirti) are a major event in the history of Indian epistemology and shaped the development of Nyaya as well.
- **Is the Veda really "authorless"?** Mimamsa's defense of this claim is among the strangest and most carefully argued theses in classical Indian thought; it is connected to Mimamsa's broader view that words and meanings have an eternal, non-conventional relation.

## Common misreadings

- **"Mimamsa = old ritualism, not philosophy."** No. Mimamsa is a serious philosophical tradition with distinctive epistemology, philosophy of language, and hermeneutic methods. Its subject happens to be ritual, but its methodology is philosophy.
- **"Mimamsa is the same as Vedanta."** No. Vedanta is Uttara Mimamsa (later/Upanishadic); Purva Mimamsa is the earlier/ritual wing. They share a family resemblance and hermeneutic lineage, but their subjects and conclusions differ sharply.
- **"Mimamsa doesn't believe in God."** This is an oversimplification but has a core of truth: Mimamsa is formally silent on a creator God, and Kumarila specifically argues against the coherence of the standard arguments for a creator. Mimamsa grounds Vedic authority not in a divine author but in the Veda's authorlessness.
- **"Mimamsa hermeneutics is just Indian scholasticism."** The comparison to Western scholasticism is illuminating but risks flattening the distinctive concerns — particularly the centrality of injunctive (command) meaning and the elaborate theory of how parts of the ritual relate to the whole.

## Modern bridges

- **Philosophy of language**: Mimamsa has a fully worked-out philosophy of sentential meaning, with positions on how individual words combine into sentence-meaning, how commands differ from descriptions, and how context disambiguates. This tradition has been put in dialogue with modern semantics and pragmatics by scholars like Jonardon Ganeri and Arindam Chakrabarti. *Breaks because* the motivating concerns differ: Mimamsa is driven by the need to interpret ritual injunctions, not by the concerns of logical or natural-language semantics.
- **Legal reasoning and statutory interpretation**: Mimamsa's hermeneutic rules were explicitly used in the interpretation of classical Indian law (Dharmashastra) and influenced the methodology of classical legal reasoning. The connection to modern legal hermeneutics is real and has been studied by scholars like Donald Davis Jr. *Breaks because* modern legal interpretation has a different underlying theory of textual authority.
- **Hermeneutics as a discipline**: Mimamsa is a full hermeneutic tradition in the technical sense — a systematic philosophy of interpretation — and is a peer to Western hermeneutic traditions (Schleiermacher, Gadamer) without being derivative of them. *Breaks because* the two traditions emerged from different kinds of canonical-text problems.
