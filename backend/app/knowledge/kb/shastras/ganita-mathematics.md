## Territory

**Ganita** (literally "calculation" or "reckoning") is the name the classical Indian tradition gave to its mathematics. Unlike the Vedanga Jyotisha's calendrical arithmetic, ganita as a systematic shastra emerges in the middle of the first millennium CE and runs through the 17th century, with major centers in north India (Aryabhata, Brahmagupta) and a remarkable late flowering in the Kerala school (Madhava and his students, 14th–16th c. CE). The tradition contributed the **decimal place-value system with zero**, systematic arithmetic of negatives, sophisticated algebra (bijaganita), trigonometry (jya/kojya — the ancestors of "sine" and "cosine"), indeterminate equations (kuttaka and chakravala methods), and — in the Kerala school — **infinite series for pi, sine, cosine, and arctangent that are recognizably the groundwork for the calculus**, some 200–300 years before Newton and Leibniz.

Ganita is tied to [Jyotisha (astronomy and timekeeping)](jyotisha-astronomy.md) throughout its history: most of the major mathematicians were also astronomers, and much of their mathematical work was developed to solve astronomical problems.

## Textual strata

- [Aryabhatiya (Aryabhata)](../texts/aryabhatiya.md) — 499 CE; 4 padas (Gitika, Ganita, Kalakriya, Gola); place-value, sine tables, pi ≈ 3.1416, earth's rotation
- [Brahmasphutasiddhanta (Brahmagupta)](../texts/brahmasphutasiddhanta.md) — 628 CE; the first systematic arithmetic rules for zero and negative numbers; Brahmagupta's theorem on cyclic quadrilaterals
- **Mahasiddhanta (Aryabhata II)** — c. 950 CE, a later synthesis
- [Lilavati (Bhaskara II)](../texts/lilavati.md) — 1150 CE; the arithmetic book of the Siddhanta Shiromani; arguably the most widely used Indian math textbook ever written
- **Bijaganita (Bhaskara II)** — 1150 CE; the algebra book of the Siddhanta Shiromani; solutions of indeterminate equations, chakravala method
- **Tantrasangraha (Nilakantha Somayaji)** — 1501 CE, Kerala school; refinements of Aryabhata's planetary model
- **Yuktibhasa (Jyesthadeva)** — 1530 CE, Kerala school; written in Malayalam; preserves the derivations and "proofs" of the Madhava tradition including infinite series for pi and sine/cosine
- **Karanapaddhati (Putumana Somayaji)** — 17th c. CE, Kerala school

## Key figures

- [Aryabhata](../people/aryabhata.md) — 476–550 CE, from Kusumapura (Patna); the first named mathematician whose work survives
- [Brahmagupta](../people/brahmagupta.md) — 598–668 CE, from Bhillamala (Rajasthan); zero arithmetic, negative numbers
- **Mahavira** — 9th c. CE, Jain mathematician; author of Ganita Sara Sangraha
- **Sridhara** — 9th–10th c. CE; Trisatika and Patiganita
- [Bhaskara II](../people/bhaskara-ii.md) — 1114–1185 CE; author of the Siddhanta Shiromani (Lilavati, Bijaganita, Ganitadhyaya, Goladhyaya); the culminating figure of classical Indian mathematics
- [Madhava of Sangamagrama](../people/madhava-sangamagrama.md) — c. 1340–1425 CE, Kerala; founder of the Kerala school; his own works are mostly lost but his results are preserved in disciples' texts
- **Parameshvara, Nilakantha Somayaji, Jyesthadeva** — the Kerala school transmission lineage
- [Kim Plofker](../people/plofker.md) — modern historian; her Mathematics in India is the current standard single-volume history

## Core concepts

- **The decimal place-value system with zero** — the most consequential single contribution; the notation, the positional convention, and the treatment of zero as a number (not just a placeholder) all come together in classical Indian ganita
- **Zero as a number with arithmetic operations** — Brahmagupta in 628 CE gives explicit rules for addition, subtraction, and multiplication involving zero and negative numbers. The Bakhshali manuscript (recent Oxford carbon dating suggests 3rd–4th c. CE for at least some folios) already uses a dot-form zero as placeholder. See the [Indian zero claim](../claims/indian-zero-claim.md).
- **Trigonometric tables (jya, kojya, utkrama-jya)** — Aryabhata's sine tables (jya, from which Arabic *jiba* and thence Latin *sinus* = "sine" derives etymologically) are among the earliest systematic trigonometric tables in world mathematics
- **Indeterminate equations (kuttaka and chakravala)** — systematic methods for solving linear and quadratic indeterminate equations; the chakravala method of Jayadeva and Bhaskara II for solving Pell's equation is a major achievement, not matched in European mathematics until the 17th c.
- **Infinite series (Kerala school)** — Madhava and his school derived infinite series for pi (now called the "Madhava-Leibniz series"), sine, cosine, and arctangent; these are the recognizable groundwork of calculus, with remainder terms and convergence estimates

## Live debates

- **Did the Kerala school "invent calculus"?** The Kerala school's infinite series are genuinely calculus-like and predate Newton/Leibniz by centuries. Whether this counts as "inventing calculus" depends on what one means by calculus — specifically whether one includes the differential/integral duality and the fundamental theorem. Most historians credit the Kerala school with substantial calculus-like results without claiming they built the full differential/integral apparatus.
- **Was there transmission from Kerala to Europe?** C. K. Raju argues for direct transmission via Jesuit missionaries in Kerala in the 16th–17th c.; most historians find the evidence suggestive but not conclusive. This is a live scholarly debate.
- **The status of "proof" in Indian mathematics**: Indian mathematicians gave derivations (upapatti, yukti) and justifications, but the Euclidean axiomatic-deductive style is largely absent. Whether this means Indian math "lacked proof" (old view) or has a different, valid style of mathematical reasoning (the Chemla/Plofker/Raju position) is the central methodological debate in the field.
- **Bakhshali manuscript dating**: the 2017 Oxford carbon-14 dating suggested at least some folios date to the 3rd–4th c. CE, which would push back the documented use of the zero symbol significantly. The dating has been contested; the question is live.

## Common misreadings

- **"India invented the zero out of nothing."** The story is more textured — different ingredients (place value, the symbol, zero as number) were developed and combined over several centuries. See the [Indian zero claim](../claims/indian-zero-claim.md).
- **"The Kerala school invented calculus."** They developed calculus-like infinite series with remarkable precision. Whether they "invented calculus" depends on a definition of calculus; careful historians frame the contribution as "substantial pre-calculus work" rather than "calculus."
- **"Classical Indian mathematics had no proofs."** Indian mathematicians gave derivations and justifications (upapatti / yukti) systematically; the Yuktibhasa in particular preserves detailed derivations. The absence of Euclidean axiomatic-deductive style is not an absence of mathematical reasoning.
- **"Vedic mathematics is classical Indian mathematics."** "Vedic Mathematics" by Bharati Krishna Tirtha (published 1965) is a 20th-century compilation of mental-arithmetic shortcuts; it is not from the Vedas and is not the same thing as classical Indian ganita. This confusion is widespread and should be actively corrected.

## Modern bridges

- **History of mathematics**: classical Indian ganita is foundational for the global history of mathematics. The decimal system transmitted through Arabic mathematicians to Europe is the most consequential mathematical export of any civilization.
- **Mathematical notation**: the modern decimal place-value notation is a direct descendant of the Indian system.
- **Calculus**: the Kerala school's infinite series for pi, sine, cosine, and arctangent are recognizable pre-calculus results that would be at home in a modern analysis course.
- **Number theory**: the chakravala method and the systematic treatment of indeterminate equations are a major achievement that stands comparison with the best European number theory of the 17th and 18th c.
