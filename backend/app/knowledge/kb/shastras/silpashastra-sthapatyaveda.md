## Territory

**Silpashastra** (the "science of craft") and **Sthapatyaveda** (the "Veda of architecture") are two names for the same classical Indian tradition: architecture, sculpture, iconography, site selection, temple design, town planning, and the ritual-aesthetic geometry that underlies them. Sthapatyaveda is the Upaveda classification — the tradition's place in the larger Vedic corpus as one of the applied knowledge systems. Silpashastra is the operational/technical name used inside the texts themselves. The two terms denote the same subject matter; the choice of term depends on whether one is classifying (Sthapatyaveda) or technically describing (Silpashastra). **Vastushastra** and **Vastu Vidya** are further overlapping names emphasizing the domestic-architectural and site-selection aspects.

The tradition produced an extensive literature from roughly the mid-first millennium CE through the 17th century, with regional schools developing distinct stylistic and technical vocabularies for what became the three major temple idioms (Nagara in the north, Dravida in the south, Vesara in between). It is the classical Indian tradition within which the great stone temples — Khajuraho, Konark, Thanjavur, Meenakshi, Ellora — were conceived and executed.

## Textual strata

- **Mayamata (Maya)** *(not yet seeded)* — c. 9th–12th c. CE, south Indian; one of the two canonical Dravida-tradition texts. Dagens' IGNCA translation is the standard.
- **Manasara** *(not yet seeded)* — c. 5th–7th c. CE (uncertain); the other canonical Dravida-tradition text; Acharya's Oxford edition is the standard English reference.
- **Samarangana Sutradhara (Bhoja)** *(not yet seeded)* — 11th c. CE; attributed to King Bhoja of Dhara; broad treatise covering city planning, residential building, and even mechanical contrivances (yantras)
- **Vishvakarma Vastushastra** *(not yet seeded)* — tradition-attributed to the divine architect Vishvakarma; a generic label rather than a single text
- **Aparajitapriccha** *(not yet seeded)* — 12th c. CE, western Indian tradition
- **Shilpa Prakasha** *(not yet seeded)* — Orissan tradition; important for the Kalinga / Orissan temple idiom
- **Agni Purana and Matsya Purana** — both contain substantial sections on silpashastra alongside their primary genre content
- **Brihat Samhita (Varahamihira)** — 6th c. CE; an astronomical/omen text that also contains important early chapters on architecture

## Core concepts

- **Vastu Purusha Mandala** — the geometric-cosmological grid underlying site layout: a square diagram populated by deities (usually a 9×9 or 8×8 grid) that encodes the orientation, module system, and ritual geometry of a building. This is the central organizing concept of the tradition.
- **Ayadi shadvarga** — the six calculations determining the fitness of a building's dimensions (length, perimeter, height, yoni, etc.); an astrological-numerological framework that the builder follows
- **Tala, hasta, angula** — the proportional units (a tala is typically the length of the deity's face, used to set all other proportions); proportion is dharmic, not arbitrary
- **Shikhara, vimana, gopura, mandapa** — the named architectural components: shikhara (the tower over the sanctum, northern tradition), vimana (the Dravidian counterpart), gopura (the monumental entrance tower), mandapa (pillared hall)
- **Nagara, Dravida, Vesara** — the three major temple idioms. Nagara is the curvilinear-tower northern style; Dravida is the pyramidal-tiered southern style; Vesara is a hybrid developed in the Deccan

## Live debates

- **How prescriptive were the texts for actual practice?** Temple builders in different regions followed different schools. Some regional traditions (Kalinga, Chola, Chandela) developed their own distinct technical vocabularies that don't exactly match the canonical texts. The texts are normative-ideal; practice was regional-empirical.
- **Dating the major texts**: the Mayamata, Manasara, and Samarangana Sutradhara are all composite works whose dating is uncertain. Individual chapters may be much older than the received text.
- **Vastu in modern practice**: "vastu consultation" for modern houses and offices is a 20th–21st c. phenomenon that draws selectively on vastushastra texts. Its relationship to classical practice is tenuous.
- **Yantras / machines in Samarangana Sutradhara**: Chapter 31 of the Samarangana Sutradhara contains a description of mechanical devices including flying yantras. These have been interpreted variously as literal ancient technology (by popular authors) and as imaginative literary motifs or ritual-magical devices (by most historians). Careful reading is required.

## Common misreadings

- **"Silpashastra is feng shui."** There are loose thematic parallels (both are traditions of site selection and auspicious layout), but the underlying metaphysics, the geometric systems, and the architectural traditions are entirely independent.
- **"Vastu is an exact science."** The classical tradition presents vastu as a ritually and cosmologically grounded practice with clear rules, but its rules are not empirically testable physics. Modern claims that vastu has been scientifically validated do not hold up under scrutiny.
- **"Ancient Indians had airplanes because of Samarangana Sutradhara's yantras."** The text describes mechanical devices (including flying contraptions) in the manner of wonder-tales; most historians read these as literary-magical rather than as historical engineering. Claims that these were actual working vehicles are not supported by evidence.
- **"All Hindu temples follow the same plan."** Regional and historical variation is enormous. Khajuraho (Chandela, 10th–11th c.), Konark (Orissan, 13th c.), Thanjavur (Chola, 11th c.), and Meenakshi (Nayaka, 12th–17th c.) use radically different stylistic and technical vocabularies within the broader tradition.

## Modern bridges

- **Architecture and geometric design**: vastu purusha mandala is a sophisticated modular-geometric design system that parallels modern proportional systems (Le Corbusier's Modulor, the classical Greek orders). *Breaks because* vastu is cosmologically-ritually grounded in ways modern proportional systems are not.
- **Urban planning**: classical Indian texts on town planning (grama-vinyasa) describe systematic approaches to city layout that predate comparable European treatises. *Breaks because* the cosmological framing is essential to the system in a way modern urban planning is not.
- **Heritage conservation**: silpashastra texts are used today in the conservation and restoration of classical temples, where traditional building methods and proportional systems are still being recovered from the texts and from surviving craftspeople.
