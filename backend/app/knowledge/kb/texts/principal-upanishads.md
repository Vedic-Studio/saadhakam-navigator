## What it is

The **Principal Upanishads** (mukhya upanishads) are the classical core of Upanishadic literature — the philosophical and contemplative texts that are grouped as the **culmination of the Vedas** (Vedanta literally means "end of the Veda") and form the primary scriptural source for the Vedanta darshana. The traditional "principal" list consists of the **ten Upanishads on which Shankara wrote commentaries**: Isha, Kena, Katha, Prashna, Mundaka, Mandukya, Taittiriya, Aitareya, Chandogya, and Brihadaranyaka. Some classifications add Shvetashvatara and Kaushitaki to make a list of twelve (or thirteen).

The Upanishads are the primary source for classical Indian philosophy's central concepts: **Brahman** (ultimate reality), **atman** (self), the identity of atman and Brahman ("tat tvam asi" — "that thou art"), **karma** and **rebirth**, **moksha** (liberation), and the **mahavakyas** (the "great sayings" that become the focus of Vedanta meditation). They are shruti — revealed literature with canonical authority — and are attached to the four Vedas: Brihadaranyaka and Isha to the Yajurveda, Chandogya and Kena to the Samaveda, Aitareya and Kaushitaki to the Rigveda, Prashna and Mundaka and Mandukya to the Atharvaveda.

The Principal Upanishads are not a single work but a genre — they were composed over several centuries, in different regions, within different Vedic schools — and they reflect an enormously productive period of Indian philosophical and religious creativity in which the transition from ritual Vedism to the classical contemplative and philosophical traditions of India was being worked out.

## Textual history

**Patrick Olivelle's 1996/1998 critical editions and translations** are the current scholarly standard. He places the earliest Upanishads (the prose sections of the Brihadaranyaka and Chandogya) at around 800–600 BCE — making them roughly contemporary with the pre-Socratic philosophers in Greece and earlier than the Buddha — and the latest of the principal Upanishads (e.g., Shvetashvatara) at around 400 BCE. The "principal" categorization is classical and mostly reflects Shankara's commentarial choices; many other Upanishads exist (traditionally 108 or more, including many late sectarian Upanishads).

## Structure

Each Upanishad has its own internal structure and character; there is no single compositional plan for the group. Broadly:

**Earliest (prose) layer** (~800–600 BCE):
- **Brihadaranyaka Upanishad** — the longest and arguably the most important; contains the famous Yajnavalkya dialogues, the "neti neti" ("not this, not this") formulation, and the first clear articulation of karma and rebirth
- **Chandogya Upanishad** — second longest; contains the "tat tvam asi" teaching and the Satyakama Jabala story

**Middle (verse and prose) layer** (~600–400 BCE):
- **Taittiriya Upanishad** — the five-sheath (pancha-kosha) model of the self
- **Aitareya Upanishad** — the short Rigvedic Upanishad
- **Kaushitaki Upanishad** — another Rigvedic Upanishad
- **Kena Upanishad** — the "by whom" Upanishad, on the nature of consciousness
- **Katha Upanishad** — the dialogue between Nachiketa and Yama (Death) on the nature of atman

**Later (verse) layer** (~500–400 BCE):
- **Isha Upanishad** — the short opening Upanishad of Shankara's canonical sequence; dense and philosophically rich
- **Mundaka Upanishad** — famous for the "two birds on a tree" verse and the distinction between higher and lower knowledge (para/apara vidya)
- **Mandukya Upanishad** — the shortest principal Upanishad (12 verses); the classical source for the four states of consciousness (waking, dreaming, deep sleep, turiya) and the teaching on Om
- **Prashna Upanishad** — structured as six questions on philosophical topics
- **Shvetashvatara Upanishad** — theistic Upanishad; important for the development of classical theism within Vedanta

## Key passages

- **Brihadaranyaka 1.4.10**: "aham brahmasmi" ("I am Brahman") — one of the four mahavakyas
- **Brihadaranyaka 2.4 and 4.5**: the Yajnavalkya-Maitreyi dialogue on love, self-knowledge, and what survives at death
- **Brihadaranyaka 3.8**: the Yajnavalkya-Gargi dialogue on the nature of the imperishable (aksharam)
- **Chandogya 6.8.7**: "tat tvam asi" ("that thou art") — the mahavakya that Shankara makes the center of Advaita teaching; Uddalaka's teaching to his son Shvetaketu
- **Chandogya 3.14.1**: "sarvam khalvidam brahma" ("all this is indeed Brahman") — another of the four mahavakyas
- **Mandukya Upanishad (entire, 12 verses)**: the four states of consciousness and the analysis of Om (A-U-M-silence)
- **Katha Upanishad 1.2.23**: "This self cannot be attained by instruction, nor by intellect, nor by much learning" — a famous passage on the conditions for self-realization
- **Isha Upanishad 1**: "isha vasyam idam sarvam" ("all this is pervaded by the Lord") — the celebrated opening
- **Mundaka 3.1.1–3**: the "two birds on one tree" image — one eats, the other watches

## Modern translations worth citing

- **Olivelle, Upanisads (Oxford World's Classics 1996)** — the current best general-reader scholarly English translation
- **Olivelle, The Early Upanisads (Oxford 1998)** — the critical scholarly edition with Sanskrit text and full annotation
- **Radhakrishnan, The Principal Upanisads (1953)** — historically influential philosophical translation with extensive introduction; still cited for Radhakrishnan's own interpretive framing
- **Valerie Roebuck, The Upanisads (Penguin 2003)** — accessible general-reader translation
- **Eknath Easwaran, The Upanishads (Nilgiri Press)** — not scholarly but widely read devotional translation; useful for readers approaching the texts first time in a practitioner frame
