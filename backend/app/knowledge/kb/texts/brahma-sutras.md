## What it is

The Brahma Sutras — attributed to **Badarayana** — are the foundational systematic text of the **Vedanta darshana** ("the end/culmination of the Veda"). The work is also called Uttara Mimamsa Sutras ("later inquiry sutras," pairing with Jaimini's Purva Mimamsa), Vedanta Sutras, and Shariraka Sutras ("sutras on the embodied soul"). Its stated purpose is to systematize the teaching of the **Upanishads** about the nature of **Brahman** (ultimate reality) and the relationship between Brahman, the world, and the individual self (atman/jiva) — harmonizing the many Upanishadic passages into a coherent doctrine and refuting rival interpretations.

The Brahma Sutras are the single most commented-on text in classical Indian philosophy, because all the major Vedanta subschools — **Advaita** (Shankara), **Vishishtadvaita** (Ramanuja), **Dvaita** (Madhva), **Bhedabheda** (Bhaskara), **Shuddhadvaita** (Vallabha), **Achintya-bheda-abheda** (Chaitanya tradition) — ground their distinctive theologies in their own commentaries on these same sutras. The sutras themselves are so terse that they are often nearly unintelligible without a commentary, and different commentators read the same sutra as supporting opposite positions. **Shankara's Brahma Sutra Bhashya** (~8th c. CE) is the most influential single commentary historically.

## Textual history

The received text is a composite and its dating is disputed. The text's polemical engagement with Buddhist, Samkhya, and Vaisheshika positions places its received form at earliest in the late pre-Common Era and at latest in the mid-first millennium CE. "Badarayana" is a traditional designation and is sometimes identified with Vyasa, the legendary compiler of the Mahabharata and Vedas — this identification is traditional rather than historical.

## Structure

Four chapters (adhyayas), each with four sections (padas), for a total of 555 sutras in the received text (slight variations between commentators' numbering):
- **Samanvaya Adhyaya** (Book 1): demonstrates that all Upanishadic passages coherently teach Brahman as the cause and essence of the world — harmonizing apparently disparate texts
- **Avirodha Adhyaya** (Book 2): refutes the objections of rival schools (Samkhya, Vaisheshika, Buddhist, Jain, etc.) and shows that Vedanta doctrine contains no internal contradictions
- **Sadhana Adhyaya** (Book 3): the path of spiritual practice — the nature of the jiva, the means of knowledge, meditation on Brahman, the destiny of the soul after death
- **Phala Adhyaya** (Book 4): the fruits of realization — the liberation of the knower of Brahman, the state of the liberated soul

## Key passages

- **BS 1.1.1 (athato brahma jijnasa)**: "Now therefore the inquiry into Brahman" — the opening sutra, parallel to Mimamsa Sutras 1.1.1 ("inquiry into dharma"); establishes Vedanta as distinct inquiry
- **BS 1.1.2 (janmadyasya yatah)**: "That from which the origin etc. of this [world proceeds]" — Brahman as the cause of the world
- **BS 1.1.4 (tat tu samanvayat)**: "But that is [established] by harmonization [of scriptural texts]" — the crucial sutra establishing coordinated interpretation of the Upanishads
- **BS 2.1.14**: the passage on non-difference and difference that becomes the battleground between Advaita and qualified-nondualist readings
- **BS 4.4.22**: the final sutra on the non-return of the liberated soul

## Modern translations worth citing

- **Thibaut's Sacred Books of the East translations** (Shankara 1890–96, Ramanuja 1904) — still the standard English academic reference; Thibaut compares the major commentarial positions
- **Gambhirananda, Brahma Sutra Bhasya (1965)** — the standard Advaita Ashrama English translation of Shankara's commentary
- **Clooney, Theology After Vedanta (1993)** — sophisticated philosophical-theological engagement with the Vedanta tradition as a comparative theological resource
- For readers interested in Advaita specifically, Shankara's commentary is the indispensable reference; for Vishishtadvaita, Ramanuja's Sri Bhashya
