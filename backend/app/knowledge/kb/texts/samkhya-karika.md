## What it is

The Samkhya Karika, composed by **Ishvarakrishna** (c. 4th–5th c. CE), is the **earliest surviving systematic exposition** of the Samkhya darshana. It consists of 72 or 73 verses (karikas) in arya meter, compressing the school's metaphysics, epistemology, psychology, and soteriology into a tight, highly polished presentation. Although the Samkhya Sutras attributed to Kapila are traditionally considered foundational, the received Samkhya Sutras are a late compilation (15th c. CE); the Samkhya Karika is effectively the oldest classical Samkhya text we possess, and it is what the entire later tradition engages with.

The Karika is the reference text for Samkhya's two-principle metaphysics (purusha and prakriti), the theory of the three gunas (sattva, rajas, tamas), the evolutionary unfolding of the 23 tattvas from prakriti, the Samkhya theory of causation (satkaryavada — the effect pre-exists in the cause), and the path to liberation (kaivalya) through discriminative knowledge (viveka-khyati) distinguishing purusha from prakriti.

## Textual history

The earliest external witness is **Paramartha's Chinese translation** (c. 560 CE), which gives us a firm latest-by date and preserves a distinct early recension. The received Sanskrit text comes with a cluster of early commentaries: **Gaudapada's Samkhya Karika Bhashya** (~6th c., distinct from the Advaita Gaudapada), **Yuktidipika** (~7th–8th c., the richest early commentary), **Vachaspati Mishra's Samkhya Tattva Kaumudi** (~9th c., the standard later-medieval exposition), and others. The Yuktidipika and Tattva Kaumudi are the two commentaries most actively used by modern scholarship.

## Structure

The 72 (or 73) verses proceed in roughly this order:
1. **Opening frame** (1–3): the problem of suffering and the inadequacy of ordinary means; the promise of discriminative knowledge
2. **Epistemology** (4–8): the three valid means of knowledge (perception, inference, verbal testimony)
3. **The two principles** (9–14): purusha (pure consciousness) and prakriti (primal nature); the three gunas
4. **The tattva enumeration** (15–38): the evolutionary sequence from prakriti through buddhi (intellect), ahamkara (ego-sense), the eleven sense-faculties, the five subtle elements (tanmatras), and the five gross elements (mahabhutas) — 23 + 2 = 25 tattvas total
5. **Psychology and the path** (39–68): how the mind-body complex functions, the conditions for the body's dissolution, discriminative knowledge, and kaivalya
6. **Transmission frame** (69–72): the lineage in which the teaching has come down

## Key passages

- **SK 1–3**: the opening statement of the problem of suffering (duhkha-traya — the threefold suffering) and the promise of discriminative knowledge as the means to its end
- **SK 9 (satkaryavada)**: "the effect exists before the operation of its cause" — the Samkhya theory of causation, contrasted with Nyaya-Vaisheshika's asatkaryavada
- **SK 11–12**: the characterization of prakriti and the three gunas — sattva (illumination/lightness), rajas (activity/passion), tamas (inertia/darkness)
- **SK 19–20**: purusha as pure consciousness, witnessing, inactive, solitary — not the agent of action, not involved in transformation
- **SK 21–22**: the famous lame-and-blind analogy — purusha (lame but seeing) and prakriti (blind but moving) cooperate for liberation
- **SK 64–68**: the discriminative knowledge (viveka-khyati) that culminates in kaivalya
