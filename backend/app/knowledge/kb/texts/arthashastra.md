## What it is

The Arthashastra is a systematic treatise on statecraft, economics, law, administration, intelligence, and war. It is traditionally attributed to **Kautilya** (also called Chanakya or Vishnugupta), historically identified with the minister of Chandragupta Maurya. The scope is much broader than what the "Indian Machiavelli" comparison suggests — it is closer to a *complete manual of government* than to a short prince-instruction tract.

## Textual history

For centuries the Arthashastra was essentially lost — known only through references in other texts. In **1905**, a librarian-scholar in Mysore named [R. Shamasastry](../people/shamasastry.md) discovered a manuscript and subsequently published the Sanskrit edition (1909) and an English translation (1915). This rediscovery is one of the genuinely dramatic episodes in modern Indology and is a strong narrative hook for content.

The received text has been extensively studied for layered composition. Modern scholarship treats it as a composite tradition with material ranging roughly from the 3rd c. BCE to the 3rd c. CE, with Kautilya functioning as the tradition's attributed founder rather than as the single author of every word.

## Dating debate

- **Traditional view**: Kautilya wrote it in the 4th c. BCE during Chandragupta Maurya's reign.
- **Modern philological view**: composite tradition spanning several centuries; the attribution to Kautilya is a compression of the tradition's actual complexity.
- Olivelle's 2013 edition synthesizes the current state of the question.

## Structure

- 15 books (adhikaranas), 150 chapters
- Covers: education of the prince, duties of officials, civil and criminal law, economic administration, foreign policy and interstate relations, war and strategy, secret operations
- Systematic treatment of **spies, intelligence, and counter-intelligence** as a structural component of governance

## Key passages

- **Book 1 (Vinayadhikarika)** — training of the king, duties of ministers, *use of spies*
- **Book 2 (Adhyakshaprachara)** — administration of various government departments
- **Book 6–7** — the mandala theory of interstate relations
- **Book 10** — war and strategy
- **Book 13** — siegecraft and special operations

## Modern translations worth citing

- **Patrick Olivelle, King, Governance, and Law in Ancient India (Oxford 2013)** — the current modern reference translation in English
- **R. Shamasastry's 1915 translation** — tag as *early translation*; historically important as the first full English rendering and still widely cited, particularly for its explicit treatment of intelligence passages
- **R.P. Kangle, The Kautiliya Arthasastra (three volumes, 1965–72)** — the mid-20th century scholarly edition and translation bridging Shamasastry and Olivelle
