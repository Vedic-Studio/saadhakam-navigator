## What it is

The Mimamsa Sutras — attributed to **Jaimini** — are the foundational text of **Purva Mimamsa** ("earlier inquiry"), the Vedic exegetical and ritual-hermeneutical darshana. The work articulates the system's core project: working out the precise meaning and ritual application of Vedic injunctions (codana/vidhi), developing a sophisticated theory of how language conveys commands, defending the eternality and authorlessness (apaurusheyatva) of the Veda, and establishing the interpretive rules by which apparently conflicting or ambiguous Vedic statements are reconciled.

Purva Mimamsa is less famous outside India than Vedanta but was historically one of the dominant intellectual traditions — the source of most classical Indian hermeneutics, much of dharmashastric interpretive method, and a substantial portion of Sanskrit philosophy of language. Its two great classical commentators, **Kumarila Bhatta** and **Prabhakara** (both ~7th c. CE), founded distinct sub-schools that shaped later Indian epistemology and philosophy of language.

## Textual history

The sutras themselves are terse; the school's classical form is in **Shabara's Bhashya** (~4th–5th c. CE), which is the controlling commentary. Kumarila's **Shlokavarttika, Tantravarttika, and Tuptika** (~7th c.) and Prabhakara's **Brihati** (~7th c.) are the two great sub-school expositions. The Mimamsa tradition reads as a continuous conversation between the sutras, Shabara's Bhashya, and these two commentators.

## Structure

Twelve chapters (adhyayas), each divided into four sections (padas) of four topics (adhikaranas) each — the densest formal architecture of any classical Indian philosophical text. The chapters cover:
- Source hierarchy and the authority of the Veda
- Technical vocabulary and interpretive principles
- The application of injunctions
- Subsidiary rites and their relation to principal rites
- Substitution rules when ritual items are unavailable
- Sequence of performance
- Transference of ritual elements between contexts
- The sacrificial fire and its details

## Key passages

- **Mimamsa Sutra 1.1.1 (athato dharma-jijnasa)**: "Now therefore the inquiry into dharma" — parallel opening to the Brahma Sutras' "inquiry into Brahman"
- **Mimamsa Sutra 1.1.2**: "Dharma is that which is indicated by Vedic injunction (codana-lakshana)" — the school's distinctive definition of dharma as injunction-indicated
- **The apaurusheyatva argument (1.1.5 ff.)**: the classical defense of the Veda as authorless and eternal
- **The bhavana theory of meaning**: Mimamsa's theory of how injunctive language conveys the motivation to act, developed systematically by Kumarila and Prabhakara
- **The six means of knowledge**: Mimamsa (especially in Kumarila's version) recognizes six pramanas — the four of Nyaya plus presumption (arthapatti) and non-apprehension (anupalabdhi)

## Modern translations worth citing

- **Ganganatha Jha's Shabara-Bhashya** (3 vols., 1933–36) — the classic English translation of the primary commentary; still standard
- **Francis X. Clooney, Thinking Ritually** (1990) — the best philosophical introduction to what Mimamsa is actually doing
- **Elisa Freschi, Duty, Language, and Exegesis in Prabhakara Mimamsa** (2012) — excellent modern engagement with Prabhakara's theory of meaning
- **Kei Kataoka, Kumarila on Truth, Omniscience, and Killing** (2011) — sophisticated modern scholarship on Kumarila
