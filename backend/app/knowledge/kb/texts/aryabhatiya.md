## What it is

The Aryabhatiya is the foundational text of the classical Indian tradition of **mathematical astronomy** — the tradition of siddhanta astronomy that dominates Indian astronomy from the 5th c. CE through the 17th c. Composed by **Aryabhata** in 499 CE (at age 23, as the text says), it is a short work — just 121 verses — but densely packed with mathematics, trigonometry, planetary theory, and the theory of eclipses. The text is remarkable for its compression: the material that later astronomers like Brahmagupta, Bhaskara II, and the Kerala school would elaborate over centuries is often already present in seed form in the Aryabhatiya.

The text has several **distinctive and historically important** features. Aryabhata argues that the earth rotates on its axis (producing the apparent motion of the stars), gives a remarkable value for pi (62832/20000 = 3.1416), provides the earliest surviving sine table in Indian mathematics (with sine values computed at 3.75° intervals), and presents mathematical methods — including the kuttaka method for solving linear indeterminate equations and a recursive formula for computing sine values — that would be foundational for later ganita.

## Textual history

The text is well-preserved. The most important early commentary is **Bhaskara I's Aryabhatiya Bhashya** (~629 CE, distinct from the much later Bhaskara II), which is essential for understanding the algorithms. Other important commentaries include **Somesvara's** (~11th c.) and **Nilakantha Somayaji's** (~1500 CE, Kerala school) — the Kerala school continued to engage with the Aryabhatiya well into the second millennium.

The **Shukla-Sarma INSA edition (1976)** is the current scholarly standard: critical Sanskrit text, English translation, and extensive mathematical-astronomical commentary by two of the major 20th-century scholars of Indian astronomy.

## Structure

Four chapters (padas), 121 verses total:
- **Gitika Pada** (13 verses): cosmological preliminaries, a table of trigonometric and astronomical parameters, and the alphabetical numerical notation Aryabhata uses to encode large numbers compactly
- **Ganita Pada** (33 verses): the **mathematics** chapter — place-value arithmetic, square and cube roots, areas and volumes, trigonometry, the sine table, quadratic equations, interest problems, and the kuttaka method for linear indeterminate equations
- **Kalakriya Pada** (25 verses): time units, the calendar, the cycle of the yugas, mean planetary positions, and the equation of time
- **Gola Pada** (50 verses): spherical astronomy — the celestial sphere, the motions of the sun, moon, and planets, the theory of eclipses, and observational geometry

## Key passages

- **Aryabhatiya, Ganita Pada, verse 10**: the famous pi value — "Add four to one hundred, multiply by eight, and add sixty-two thousand; the result is the approximate circumference of a circle of diameter twenty thousand" — i.e., pi ≈ 3.1416, explicitly noted as **approximate (asanna)**, which is scientifically interesting as an acknowledgment of irrationality
- **Aryabhatiya, Ganita Pada, verses 11–12**: the **recursive sine-difference formula** that generates the sine table
- **Aryabhatiya, Ganita Pada, verses 32–33**: the **kuttaka method** — the earliest systematic treatment of solving linear indeterminate equations of the form ax + by = c (related to what Europeans would later call the extended Euclidean algorithm)
- **Aryabhatiya, Gola Pada, verse 9**: the celebrated earth-rotation passage — "like a person in a moving boat sees the unmoving trees on the bank moving in the opposite direction, so from Lanka (the equator) a person sees the unmoving asterisms moving uniformly westward"
- **Aryabhatiya, Gitika Pada, verse 1**: the invocation of Brahma — setting Aryabhata's work explicitly within a religious-cosmological frame, even though the mathematical content is thoroughly secular in character
