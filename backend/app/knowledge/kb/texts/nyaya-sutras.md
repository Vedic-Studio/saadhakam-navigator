## What it is

The Nyaya Sutras are the foundational text of the **Nyaya darshana** — classical Indian logic, epistemology, and the theory of rational debate. Attributed to **Gautama** (also called **Akshapada** — "eye-footed," a traditional epithet), the sutras articulate the school's four means of knowledge (pramanas: perception, inference, analogy, testimony), its five-membered inference schema (pratijna, hetu, udaharana, upanaya, nigamana), its categories of debate (vada, jalpa, vitanda), and the fallacies that disqualify an argument (hetvabhasa). The text is the starting point for two millennia of continuous logical and epistemological inquiry in the Indian tradition.

The sutras themselves are terse; the school's mature philosophical expression is in **Vatsyayana's Nyaya Bhashya** (~450 CE), followed by **Uddyotakara's Nyaya Varttika** (~6th c.), **Vachaspati Mishra's Tatparya Tika** (~9th c.), and eventually the radical reformulation in **Gangesha's Tattvachintamani** (13th c.) that inaugurates Navya-Nyaya ("New Nyaya").

## Textual history

The received text is a composite work. Modern scholarship treats it as reaching its received form between roughly 200 BCE and 200 CE, with later chapters likely added in stages. The text cannot be read fruitfully without Vatsyayana's Bhashya, which is the controlling commentary on which later tradition depends. The Dasti-Phillips Hackett edition (2017) provides a scholarly English translation of the sutras with selections from the early commentaries.

## Structure

Five books (adhyayas), each divided into two daily lessons (ahnikas):
- **Book 1**: the sixteen topics (padarthas) of Nyaya — pramana, prameya (objects of knowledge), samshaya (doubt), prayojana (purpose), etc.; and the definitions of the four pramanas
- **Book 2**: extended treatment of perception, inference, and the reality of the external world
- **Book 3**: the self (atman), body, sense organs, and the problem of embodiment
- **Book 4**: suffering, liberation (apavarga), and the refutation of rival schools (Buddhists, Samkhya, Vaisheshika where they differ)
- **Book 5**: fallacies (hetvabhasa), quibbles (chala), false rejoinders (jati), and disqualification points (nigrahasthana) — the technical apparatus of debate

## Key passages

- **Nyaya Sutra 1.1.1**: opens with the list of sixteen topics whose right understanding produces liberation — the school's framing move
- **Pramana definitions (1.1.3–8)**: formal definitions of perception, inference, analogy, and verbal testimony as the four valid means of knowledge
- **Five-membered inference schema (1.1.32–39)**: the classical Nyaya inference form — thesis, reason, example, application, conclusion
- **Debate categories (1.2.1–3)**: vada (honest debate for truth), jalpa (debate for victory with any valid moves), vitanda (destructive debate with no positive thesis)
- **The self as knower (3.1.1–ff)**: the arguments for the existence of atman as the bearer of cognitive states, against Buddhist no-self positions

## Modern translations worth citing

- **Dasti and Phillips, The Nyaya-sutra (Hackett 2017)** — the current best English translation with selections from early commentaries
- **Matilal, The Character of Logic in India (1998)** — the best philosophical exposition of Nyaya logic and its relation to modern logic
- **Ganeri, Philosophy in Classical India (2001)** — excellent philosophical engagement with Nyaya epistemology and inference theory
- **Stephen Phillips, Epistemology in Classical India (2012)** — systematic treatment of Nyaya epistemology in dialogue with analytic philosophy
