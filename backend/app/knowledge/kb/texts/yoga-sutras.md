## What it is

The Yoga Sutras are the foundational text of **classical Yoga (Patanjala Yoga)** — the orthodox darshana that systematizes meditative practice into a unified philosophical-soteriological framework. Attributed to **Patanjali**, the text consists of 195 (or 196) terse sutras organized into four chapters (padas). It articulates the celebrated eight-limbed yoga (ashtanga: yama, niyama, asana, pranayama, pratyahara, dharana, dhyana, samadhi), the theory of citta-vritti-nirodha ("the stilling of the fluctuations of the mind"), the concept of samyama (the combined practice of dharana-dhyana-samadhi on a single object), the stages of samadhi, the supernormal powers (siddhis/vibhutis) and their warning, and the final state of kaivalya ("aloneness" — the liberated isolation of purusha from prakriti).

The text inherits Samkhya metaphysics almost wholesale (the purusha/prakriti framework, the gunas, the tattva enumeration) and adds to it the technical and practical apparatus of meditative attainment and — distinctively — a concept of **Ishvara** (a special purusha untouched by karma) as a focus of devotion and meditative absorption. Yoga is sometimes called *seshvara-samkhya* ("Samkhya with a god") on this basis.

## Textual history

The received text is usually dated to the 4th–5th c. CE. **Philipp Maas's 2006 critical work** made the influential argument that the sutras and the **Yoga Bhashya** (traditionally attributed to "Vyasa") are actually a single work by a single author — the **Patanjala Yogashastra** hypothesis — and should be read together. This is the current best scholarly position, though it is not unanimously accepted. Later commentators include **Vachaspati Mishra** (Tattva Vaisharadi, ~9th c.) and **Vijnanabhikshu** (Yoga Varttika, ~16th c.).

Importantly: the Yoga Sutras are **not** the only "yoga" text in the classical tradition. Hatha yoga (the tradition that produces most of the asanas of modern postural yoga) develops later and in a different textual lineage. The Yoga Sutras contain only minimal discussion of asana (Sutra 2.46: "sthira-sukham asanam" — "posture should be stable and comfortable") and pranayama.

## Structure

Four chapters (padas):
- **Samadhi Pada** (51 sutras): the nature of yoga, the fluctuations of the mind, the types of samadhi, the role of Ishvara, obstacles to practice
- **Sadhana Pada** (55 sutras): the path of practice — kriya yoga, the five afflictions (kleshas), karma and its fruits, the first five limbs (yama, niyama, asana, pranayama, pratyahara)
- **Vibhuti Pada** (55 sutras): the inner three limbs (dharana, dhyana, samadhi), samyama as their combination, and the supernormal powers (vibhutis) that arise from samyama on specific objects
- **Kaivalya Pada** (34 sutras): the final state of liberation, the nature of consciousness, and closing metaphysics

## Key passages

- **YS 1.2**: "yogah citta-vritti-nirodhah" — "yoga is the stilling of the fluctuations of the mind"; the definitional sutra
- **YS 1.23–29**: the Ishvara passages — devotion to Ishvara (ishvara-pranidhana) as a means of samadhi; Ishvara as the teacher of the earliest teachers
- **YS 2.29**: the list of the eight limbs (ashtanga)
- **YS 2.46–48**: the minimal asana passage — "posture should be stable and comfortable"
- **YS 3.1–4**: samyama as the combined inner practice of dharana, dhyana, and samadhi on a single object
- **YS 3.37 ("warning")**: the supernormal powers are obstacles to samadhi when pursued as ends
- **YS 4.34**: the definition of kaivalya as the establishment of the purusha in its own nature

## Modern translations worth citing

- **Bryant, The Yoga Sutras of Patanjali (2009)** — the current best general-reader scholarly translation with extensive traditional commentary summary
- **Maas, Samadhipada (2006)** — critical text of the first chapter; the scholarly gold standard
- **Mallinson and Singleton, Roots of Yoga (2017)** — the best single volume on yoga's textual history across classical and medieval traditions
- **Singleton, Yoga Body (2010)** — essential for understanding how modern postural yoga relates (and doesn't) to the Yoga Sutras
