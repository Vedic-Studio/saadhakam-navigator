## What it is

The Brahmasphutasiddhanta ("the correctly established treatise of Brahma") is the magnum opus of **Brahmagupta**, composed in 628 CE at Bhillamala (modern Rajasthan). Along with the Aryabhatiya, it is one of the two most influential texts in the classical Indian tradition of mathematical astronomy. The text is best known to historians of mathematics for containing the **earliest surviving systematic arithmetic of zero and negative numbers** — Brahmagupta's rules for adding, subtracting, multiplying, and (attempting to) divide with zero and negatives are a landmark in the global history of mathematics.

The text is also notable for **Brahmagupta's theorem** on cyclic quadrilaterals (the area formula for a quadrilateral inscribed in a circle in terms of its sides), the **Brahmagupta-Fibonacci identity** for products of sums of two squares, sophisticated treatment of indeterminate quadratic equations (including the ancestor of what would later be called Pell's equation), and a complete astronomical system with planetary theory and eclipse prediction. Brahmagupta was critical of some of Aryabhata's positions (including the earth's rotation, which Brahmagupta rejected) and the text contains extended polemical chapters against rival astronomical traditions.

The historical importance of the Brahmasphutasiddhanta extends beyond India: it was **translated into Arabic** in the 8th century (as the *Sindhind*) at the court of al-Mansur in Baghdad, and was one of the key channels through which Indian numerical notation and mathematical techniques reached the Islamic world and then Europe.

## Textual history

The text is preserved in a full form with several commentaries. **Prithudaka Svami's Vasana Bhashya** (~860 CE) is the classical commentary most often cited. The text's transmission to the Islamic world via the 8th-century Arabic translation is independently significant for the global history of mathematics — this is one of the principal routes by which the Hindu-Arabic numeral system entered the Mediterranean and European mathematical worlds.

## Structure

Twenty-four chapters organized into astronomical and mathematical sections:
- **Chapters 1–10**: astronomy proper — mean motions, true positions, the three problems (direction, place, and time), lunar and solar eclipses, risings and settings of planets, lunar crescents, conjunctions, and phases
- **Chapter 11**: criticism of previous tantras (polemical chapter against rival traditions including Aryabhata's)
- **Chapter 12 (Ganitadhyaya)**: **arithmetic** — fundamental operations, fractions, interest, series, geometry, approximate and exact values
- **Chapter 18 (Kuttakadhyaya)**: **algebra** — including the rules for zero and negative numbers, indeterminate equations (kuttaka for linear, vargaprakriti for quadratic), and Brahmagupta's own innovations
- **Chapters 19–24**: instruments, astronomical observation, and concluding material

## Key passages

- **Chapter 18 on zero and negatives**: Brahmagupta's explicit arithmetic rules — "The sum of two positives is positive, of two negatives is negative; of a positive and a negative, their difference." "Zero added to zero is zero. A positive or negative subtracted from zero becomes negative or positive respectively. Zero subtracted from a positive or negative is the positive or negative itself." "The product of a positive and negative is negative; of two negatives or two positives, positive. The product of zero and a positive or negative, or of zero and zero, is zero." Brahmagupta's treatment of division by zero is less successful (he incorrectly writes zero/zero = zero), and it would be another five hundred years before Bhaskara II attempted a more careful treatment.
- **Chapter 12 on cyclic quadrilaterals**: Brahmagupta's theorem — the area of a cyclic quadrilateral with sides a, b, c, d is √((s−a)(s−b)(s−c)(s−d)), where s is the semi-perimeter (the generalization of Heron's formula to cyclic quadrilaterals)
- **The Brahmagupta identity**: (a² + b²)(c² + d²) = (ac − bd)² + (ad + bc)² — a result later known as the Brahmagupta-Fibonacci identity
- **The vargaprakriti (indeterminate quadratic equation) treatment**: the method for generating solutions to x² − Ny² = 1 (later called Pell's equation); the systematic chakravala method was developed in full by Bhaskara II in the 12th c.
- **Chapter 11 critique of Aryabhata**: the polemical chapter rejecting Aryabhata's claim of earth rotation and defending the standard siddhanta view of a stationary earth

## Modern translations worth citing

- **Colebrooke's 1817 translation** of the mathematical chapters is historically important — it is the document through which European mathematicians first encountered Brahmagupta's rules for zero and negatives
- **Plofker, Mathematics in India (2009)** — the current best scholarly contextualization of Brahmagupta within the broader tradition
- A modern scholarly English translation of the full treatise is not yet widely available; scholarship mostly works with the Sanskrit and individual chapter translations
