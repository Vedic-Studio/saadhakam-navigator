## What it is

The Manusmriti — more formally the **Manava Dharmashastra** ("the dharmashastra of Manu") — is the most famous and most translated work of classical Indian dharmashastra. It is a verse work of 2,685 shlokas organized into 12 chapters, attributed to the mythic lawgiver **Manu**, which presents itself as a comprehensive treatment of dharma covering creation, the varna system, the four life-stages (ashramas), civil and criminal law, penance (prayashchitta), and the fruits of action after death.

The text's **modern political salience** is enormous: because it contains prescriptions on caste, women, and outcastes that are morally and politically explosive by modern standards, Manusmriti has been both the target of reformist and anti-caste critique (including Ambedkar's famous 1927 public burning) and the focus of reactive defensive readings. The **scholarly** picture is different from the popular one: Manusmriti is **one dharmashastra text among many**, and in actual classical legal practice the **Yajnavalkya Smriti** (via the Mitakshara commentary) was often more influential than Manusmriti. Treating Manusmriti as "the" classical Hindu legal code is a modern distortion, partly inherited from colonial legal history.

Patrick Olivelle's 2005 Oxford critical edition, based on a systematic study of the manuscript tradition, is the current scholarly gold standard and corrected longstanding errors in the inherited textus receptus.

## Textual history

The text is composite but reached its received form in a narrower window than most classical Sanskrit texts — Olivelle places its composition between ~200 BCE and ~200 CE, with ~2nd c. CE as the best single estimate. The composite character is visible in internal inconsistencies (some passages seem to assume different social-political contexts than others) and in the relationship between the verse and prose strata. "Manu" is a mythic lawgiver figure — the "father of humankind" — not a historical author. The text presents itself as authoritative because it is given by a divine source, not because it is the work of a named individual at a datable time.

The later commentarial tradition is large: **Medhatithi** (~9th c.), **Govindaraja** (~12th c.), **Kulluka** (~13th–15th c.), and **Sarvajnanarayana** are the major classical commentators; Kulluka's commentary is the one most often printed with the text.

## Structure

Twelve chapters:
1. Creation of the world and the source of dharma
2. The sources of dharma (shruti, smriti, sadachara, atmasantushti); samskaras (life-cycle rites); the brahmacharya (student) stage
3. The grhastha (householder) stage; marriage; domestic ritual
4. Householder conduct; prohibited foods; right livelihood
5. Purity and impurity; women's conduct; prohibited food details
6. The vanaprastha (forest-dweller) and sannyasa (renunciate) stages
7. The king's dharma; rajadharma
8. Civil and criminal law; the eighteen titles of litigation
9. Family law; inheritance; women's property; royal duties continued
10. Mixed varnas; the circumstances of the different varnas
11. Prayashchitta (penance and expiation)
12. Karma and its fruits; rebirth; the highest good

## Key passages

- **Manu 1.1–4**: the mythic setting — the sages approach Manu and request the dharma; Manu's response as the authoritative text
- **Manu 2.6–12**: the **source hierarchy** — shruti > smriti > sadachara > atmasantushti; the classical order for resolving conflicts
- **Manu 2.224**: "where women are honored, there the gods are pleased" — the famous passage often cited to complicate simple characterizations of the text's attitude toward women
- **Manu 5.147–166**: the prescriptions on women's dependence ("na stri svatantryam arhati") — among the text's most politically contested passages
- **Manu 7.1–ff**: rajadharma — the king's duties, the nature of the danda (scepter/punishment), the king as protector of dharma
- **Manu 8.1–ff**: the eighteen titles of litigation (vyavahara) — classical Indian civil and criminal categories
- **Manu 10.1–ff**: varna and mixed varnas; these passages are the primary modern battleground around the text

## Modern translations worth citing

- **Olivelle, Manu's Code of Law (2005)** — the current scholarly standard: critical Sanskrit edition + annotated English translation + substantial introduction covering dating, structure, and interpretive history
- **Olivelle and Olivelle, The Law Code of Manu (2004)** — the shorter Oxford World's Classics edition for general readers
- **Doniger, The Laws of Manu (1991)** — a widely read Penguin translation; scholarly but aimed at general readers
- For comparative work on how Manusmriti relates to actual classical legal practice: **Davis Jr., The Spirit of Hindu Law (2010)**
