## What it is

The Charaka Samhita is one of the two canonical compendia of classical Ayurveda (the other being the Sushruta Samhita), focused on internal medicine, diagnosis, clinical management, and the doctrinal foundation of the tridosha framework. It is widely treated as *the* foundational internal-medicine text of Ayurveda.

## Textual history

Classical tradition presents the text as Charaka's redaction of an earlier work by Agnivesha, with further redaction by Dridhabala in a later period to fill sections that had been lost. This layered attribution is not a problem — it is the normal textual-history model for a long-lived medical tradition. The received text is best understood as the product of multiple centuries of redaction.

## Dating debate

- Earliest stratum: perhaps 1st c. BCE, possibly earlier
- Later redactions: up to roughly the 4th c. CE
- Modern history-of-medicine scholarship treats the dating as layered rather than pointlike

## Structure

- Eight sections (sthanas):
  1. **Sutrasthana** — foundational doctrine, materia medica, food, drugs
  2. **Nidanasthana** — diagnosis and etiology
  3. **Vimanasthana** — clinical method, epistemology, teaching
  4. **Sharirasthana** — anatomy, embryology, philosophical preliminaries
  5. **Indriyasthana** — prognostic signs
  6. **Chikitsasthana** — therapeutics (the longest section)
  7. **Kalpasthana** — preparations
  8. **Siddhisthana** — treatment procedures and complications

## Key passages

- **Definitional passages on dosha and prakriti** — the doctrinal heart of the framework
- **Vimanasthana on clinical method and the physician's epistemology** — a surprisingly rich early discussion of how to reason about evidence in a clinical setting
- **Sutrasthana on food and daily regimen** — the lifestyle-management material most often cited in modern integrative medicine

## Modern translations worth citing

- **Priyavrat Sharma's Chaukhamba edition** — standard scholarly Sanskrit-English reference
- **Wujastyk's The Roots of Ayurveda** — carefully selected passages with modern scholarly framing; the best starting point for a reader new to the tradition
- Older full translations (e.g., A.C. Kaviratna) exist and can be cited with "early translation" tagging
