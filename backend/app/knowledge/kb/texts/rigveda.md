## What it is

The Rigveda is the oldest layer of Sanskrit shruti — a collection of 1,028 hymns in ten books (mandalas) composed for ritual recitation. Genre matters: these are *hymns*, not philosophical treatises, not historical chronicles, and not scientific manuals. Treating them as any of the latter produces predictable category errors.

## Textual history

The hymns were orally transmitted with extreme precision through specialized memorization techniques (pada-patha, krama-patha, jata-patha, ghana-patha), which is why the text survives with unusual textual stability relative to most ancient literature. The standard recension is Shakala. Composition is stratified: some mandalas (especially 2–7, the "family books") are considered older than others (mandalas 1, 8, 9, 10).

## Dating debate

- **Western Indological consensus**: composition approximately 1500–1200 BCE, in the greater Indus-Sarasvati region.
- **Oldest mandalas may predate the whole**: mandalas 2–7 are often dated earlier than 1 and 10.
- **Revisionist / traditional positions**: argue for considerably earlier dates, sometimes on the basis of astronomical references within the text. Modern historical scholarship (Jamison & Witzel) treats the chronology as hard and emphasizes methodological difficulty.

Treat the dating as *live* when writing, not settled.

## Structure

- 10 mandalas, 1,028 hymns, ~10,600 verses
- Mandala 9 is entirely devoted to Soma
- Mandala 10 contains later additions including the Nasadiya Sukta (10.129), the Purusha Sukta (10.90), and the Gambler's Lament (10.34)
- Mandalas 2–7 are the "family books" ascribed to specific rishi families

## Key passages

- **RV 10.129 (Nasadiya Sukta)** — the creation hymn; widely cited for its non-committal, question-posing cosmology
- **RV 10.90 (Purusha Sukta)** — the cosmic-person hymn; source of later varna interpretations
- **RV 10.34 (Aksha Sukta / Gambler's Lament)** — monologue of a repentant gambler; a remarkable early literary depiction of compulsive behavior and social consequences
- Hymns attributed to women rishis (see the [women rishis claim card](../claims/rigveda-women-rishis-claim.md) for attribution caution) — Lopamudra, Ghosha, Apala, and others; the tradition names them, but sweeping numerical claims ("27 women wrote X") circulating online rarely have a single primary source

## Modern translations worth citing

- **Jamison & Brereton, The Rigveda (Oxford 2014)** — the current reference English translation with extensive scholarly apparatus
- **Ralph Griffith, 1896** — tag as *early translation*; useful historically but supersede with modern work where possible
- **Stephanie Jamison & Michael Witzel, 'Vedic Hinduism'** (Harvard working paper) — excellent introduction to the methodological issues
