## What it is

The Lilavati is the **arithmetic book** of **Bhaskara II's** (1114–1185 CE) encyclopedic astronomical-mathematical work the **Siddhanta Shiromani** ("crest-jewel of treatises"). It is arguably the most widely used Indian mathematics textbook ever written: for centuries it was the standard entry point for mathematical training in India, was taught continuously from its composition in 1150 CE through the colonial era, and was among the first Indian mathematical works to be systematically studied by European scholars (via Colebrooke's 1817 translation).

The work is celebrated both for its mathematical content — a comprehensive treatment of arithmetic, algebra applied to arithmetic, geometry, mensuration, series, and combinatorial and number-theoretic problems — and for its **distinctive literary style**: the problems are addressed to a girl named Lilavati (traditionally identified as Bhaskara's daughter, though the historical reality of this is uncertain), and many of the problems are posed in elegant verse with vivid imagery (swans on a pond, bees on a lotus, peacocks in a grove). This literary framing is part of why the text has remained compelling to learners across many centuries.

## Textual history

The Lilavati is the first of four books of the Siddhanta Shiromani, the others being the **Bijaganita** (algebra), **Grahaganita** or Ganitadhyaya (planetary astronomy), and **Goladhyaya** (spherical astronomy). The text has an extensive commentarial tradition in Sanskrit and a translation history in multiple Indian vernaculars. **Ganesha Daivajna's Buddhivilasini** (~1545 CE) is the classical standard commentary. **Akbar's court** famously commissioned a Persian translation by **Fayzi** in 1587 CE, which was one of the channels through which European scholars first learned about Indian mathematics.

## Structure

Thirteen chapters covering the arithmetic curriculum:
- **Chapters 1–2**: units of measure, definitions, and the eight fundamental operations (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, squaring, square root, cubing, cube root) on integers and fractions
- **Chapter 3**: miscellaneous problems on rule-of-three, proportions, and interest
- **Chapter 4**: mixtures, series (arithmetic and geometric), permutations and combinations
- **Chapter 5**: progressions
- **Chapters 6–7**: plane geometry — triangles, quadrilaterals, circles, and mensuration
- **Chapter 8**: volumes and solid mensuration
- **Chapter 9**: timber measurement
- **Chapter 10**: measurement of stacks and piles
- **Chapter 11**: indeterminate problems (shadow problems, pursuit problems)
- **Chapter 12**: kuttaka (linear indeterminate equations)
- **Chapter 13**: combinatorics, including the general formula for combinations

## Key passages

- **Chapter 2's eight fundamental operations**: Bhaskara presents the operations systematically and includes explicit rules for operations with zero — including his careful (but still not fully correct) treatment of division by zero, which he describes as "an unchanging quantity" (khahara) when a finite number is divided by zero
- **The lotus problem**: a famous verse problem asking how deep the water is if a lotus of known height tips over to just touch the water at a given distance from its base (an application of the Pythagorean theorem)
- **The bee problem**: a charming combinatorial problem that has become emblematic of Lilavati's literary style
- **The combinatorics chapter**: presents the general formula for combinations, derived systematically — this is one of the earliest systematic treatments of combinatorial formulas in any mathematical tradition
- **The opening dedication to Lilavati**: the literary framing that gives the text its distinctive character
