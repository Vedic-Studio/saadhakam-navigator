## What it is

The Natya Shastra is the foundational text of the classical Indian tradition of **performing arts** — theatre, dance, music, stagecraft, aesthetics, and the theoretical framework within which all of these are understood. Attributed to **Bharata Muni**, the work is an enormous treatise of 36 chapters containing roughly 6,000 verses (in the longer recension; slightly fewer in the shorter). It presents itself as a revealed text — the "fifth Veda" created by Brahma for the benefit of all varnas, who were excluded from full Vedic participation — and covers everything from the myth of the origin of drama, to the technical details of playhouse construction, to the classification of dramatic genres, to musical theory, to the system of gestures (mudras), to costumes and makeup, and — most importantly for later aesthetics — the theory of **rasa**.

The Natya Shastra's most celebrated contribution is **rasa theory**, the classical Indian account of aesthetic emotion: the idea that a well-wrought performance evokes in the audience a distinctive aesthetic state (rasa) that is produced by the artistically expressed underlying emotion (bhava) of the characters. Chapter 6 — the **rasa adhyaya** — contains the foundational definition: "vibhava-anubhava-vyabhichari-samyogat rasa-nishpattih" ("rasa arises from the combination of causes, effects, and transitory emotions"). This brief passage has been the subject of sustained philosophical commentary for nearly two thousand years and is the single most important passage in classical Indian aesthetics.

## Textual history

The Natya Shastra is a composite work whose dating is disputed. Internal evidence suggests layered composition: the musical chapters, the rasa chapter, and the chapters on dramatic form may represent different strata. Most scholarship places the core text between ~200 BCE and ~200 CE, with later accretions continuing through the early-first-millennium.

The most important commentary is **Abhinavagupta's Abhinavabharati** (~1000 CE), a monumental work that elaborates the rasa theory far beyond what is explicit in the Natya Shastra itself and has been more influential on later Indian aesthetic theory than parts of the original text. Abhinavagupta's theory of rasa as a distinctive mode of aesthetic consciousness (rasasvada) is the classical high point of Indian aesthetics.

## Structure

Thirty-six chapters organized roughly by subject:
- **Chapters 1–5**: origin myth of drama, construction of the playhouse, ritual consecration of the stage, preliminary rites
- **Chapter 6 (Rasa Adhyaya)**: the eight rasas and their definition — **the crucial aesthetic chapter**
- **Chapter 7 (Bhava Adhyaya)**: the 49 bhavas (eight sthayi-bhavas, eight sattvika-bhavas, 33 vyabhichari-bhavas)
- **Chapters 8–12**: abhinaya — the four modes of expression (angika/bodily, vachika/vocal, aharya/costume, sattvika/emotional)
- **Chapters 13–14**: technique of dance and movement
- **Chapters 15–22**: dramatic form, plot construction, character types, dramatic styles (vrittis) and genres (dasharupaka — the ten forms of drama)
- **Chapters 23–27**: costume, makeup, and stagecraft
- **Chapters 28–33**: music — swaras (notes), gramas (scales), jatis (early raga categories), talas (rhythm cycles), instruments
- **Chapters 34–36**: concluding material and the descent of dramatic knowledge from heaven

## Key passages

- **NS 1.106–116**: the origin myth — the gods approach Brahma for a fifth Veda accessible to all; Brahma creates the Natya Veda by combining elements from the four Vedas; he teaches it to Bharata, who teaches it to his sons
- **NS 6.31 (the rasa sutra)**: "vibhava-anubhava-vyabhichari-samyogat rasa-nishpattih" — the foundational definition of how rasa arises
- **NS 6.44–45**: the list of the **eight classical rasas** — shringara (erotic/romantic), hasya (comic), karuna (pathetic), raudra (furious), vira (heroic), bhayanaka (fearful), bibhatsa (disgusting), adbhuta (marvelous). The ninth rasa (shanta, peaceful) is added by Abhinavagupta in the Abhinavabharati.
- **NS Chapter 28 on music**: the early systematic treatment of swaras, srutis, grama-ragas, and murchanas — the classical Indian musical system in its early documented form
- **NS Chapter 15 on sandhis**: the five junctures of dramatic plot — the classical Indian counterpart to Aristotle's account of dramatic structure in the Poetics

## Modern translations worth citing

- **Manomohan Ghosh, The Natyasastra (1950–61)** — the standard complete English translation, 2 volumes; still the reference, though some of its interpretive choices are dated
- **Adya Rangacharya, The Natyasastra (1996)** — a more accessible complete English translation
- **Sheldon Pollock, A Rasa Reader (2016)** — provides the crucial rasa passages in new translation alongside the history of their interpretation; the best modern entry point for the rasa tradition
- **Kapila Vatsyayan, Bharata: The Natyasastra (1996)** — compact scholarly introduction to the text and its world
