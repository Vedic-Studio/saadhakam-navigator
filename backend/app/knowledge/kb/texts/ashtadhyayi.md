## What it is

The Ashtadhyayi ("Eight Chapters") is Panini's grammar of Sanskrit. It is an exceptionally compact system of rules that together generate the well-formed forms of Classical Sanskrit. It is not a descriptive grammar in the modern linguistic sense but a **derivational** system: the rules take abstract roots and affixes and, when applied in a disciplined order, produce correct Sanskrit words and forms.

## Textual history

The Ashtadhyayi is incomprehensible without its commentarial tradition:

- **Panini** (~5th–4th c. BCE) — the sutras themselves
- **Katyayana** (~3rd c. BCE) — Varttikas (critical notes)
- **Patanjali** (~2nd c. BCE) — Mahabhashya ("Great Commentary"), the most important classical commentary
- **Jayaditya & Vamana, Kashika Vritti** (~7th c. CE) — a standard pedagogical commentary
- **Bhattoji Dikshita, Siddhanta Kaumudi** (~17th c.) — reorders Panini's rules for teaching, becoming the dominant later pedagogical tradition

## Dating debate

The traditional 5th–4th c. BCE dating for Panini is widely used as a working range but not universally accepted. Scholars have argued for both earlier and later dates. For content purposes, cite the traditional range with a note about the uncertainty.

## Structure

- Eight chapters (adhyayas), each divided into four sections (padas)
- Approximately 4,000 sutras
- Rules operate on abstract roots (dhatu), affixes, and meta-rules that govern rule application
- The system includes **anubandhas** (diacritical markers used only in the metalanguage), explicit rule-ordering conventions, and conflict-resolution principles

## Key passages

- **Shiva Sutras** — the fourteen pratyahara sutras at the start, which define the phonological inventory in a compressed, set-theoretic way
- **Rule-ordering sutras** — the meta-rules that govern how other rules apply
- **Karaka definitions** — the sutras that define semantic-grammatical roles (agent, patient, instrument, location, etc.) which underpin the derivation of sentences

## Modern translations worth citing
