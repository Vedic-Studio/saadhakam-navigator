## What it is

**Anumana** is the Sanskrit term for **inference** — the second of the four (or more) means of valid knowledge (pramanas) in Nyaya epistemology. In its classical Nyaya formulation, anumana is the move from perceiving one thing (the sign, linga or hetu) to knowing another thing (the sign-possessor, sadhya) that is not directly perceived, on the basis of a prior knowledge of an invariable relation (vyapti — "pervasion") between the two.

The standard Nyaya example, repeated in every textbook of Indian logic:
- *Pratijna* (thesis): "The hill is on fire"
- *Hetu* (reason): "because there is smoke"
- *Udaharana* (example): "wherever there is smoke, there is fire, as in a kitchen"
- *Upanaya* (application): "the hill has smoke that is of the kind pervaded by fire"
- *Nigamana* (conclusion): "therefore the hill is on fire"

This **five-membered schema** (pancavayava) is the classical Nyaya inference form. It is sometimes compared with the Aristotelian syllogism, but there are important differences: Nyaya's schema includes an *example* as a formal part of the inference (the udaharana), which Aristotle's syllogism does not; Nyaya's logic is fundamentally about cognitive moves from known to unknown in real inference, not about formal relations among abstract terms; and the relation of pervasion (vyapti) that grounds the inference is itself analyzed with great care in the Nyaya tradition.

## Why anumana matters

- **Classical Indian logic**: anumana is the central technical achievement of classical Indian logic. Its treatment of the conditions for valid inference, the fallacies (hetvabhasa) that disqualify an argument, and the careful analysis of the relation of pervasion is a sophisticated formal-logical apparatus.
- **The vyapti (pervasion) problem**: how do we know that "wherever there is smoke, there is fire"? Classical Nyaya develops increasingly sophisticated analyses — from Gautama's relatively simple treatment, through Vatsyayana's elaboration, to Udayana's and eventually Gangesha's Navya-Nyaya reformulation, which is one of the most technical pieces of classical philosophy anywhere in the world.
- **Debate with Buddhism**: Buddhist logicians (Dignaga, Dharmakirti) develop a three-membered inference schema and argue that the Nyaya five-membered schema is redundant. The ensuing debate is one of the central methodological conversations in classical Indian philosophy.

## Category errors to avoid

- **"Anumana is just the Aristotelian syllogism."** There are formal similarities but real differences. Aristotle's syllogism is about formal relations among universal and particular propositions; Nyaya's anumana is about cognitive moves in real inference with an explicitly epistemic (and not merely formal) character. The udaharana (example) is part of the Nyaya formal schema but not the Aristotelian one.
- **"Nyaya logic is equivalent to modern first-order logic."** The claim overclaims. Nyaya has sophisticated handling of quantification-like phenomena and of relations, and some of Navya-Nyaya's moves anticipate modern logical notation in suggestive ways. But it is not a modern formal system with a recursive syntax and model-theoretic semantics. See [Nyaya first-order logic claim](../claims/nyaya-first-order-logic-claim.md).
- **"The five-membered schema is a single canonical form."** Nyaya itself recognizes that the five-membered schema can be simplified when context allows; later tradition (including Navya-Nyaya) works with reduced forms. Treat the five-membered form as a pedagogical canonical form, not an inviolable rule.

## Modern bridges

- **Logic and epistemology**: Nyaya inference theory is a sophisticated premodern treatment of the conditions for rational belief formation that productively engages with contemporary work in formal epistemology. *Breaks because* Nyaya's home problems (pramana validation, vyapti establishment, debate protocols) are not exactly the same as the home problems of contemporary analytic epistemology.
- **Argumentation theory**: Nyaya's taxonomy of debate types (vada, jalpa, vitanda) and fallacies is a careful classical treatment of what we now call argumentation theory and has real resonances with modern work on defeasible reasoning and dialectical logic.
