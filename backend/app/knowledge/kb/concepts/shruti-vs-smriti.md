## Definition

**Shruti** ("that which is heard") is the category of most-authoritative sacred literature in the Sanatan tradition, comprising the Vedas and their associated Brahmana, Aranyaka, and Upanishad layers. It is classically described as *revealed* — heard by the rishis in meditative seership — and therefore holding the highest scriptural authority.

**Smriti** ("that which is remembered") is the category of derivative, interpretive, and codifying literature that elaborates and applies Vedic thought. This is a very wide category, including dharmashastras, itihasas (Ramayana, Mahabharata), Puranas, and various sutras and shastras.

## Where it appears

The distinction is a classical one, articulated across commentarial literature and reference grammars of the tradition. It is not confined to a single locus; it is the organizing principle for how the tradition classifies its own textual corpus.

## Interpretive history

- **Classical view**: shruti is apauruṣeya (not of human origin) and eternal; smriti is human composition, however authoritative.
- **Mimamsa schools**: give the strongest formal account of why shruti has ultimate authority.
- **Vedanta schools**: treat shruti as the final pramana (means of knowledge) for questions of ultimate reality, with smriti valuable as elaboration.
- **Modern historical scholarship**: treats both as historical textual strata composed over time; the classical apauruṣeya claim is handled as a tradition-internal theological position, not a historical thesis.

## Modern bridges

- **Primary sources vs interpretation**: the shruti/smriti distinction tracks something like the modern methodological distinction between primary sources and interpretive literature. *Breaks because* the classical distinction is theologically grounded, while modern historiography is methodological.
- **Constitutional law vs case law**: shruti functions like a foundational constitutional text whose authority constrains subsequent interpretation, while smriti is more like the accumulated case-law tradition that applies and extends it. *Breaks because* the religious authority of shruti is of a different kind than constitutional authority.

## Category errors to avoid

- **Treating "Hindu shastras" as a single homogeneous corpus.** This is the single most common mistake in public writing about Indian knowledge traditions. The shruti/smriti distinction, layered with the further genre distinctions (sutra, shastra, bhashya, purana, itihasa, etc.), is what prevents it.
- **Reading later texts back into earlier revelation.** Many viral "the Vedas contain X modern science" claims come from reading later syntheses (e.g., Upavedas, Puranas) as if they were Vedic hymns.
- **Dismissing smriti as unimportant.** Smriti includes the Mahabharata, Ramayana, the Manusmriti, the Puranas, and the classical philosophical sutras — the *lived* texture of the tradition for most practitioners throughout history.
