## What it is

The **Aksha Sukta** (also called the **Gambler's Lament** or **Gamester's Lament**) is Rigveda 10.34 — a hymn of 14 verses voiced by a compulsive gambler describing how his dice addiction has destroyed his marriage, alienated his family, driven him into debt, and left him ashamed but still unable to stop. Mandala 10 is the latest layer of the Rigveda and contains several hymns on non-ritual, philosophical, and social topics; the Aksha Sukta is the best-known of these and is often cited as the earliest surviving first-person portrait of addiction in world literature.

## Where it sits in the Rigveda

- **Mandala 10**, the tenth and latest book of the Rigveda, contains many hymns that break from the ritual-and-praise pattern of Mandalas 2–7 (the "family books"). Mandala 10 includes the Purusha Sukta, the Nasadiya Sukta (creation hymn), and the Aksha Sukta.
- Traditional attribution names the seer as **Kanvasha Ailusha** (or **Aksha Maujavant** in some lists) — the attribution is traditional, and the hymn itself is the surviving textual fact.
- The game being described uses **vibhidaka** nuts (the hard, brown seeds of Terminalia bellirica) as dice, thrown on a grooved board. The line "the brown ones, born on a windy place" refers to these nuts.

## What the hymn actually says

Reading the hymn verse by verse (following Jamison & Brereton's modern critical translation) gives six clear narrative beats:

1. **The opening (verses 1–2)**: the dice "delight me as a draught of soma from Mujavant" — the addiction framed as pleasure before the turn.
2. **The wife leaves (verse 2)**: "my devoted wife hated me; she pushed me away" — the first-person voice records the social cost.
3. **Family alienation (verse 3)**: "the mother-in-law hates me; the wife rejects me; the afflicted man finds no sympathizer."
4. **Shame and compulsion (verses 4–6)**: the gambler describes his resolve to quit, how it dissolves as soon as he sees the dice again — "when I resolve, 'I will not play with them,' my friends depart and leave me; but when the brown ones rattled have raised their voice, I go to their rendezvous like a woman to her lover."
5. **Social and economic ruin (verses 7–10)**: debt mounts, relatives disown him, and he is reduced to wandering at night seeking money.
6. **The closing exhortation (verses 13–14)**: "play not with dice; plough thy field; rejoice in thy portion; there are thy cattle, there thy wife, O gambler." The hymn ends in a direct moral address to the gambler (or to the listener).

The Jamison & Brereton translation is the current scholarly standard in English; Doniger's Penguin translation is the most accessible. C. K. Raju has also published a working translation with commentary.

## Why it matters for content

- **First-person voice is rare in the Rigveda.** Most hymns are addressed *to* a god. The Aksha Sukta is addressed *to the listener* by a gambler speaking in the first person about his own ruin. This is a structural feature worth naming.
- **It is a psychological portrait, not just a moral fable.** The hymn captures the classic ambivalence of addiction: resolve, relapse, shame, inability to stop, full awareness that stopping would be better. Modern readings compare this to the phenomenology of addiction that cognitive-behavioural therapy attempts to address.
- **It shows that the Vedic corpus is not uniformly ritual.** The Rigveda is usually introduced as a book of hymns to the gods. The Aksha Sukta is a good corrective: Mandala 10 includes social observation, creation speculation, and first-person ethical reflection.

## Modern bridges

- **Addiction psychology and CBT**: the hymn's description of resolve-and-relapse, environmental triggers ("when the dice rattle"), and social collapse around the user closely matches the phenomenology CBT models for addiction target. *Breaks because* the hymn offers no treatment protocol; its "cure" is the moral exhortation in the final verse, not a structured intervention.
- **Confessional literature**: the first-person voice prefigures a genre (confession, testimony, autobiographical moral reflection) that Western literature does not clearly exhibit until much later — Augustine's Confessions is a standard comparison point. *Breaks because* the Aksha Sukta is a single hymn, not a sustained narrative.
- **Behavioural economics and "hot-cold empathy gap"**: the gambler's "when I resolve not to play, I walk away; when I hear the dice, I go like a woman to her lover" is a textbook description of how in-the-moment cue exposure defeats prior commitments — exactly what modern behavioural economics studies as the hot-cold empathy gap.

## Category errors to avoid

- **"The Rigveda had cognitive-behavioural therapy."** No — the hymn describes addiction with remarkable psychological clarity but does not describe a therapeutic intervention. The responsible claim is about *descriptive* insight, not treatment.
- **"The Vedic period had a widespread gambling problem."** The hymn is a specific voice, not a sociological report. Drawing general conclusions about the prevalence of gambling in Vedic society from one hymn is over-reading.
- **"This proves ancient Indians discovered addiction."** Addiction is a universal human phenomenon. The claim worth making is that this hymn is a remarkably early and psychologically acute *record* of it in world literature.
