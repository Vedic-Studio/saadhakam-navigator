## What it is

**Apaurusheyatva** — literally "not-made-by-a-person-ness" — is the technical Mimamsa doctrine that the **Veda has no author**: it is neither composed by a human being nor composed by God. The Veda is eternal and authorless; it exists as a real structure of sacred sound that is *discovered* by rishis in meditative vision, not *composed* by them, and it does not depend for its authority on any creator.

This is one of the most distinctive and philosophically sophisticated positions in classical Indian thought, and it is specifically the position of **Purva Mimamsa**. The doctrine is not shared by all orthodox schools: Nyaya-Vaisheshika, for instance, holds that the Veda is authored by Ishvara (God), which is closer to the Christian and Islamic position that a revealed text has a divine author. Mimamsa's distinctive move is to deny that the Veda has *any* author — human or divine — and to argue that this authorlessness is the ground of its epistemic authority.

## The argument structure

The Mimamsa case for apaurusheyatva rests on several interlocking arguments:

1. **The absence of a remembered author**: no one remembers an author of the Veda. Human authored texts (kavya, shastra) come with a remembered author and a remembered composition; the Veda does not. This suggests that the Veda has no author to be remembered.

2. **The unsuitability of a human author**: a human author making such a huge claim about ritual efficacy would need to have known the (invisible) future effects of the rites they prescribe — but no human has that knowledge. Therefore no human could have composed the Veda.

3. **The problem of a divine author**: if Ishvara is the author, then the Veda's authority depends on Ishvara's reliability, which itself requires proof. This either leads to an infinite regress or to circular reasoning (the Veda tells us Ishvara exists; Ishvara's authorship tells us the Veda is reliable). Mimamsa chooses to cut this knot by denying a divine author.

4. **Sound is eternal**: Mimamsa defends the eternality of sound (a surprisingly technical and important philosophical position) — the phoneme "a" is one real, eternal entity that is instantiated in every utterance. This grounds the argument that Vedic verses, as structured sequences of eternal sounds, can themselves be eternal.

5. **Svatah-pramanya** (intrinsic validity): Kumarila's distinctive principle — that all cognitions are intrinsically valid (reliable prima facie) unless specifically defeated by a defeater. Applied to the Veda: if the Veda gives us a cognition about dharma, that cognition is intrinsically valid unless we have some specific reason to doubt it, and we do not (since the Veda's authorlessness means there is no human error-introducing origin to worry about).

## Why apaurusheyatva matters

- **An argument for scriptural authority that does not invoke God**: this is a historically and philosophically striking position. Most classical theories of sacred texts ground their authority in a divine source. Mimamsa insists that the Veda is authoritative precisely because it has no source.
- **A sophisticated philosophy of language**: the position forces Mimamsa to develop highly detailed accounts of what sounds are, how words refer, how sentences mean, and how injunctions differ from descriptions. Mimamsa philosophy of language is one of the most technically sophisticated in the classical world.
- **A central point of debate**: the Buddhist tradition explicitly denies apaurusheyatva (the Buddhists argue that the Buddha's teaching, not the Veda, is the source of correct knowledge), and the Nyaya-Vaisheshika tradition also denies it (they argue the Veda has Ishvara as its author). The Mimamsa defense of apaurusheyatva is one of the central polemical battlegrounds in classical Indian philosophy.

## Category errors to avoid

- **"Apaurusheyatva is the same as saying the Veda is 'divine.'"** No — Mimamsa explicitly denies that the Veda is divine in the sense of having a divine author. Mimamsa often looks atheistic or naturalistic to modern eyes precisely because it denies the divine-author frame.
- **"All Hindu schools agree that the Veda is authorless."** No. Mimamsa holds apaurusheyatva; Nyaya-Vaisheshika holds that the Veda is authored by Ishvara (God); some Vedanta sub-schools lean one way or the other. The question is contested within classical orthodoxy.
- **"This means the Veda 'just appeared' out of nothing."** No — the Mimamsa view is that the Veda is eternal. It has no temporal beginning; it is not that it appeared at some point. The rishis *discover* Vedic mantras in meditative vision; they do not compose them.
- **"Apaurusheyatva is a primitive dogma that modern scholarship has dispatched."** No — it is a serious philosophical position with sophisticated argumentative support, and modern work (Arnold, Taber, Kataoka, Freschi, Dan Arnold) takes it seriously as a philosophical position worth engaging.

## Modern bridges

- **Philosophy of language and semantics**: Mimamsa's theory of meaning, eternality of sound, and speech-act-like analysis of injunctions has productive resonance with modern philosophy of language (Frege, Austin, Grice), though the metaphysical framing differs.
- **Epistemology**: Kumarila's svatah-pramanya principle is structurally close to modern foundationalist positions in epistemology (a cognition is prima facie reliable unless defeated) and has been engaged by modern analytic philosophers.
- **Philosophy of religion**: the argument that a sacred text can have authority without an author is a distinctive contribution to the philosophy of religion that contrasts sharply with Abrahamic models.
