## What it is

In Samkhya-Yoga metaphysics, the **three gunas** are the three fundamental constituent principles or "strands" (literally: guna = "strand, rope") of **prakriti** — the material-phenomenal side of reality distinct from purusha (pure consciousness). Every phenomenal object, every mental state, every qualitative feature of experience is understood as a particular configuration of the three gunas in varying proportions. The three are:

1. **Sattva** — lightness, clarity, illumination, buoyancy, transparency; the guna of pleasure, intelligence, and calm knowing
2. **Rajas** — activity, motion, passion, stimulation, restlessness; the guna of action and emotional engagement
3. **Tamas** — heaviness, inertia, obscuration, dullness, darkness; the guna of resistance, confusion, and depression

Importantly, the gunas are **not qualities** in the Vaisheshika sense (where guna means "quality inhering in a substance"). In Samkhya-Yoga, the gunas are **constituent principles of prakriti itself** — prakriti just *is* the three gunas in dynamic equilibrium (when unmanifest) or in differentiated imbalance (when manifest as the phenomenal world). The three terms "sattva, rajas, tamas" do *not* mean "quality, quality, quality" — they name three irreducible cosmological-psychological strands whose interaction produces everything in the manifest world.

In unmanifest prakriti, the three gunas are in perfect equilibrium and the world is undifferentiated potential. When the equilibrium is disturbed (in Samkhya this happens through the mere presence of purusha), the gunas enter into differentiated combinations, and the phenomenal world evolves in stages: first **buddhi** (intellect), then **ahamkara** (ego-principle), then the senses, the subtle elements, and finally the gross elements.

## Where it appears

- [Samkhya Karika (Ishvarakrishna)](../texts/samkhya-karika.md) — verses 11–14 give the classical definition: sattva is illumination and joy, rajas is activity and suffering, tamas is inertia and delusion
- [Yoga Sutras (Patanjali)](../texts/yoga-sutras.md) — assumes Samkhya guna theory throughout; citta-vritti-nirodha is partly understood as gradually clearing the chitta of rajasic and tamasic modes so that sattva predominates
- **Bhagavad Gita** — Chapters 14, 17, and 18 contain extensive passages on how the three gunas shape action, knowledge, worship, food, and character; the Gita popularizes Samkhya guna theory for a much wider audience
- Classical Ayurveda — draws on guna theory for psychological and medical classification
- Classical aesthetics (Natya Shastra, later rasa theory) — uses guna concepts as a secondary vocabulary

## Why guna theory matters

- **A non-reductive analysis of experience**: Samkhya's three-guna framework is an analysis of experience that doesn't reduce qualitative features (pleasure, activity, heaviness) to purely physical or purely mental categories. It is a classical Indian contribution to what modern philosophy of mind calls "qualitative character" or "phenomenal feel."
- **A framework for ethics and psychology**: the gunas underlie classical Indian characterizations of personality types, ethical dispositions, and states of mind. A person is sattvic, rajasic, or tamasic to varying degrees, and the path of practice aims at purification toward sattva and ultimately toward freedom from all three.
- **A theory of everything in prakriti**: the gunas provide a unified principle for the structure of matter, mind, and psychology — from physical substances to emotional states to cognitive dispositions — all are configurations of the three strands.

## Category errors to avoid

- **"The gunas are the same as modern dimensional emotion theory."** There are real points of contact — Russell's dimensional models of emotion (valence/arousal) and Samkhya's rajas-tamas-sattva analysis do overlap in suggestive ways. But the Samkhya gunas are cosmological-metaphysical principles (they constitute prakriti, not just describe it), and their role in Samkhya's soteriology is alien to psychological affect theory.
- **"Sattva is good, rajas and tamas are bad."** This is a simplification. The goal of Samkhya-Yoga is liberation from *all three* gunas, not the triumph of sattva. Sattva is a means, not the end; even a purely sattvic state is a state of prakriti and so not liberation. Classical Yoga describes the final stage as dharma-megha samadhi, beyond even pure sattva.
- **"Gunas are qualities of things."** No — in Samkhya-Yoga, gunas are not properties inhering in substances. They are constituents of prakriti itself. Using "guna" in the Vaisheshika sense (quality) and the Samkhya sense (cosmic strand) are two different technical meanings of the same word.
- **"The three gunas are the same as the three doshas of Ayurveda."** Related and historically connected but not the same. Doshas (vata, pitta, kapha) are a distinct medical classification; gunas are the broader metaphysical framework that doshas operate within.

## Modern bridges

- **Philosophy of mind and affect theory**: guna theory offers a classical Indian framework for qualitative-characterological analysis of mental states that parallels modern dimensional theories of affect and personality, but within a radically different metaphysical frame. *Breaks because* gunas are metaphysical constituents of reality, not descriptive axes of emotion.
- **Process philosophy**: the Samkhya account of prakriti as a dynamic process of guna-configurations rather than as static substance has structural parallels with process-philosophical metaphysics (Whitehead and others). *Breaks because* Samkhya is tied to a liberation project that process philosophy is not.
