## Definition

**Pramana** is the classical Indian term for a *valid means of knowledge* — the technical category for "how do we come to know what we know reliably?" The various darshanas accept different lists of pramanas:

- **Carvaka**: only perception (pratyaksha)
- **Buddhism, Vaisheshika**: perception and inference (anumana)
- **Samkhya, Yoga**: perception, inference, and verbal testimony (shabda)
- **Nyaya**: perception, inference, testimony, and analogy (upamana)
- **Mimamsa (Prabhakara)**: perception, inference, testimony, analogy, and postulation (arthapatti)
- **Mimamsa (Bhatta), Advaita Vedanta**: all six above, plus non-cognition (anupalabdhi)

The pramana framework is classical India's theory of justification — the tradition's articulated position on what counts as knowledge-producing.

- Commentarial traditions across all the classical darshanas

## Interpretive history

- **Nyaya tradition**: develops the most formally elaborated treatment of pramana theory, particularly the logic of inference (anumana)
- **Buddhist logicians** (Dignaga, Dharmakirti): major contributors; restrict pramanas to perception and inference while developing an extraordinarily sophisticated theory of both
- **Mimamsa and Advaita**: expand the list and develop pramana theory in the context of scriptural interpretation
- **Modern analytic engagements**: Bimal Krishna Matilal and others have analyzed pramana theory using the tools of modern analytic philosophy

## Modern bridges

- **Epistemic justification in analytic philosophy**: modern debates about what justifies belief (foundationalism, coherentism, reliabilism) address the same general question as pramana theory. *Breaks because* classical pramana theorists work within different background metaphysical commitments than analytic epistemologists.
- **Testimony as a source of knowledge**: classical Indian acceptance of shabda (verbal testimony) as a pramana parallels modern epistemology's renewed interest in testimony (Burge, Coady, Lackey). *Breaks because* the classical tradition frames testimony with sensitivity to its scriptural context (shruti) that modern testimony epistemology does not.
- **Formal logic**: Nyaya's theory of inference is genuinely sophisticated and has been studied as a formal system by modern logicians. *Breaks because* the classical formalism is not identical to modern predicate logic; it is its own tradition.

## Category errors to avoid

- **"Indian philosophy had no epistemology."** The pramana tradition is a highly developed theory of knowledge and justification, going back at least to the classical sutra period.
- **"Indian epistemology is just 'scripture says so'."** This is a caricature. While shabda is accepted as a pramana in several schools, it is *one* pramana among several, and its use is tightly constrained by interpretive rules.
- **"Nyaya logic is identical to Western logic."** The two traditions are in dialogue (Matilal and others have shown this) but are not identical. The classical Indian treatment has its own vocabulary, arguments, and insights.
