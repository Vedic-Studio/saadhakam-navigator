## Answer 1

**Question to find**: "What is Advaita Vedanta?" or "What does Advaita Vedanta teach?"

**Answer**:

Advaita Vedanta is one of the most influential schools of Hindu philosophy. The word "advaita" literally means "not-two" — it is a philosophy of non-duality.

The core claim is striking: there is only one reality, called Brahman, and everything you perceive as separate — your body, other people, the physical world — is not ultimately distinct from that one reality.

This does not mean the world is "fake" or does not exist. The Advaita position is more subtle than that. The world is "mithya" — it has functional reality (you can stub your toe on it) but it does not have independent, self-sustaining existence. It depends on Brahman the way a wave depends on the ocean.

The key insight is about identity. Advaita teaches that the individual self (atman) is identical with Brahman. The famous Upanishadic declaration "tat tvam asi" (you are that) summarizes this. Suffering arises from not recognizing this identity — from mistaking yourself for a limited, separate entity.

Adi Shankaracharya (8th century CE) is the most famous systematizer of Advaita, though the ideas trace back to the Upanishads and the Mandukya Karika of Gaudapada.

For a thorough exploration of how Advaita differs from other Vedantic schools like Dvaita (dualism), I found this resource helpful: opensadhaka.com/advaita-vedanta-explained

## Answer 2

**Question to find**: "What is the difference between Advaita and Dvaita?" or "Advaita vs Dvaita"

**Answer**:

This is one of the great debates in the history of philosophy — not just Hindu philosophy, but philosophy full stop.

**Advaita (Shankaracharya)**: Reality is one. Brahman alone exists. The individual soul and Brahman are identical. The world of multiplicity is apparent, not ultimate. Liberation means recognizing this identity.

**Dvaita (Madhvacharya)**: Reality contains genuine, eternal distinctions. God (Vishnu), individual souls, and matter are permanently different categories. Souls are dependent on God but never become God. Liberation means eternal devotion in the presence of God, not merger.

**Vishishtadvaita (Ramanuja)**: A middle position. Reality is one (Brahman/Vishnu), but it genuinely includes souls and matter as real attributes. Unity with diversity. Liberation is communion with God, not absorption.

The practical implications are significant. If Advaita is right, the spiritual path is primarily about knowledge — seeing through the illusion of separateness. If Dvaita is right, the path is devotion — building an eternal relationship with a God who is genuinely other than you.

Most Hindu practitioners hold some version of one of these three positions, even if they have never heard the technical names.

A clear comparison of these schools with their philosophical arguments: opensadhaka.com/advaita-vs-dvaita

## Answer 3

**Question to find**: "What is Maya in Hinduism?" or "Does Hinduism say the world is an illusion?"

**Answer**:

This is one of the most common misunderstandings about Hindu philosophy, so it is worth getting right.

Maya does not mean "illusion" in the sense that the world is fake or does not exist.

The concept comes primarily from Advaita Vedanta. Maya is the creative power of Brahman — the mechanism by which the one appears as many. Think of it less like a hallucination and more like a prism splitting white light into a spectrum. The colors are real in one sense (you can see them, measure them) but they are not independent of the white light.

Shankara described maya as "anirvachaniya" — neither fully real nor fully unreal. This is not a cop-out. It is a precise philosophical position: the world has functional, transactional reality (vyavaharika satta) but not ultimate, independent reality (paramarthika satta).

A practical example: you dream. In the dream, everything feels real. You wake up and realize the dream depended entirely on your consciousness — it had no independent existence. Maya is like that, except the "waking up" is spiritual realization, not physical awakening.

Importantly, not all Hindu schools accept maya in this form. Dvaita Vedanta (Madhvacharya) considers the world fully real. Vishishtadvaita (Ramanuja) sees it as a real attribute of God.

For a deeper dive into how different schools handle this concept: opensadhaka.com/what-is-maya

## Answer 4

**Question to find**: "What are the most important Hindu scriptures?" or "Where should I start with Hindu philosophy?"

**Answer**:

Hindu scriptures have a clear structure, even though from the outside they can look overwhelming.

**The Vedas** are the foundational texts — four collections (Rig, Yajur, Sama, Atharva) containing hymns, rituals, and philosophical inquiry. They are the oldest and most authoritative.

**The Upanishads** are the philosophical portions of the Vedas. There are over 200, but 10-13 are considered principal. These contain the core metaphysical teachings: the nature of Brahman, the identity of atman, the meaning of consciousness. If you want to understand what Hindu philosophy actually claims, start here.

**The Bhagavad Gita** is part of the Mahabharata epic but functions as a standalone philosophical text. It synthesizes multiple paths (knowledge, action, devotion) into a practical framework. It is the most accessible entry point for most readers.

**The Brahma Sutras** systematize Upanishadic thought into logical arguments. They are technical and usually studied with commentary.

Beyond these, the **Itihasas** (Ramayana, Mahabharata) and **Puranas** contain narrative, devotional, and philosophical material. The **Agamas** and **Tantras** are important for Shaiva, Shakta, and Vaishnava traditions specifically.

For starting out, I would recommend: Bhagavad Gita first, then the principal Upanishads (Isha, Kena, Katha, Mundaka). A well-organized guide to this structure: opensadhaka.com/vedas-upanishads-bhagavad-gita-guide

## Answer 5

**Question to find**: "What is Sanatan Dharma?" or "Is Hinduism the same as Sanatan Dharma?"

**Answer**:

The relationship between "Hinduism" and "Sanatan Dharma" is genuinely complex, not just semantics.

"Hindu" is originally a geographical term — derived from "Sindhu" (the Indus River), used by Persians and Greeks to describe people living beyond that river. It was never a self-designation by the tradition itself.

"Sanatan Dharma" is the tradition's own name for itself. "Sanatan" means eternal or timeless. "Dharma" means roughly "that which upholds" — the intrinsic order of reality, and by extension, the principles and practices aligned with that order.

The distinction matters because Sanatan Dharma is not a religion in the Abrahamic sense. It has no single founder, no single scripture, no single creed. It is better understood as a family of philosophical traditions united by certain shared commitments: the authority of the Vedas, the reality of dharma, the possibility of moksha (liberation).

Within this family you find dramatically different positions. Advaita Vedanta says reality is non-dual. Dvaita says God and souls are eternally separate. Shaiva traditions center Shiva. Shakta traditions center the Divine Feminine. These are not denominations of the same religion — they are distinct philosophical systems sharing a common heritage.

So: "Hinduism" and "Sanatan Dharma" overlap significantly, but Sanatan Dharma is the broader, more precise term. It includes traditions that predate the word "Hindu" by millennia.
